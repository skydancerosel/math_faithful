## Part II. Sublevel sets and attached cells (Milnor §3)

Milnor's §3 watches the sublevel sets $M^a = f^{-1}(-\infty, a]$ of a smooth function grow. Across an interval without critical values nothing happens: $M^a$ and $M^b$ are diffeomorphic, and the larger set deforms onto the smaller one (Theorem 3.1). Across a non-degenerate critical point of index $\lambda$ a $\lambda$-cell is attached (Theorem 3.2, with Remark 3.3 for several critical points on one level and Remark 3.4 for the critical level itself). With two lemmas on attaching cells (Lemmas 3.6 and 3.7), an induction over the critical values then shows that a manifold carrying a function with non-degenerate critical points has the homotopy type of a complex with one $\lambda$-cell for each critical point of index $\lambda$ (Theorem 3.5). This part retells the formal proofs of all of these, and of the counterexample behind the Remark on p. 13.

The formal proofs work in three settings. Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 and Remark 3.4 are proved on a finite-dimensional real inner product space $E$, which is Milnor's $\mathbb R^n$ with a Riemannian metric. Theorems 3.1, 3.2 and 3.5 and Remark 3.3 are proved for $f = F|N$, the restriction of a smooth function $F$ on a finite-dimensional real normed space $V$ to a slice submanifold $N \subseteq V$ in the sense of the companion project (§0.1). These are Milnor's manifolds in $\mathbb R^K$, with the extra hypothesis that $f$ extends to a smooth function on $V$. Theorem 3.1 is moreover transferred to abstract compact Hausdorff manifolds through Whitney's embedding; Part III tells that transfer. The formal route departs from Milnor's in four places, each explained where it occurs.

1. Everything in the proof of Theorem 3.1 after the computation $\frac{d}{dt} f(\varphi_t(q)) = 1$ is proved once, for an abstract *slab flow* on a topological space. Milnor's "clearly" becomes a lemma about real functions of one variable which have unit speed while their values lie in a slab.
2. On a submanifold there is no Riemannian metric and no intrinsic flow. Milnor's field $\rho\,\operatorname{grad} f$ is replaced by an ambient field built from slice charts, tangent to $N$, whose flow preserves $N$.
3. All computations of Theorem 3.2 in Morse coordinates are done once, on a product $X \times Y$ of inner product spaces, and then transported: to $E$, to a coordinate patch of an arbitrary Hausdorff space, and to submanifolds. Milnor's function $\mu$ is constructed, his three-case deformation $r_t$ is a single formula, and the identification of the subspace $M^{c-\epsilon} \cup e^\lambda$ with $M^{c-\epsilon}$ with a cell attached is a lemma (Lemma D).
4. Mathlib has no cellular approximation theorem. In Theorem 3.5 "CW-complex" is therefore replaced by an iterated adjunction space with exactly the right number of cells in each dimension, and only compact $N$ is treated.

Results carry the labels V2.1–V2.15 of the verified edition (`Exposition/Verification.md`), so that one can move between the two texts.

### II.1 Sublevel sets and strong deformation retracts

For a real function $F$ on a set $T$ and a real number $c$, the **sublevel set** is $T^c = \{x \in T : F(x) \le c\}$, Milnor's $M^c$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · sublevel⟧ (§0.4). It increases with $c$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · sublevel_mono⟧.

A subset $A$ of a topological space $X$ is a **strong deformation retract** of $X$ if there are a continuous map $r : X \to X$ with values in $A$ and a homotopy $H : [0,1] \times X \to X$ from the identity of $X$ to $r$ such that $H(t, a) = a$ for every $t$ and every $a \in A$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsStrongDeformationRetract⟧. The homotopy is Mathlib's homotopy relative to $A$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Homotopy/Basic · ContinuousMap.HomotopyRel⟧. In Milnor's notation $r_t = H(t, \cdot)$: $r_0$ is the identity, $r_1 = r$ maps $X$ into $A$, and every $r_t$ fixes $A$, so that $r$ is a retraction onto $A$. Milnor uses "deformation retract" without a definition. Every deformation he constructs fixes the smaller set at all times, so every formal statement asserts this strong form, which implies the weaker ones.

The sets that occur are subsets of one ambient space, as in $M^a \subseteq M^b \subseteq M$. For $A \subseteq B$, "$A$ is a strong deformation retract of $B$" then means the property above for the space $B$, with the subspace topology, and the subset of $B$ formed by the points of $A$. All statements below are of this kind.

**Lemma (V2.1).** If $A \subseteq B$ and $A$ is a strong deformation retract of $B$, the inclusion $A \to B$ is a homotopy equivalence ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsStrongDeformationRetract.exists_homotopyEquiv⟧.

*Proof.* This is the last clause of Milnor's statement of Theorem 3.1 (p. 12). Let $i : A \to B$ be the inclusion and $j : B \to A$ the map $x \mapsto r(x)$, continuous because $r$ is continuous with values in $A$. Then $j \circ i$ is the identity of $A$: the homotopy fixes $A$ at all times, in particular $r(a) = a$ at time $1$. And $i \circ j = r$, which is homotopic to the identity of $B$ by $H$ run backwards. So $i$ and $j$ are mutually inverse up to homotopy, which is a homotopy equivalence in Mathlib's sense ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Homotopy/Equiv · ContinuousMap.HomotopyEquiv⟧. The homotopy inverse is Milnor's $r_1$. $\square$

Two closure properties are used throughout. Milnor uses both without stating them; the exposition calls them Lemma A.

**Lemma A(i).** If $A \subseteq B \subseteq C$, $A$ is a strong deformation retract of $B$, and $B$ is one of $C$, then $A$ is one of $C$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · IsStrongDeformationRetract.trans⟧.

*Proof.* Let $G$ deform $C$ onto $B$, with end map $s$, and let $H$ deform $B$ onto $A$, with end map $r$. A homotopy relative to $B$ is also a homotopy relative to the smaller set $A$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · homotopyRelMono⟧. First follow $G$ from the identity of $C$ to $s$; then follow $K(t, x) = H(t, s(x))$, which runs from $s$ to $r \circ s$. Both halves fix $A$: $G$ fixes $B \supseteq A$, and for $x \in A$ one has $s(x) = x$ and so $K(t, x) = H(t, x) = x$. The concatenation is a homotopy relative to $A$ from the identity of $C$ to $r \circ s$, which takes values in $A$. $\square$

**Lemma A(ii).** If $A \subseteq B \subseteq C$ and both $A$ and $B$ are strong deformation retracts of $C$, then $A$ is a strong deformation retract of $B$ ⟦Chapter2/CriticalLevel · IsStrongDeformationRetract.of_retract⟧.

*Proof.* Of the second hypothesis only the retraction $\kappa : C \to B$ is used, which fixes $B$ pointwise. Let $H$ deform $C$ onto $A$, with end map $r$, and put $K(t, b) = \kappa(H(t, b))$ for $b \in B$. At $t = 0$ this is $\kappa(b) = b$. For $a \in A$ it is $\kappa(a) = a$ at every time. At $t = 1$ it is $\kappa(r(b))$, and since $r(b) \in A \subseteq B$ is fixed by $\kappa$, the end map takes values in $A$. So $K$ deforms $B$ onto $A$ relative to $A$. $\square$

This is what Milnor's "we see easily" in Remark 3.4 needs (§II.12).

### II.2 The one-crossing lemma

Milnor's proof of Theorem 3.1 (p. 13) computes that along a trajectory $t \mapsto \varphi_t(q)$ of his field $X = \rho\operatorname{grad} f$ the derivative of $f(\varphi_t(q))$ is $+1$ whenever $\varphi_t(q)$ lies in $f^{-1}[a,b]$. He concludes that $t \mapsto f(\varphi_t(q))$ is linear with slope one while its value lies between $a$ and $b$, and reads off the rest. Outside the slab nothing is known about the derivative, and Milnor does not even require $\rho \ge 0$. That a trajectory which is in the slab stays in it, moving linearly, until it leaves through the top, and that it never crosses a level of the slab downwards, needs a continuity argument. The formal proof isolates it as a statement about one real function.

**Definition.** A function $g : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ **has unit speed in the slab** $[a,b]$ if it is continuous and has derivative $1$ at every $t$ with $a \le g(t) \le b$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · UnitSpeedInSlab⟧. Nothing is assumed about $g$ while its value lies outside $[a,b]$.

The lemmas below are proved by *continuous induction*: to show that a set which is closed in $[s, t]$ contains all of $[s,t]$, it suffices that it contains $s$ and contains a right neighbourhood of each of its points in $[s,t)$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Order/IntermediateValue · IsClosed.Icc_subset_of_forall_mem_nhdsWithin⟧.

1. *A positive derivative pushes upward.* If $g'(t) > 0$, then $g(s) > g(t)$ for all $s$ slightly larger than $t$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · eventually_lt_of_hasDerivAt_pos⟧. The difference quotients $(g(s) - g(t))/(s - t)$ tend to $g'(t) > 0$, so they are positive for $s$ near $t$, and $s - t > 0$.
2. *Time reversal.* If $g$ has unit speed in $[a,b]$, then $t \mapsto -g(-t)$ has unit speed in $[-b, -a]$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · UnitSpeedInSlab.reverse⟧. Indeed $-g(-t) \in [-b,-a]$ exactly when $g(-t) \in [a,b]$, and then the chain rule gives the derivative $-(1 \cdot (-1)) = 1$. So every statement about the future has a mirror statement about the past.
3. *No downward crossing.* If $c \in [a,b]$, $s \le t$ and $c \le g(s)$, then $c \le g(t)$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · UnitSpeedInSlab.le_of_le⟧. The set $\{r : c \le g(r)\}$ is closed and contains $s$. At one of its points $r$, either $c < g(r)$, and then $c < g$ near $r$ by continuity, or $g(r) = c$; in the second case $g(r)$ lies in the slab, so $g'(r) = 1 > 0$ and $g > c$ just to the right of $r$ by step 1. Continuous induction gives all of $[s,t]$. In mirror form: if $g(t) \le c$, then $g(s) \le c$ for every $s \le t$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · UnitSpeedInSlab.le_of_le'⟧, which is step 3 for $-g(-\cdot)$ and the level $-c$.
4. *Linear while in the slab.* If $a \le g(s)$, $s \le t$ and $g(s) + (t - s) \le b$, then $g(t) = g(s) + (t - s)$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · UnitSpeedInSlab.eq_add_of_le⟧. The hypotheses force $a \le b$. The set of $r$ with $g(r) = g(s) + (r - s)$ is closed and contains $s$. Let $r \in [s, t)$ belong to it. Then $g(r) = g(s) + (r - s) < g(s) + (t - s) \le b$, so by continuity there is $\delta > 0$ with $g < b$ on $(r - \delta, r + \delta)$. Let $r' \in (r, r + \delta)$. On $[r, r']$ the function stays in the slab: below $b$ by the choice of $\delta$, and at or above $a$ by step 3, because it was at or above the level $a$ at the earlier time $s$. So $g' = 1$ on $[r, r')$, and the function $x \mapsto g(x) - x$, continuous on $[r, r']$ with right derivative $0$ on $[r, r')$, is constant there ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/MeanValue · constant_of_has_deriv_right_zero⟧. Hence $g(r') = g(r) + (r' - r) = g(s) + (r' - s)$, and the set contains $(r, r + \delta)$.

Putting the two directions of time together gives the statement Milnor uses.

**Lemma C (one crossing).** If $g$ has unit speed in $[a, b]$ and $g(s) \in [a,b]$, then $g(s + \tau) = g(s) + \tau$ for every $\tau$ with $a - g(s) \le \tau \le b - g(s)$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · UnitSpeedInSlab.eq_add⟧.

*Proof.* For $\tau \ge 0$ this is step 4 from $s$ to $s + \tau$. For $\tau \le 0$, apply step 4 to the reversed function $-g(-\cdot)$, which has unit speed in $[-b, -a]$, from the time $-s$ to the time $-(s + \tau)$: the hypothesis $-g(s) \ge -b$ is $g(s) \le b$, and $-g(s) + (-\tau) \le -a$ is $\tau \ge a - g(s)$. $\square$

The exposition states Lemma C as a trichotomy: below $a$ before some time $\alpha$, equal to $a + (t - \alpha)$ on $[\alpha, \alpha + b - a]$, above $b$ afterwards. The formal proof never needs the trichotomy. It uses steps 3 and 4 and Lemma C directly, as the next section shows.

### II.3 Slab flows: the topological half of Theorem 3.1

**Definition.** Let $T$ be a topological space, $F : T \to \mathbb R$, and $(t, x) \mapsto \varphi_t(x)$ a map $\mathbb R \times T \to T$. This is a **slab flow** for $F$ and the slab $[a, b]$ if $(t, x) \mapsto \varphi_t(x)$ is continuous, $\varphi_0$ is the identity, $\varphi_{s+t} = \varphi_s \circ \varphi_t$, $F$ is continuous, and for all $x$ and $t$ with $F(\varphi_t(x)) \in [a, b]$ the function $s \mapsto F(\varphi_s(x))$ has derivative $1$ at $t$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsSlabFlow⟧. For Milnor, $T = M$, $F = f$, and $\varphi$ is the flow of $\rho\operatorname{grad} f$ (§II.4). The same abstraction serves for a submanifold that is invariant under an ambient flow (§II.5). Along every trajectory, $t \mapsto F(\varphi_t(x))$ has unit speed in the slab ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsSlabFlow.unitSpeed⟧, and the lemmas of §II.2 apply to it.

**Milnor's deformation as one formula.** Milnor's $r_t(q)$ is $q$ when $f(q) \le a$ and $\varphi_{t(a - f(q))}(q)$ when $a \le f(q) \le b$. The formal proof writes it as a single formula ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · deformation⟧,
$$r_t(x) = \varphi_{\,t \cdot \min(0,\ a - F(x))}(x).$$
It is visibly continuous in $(t, x)$, as a composite of continuous maps ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsSlabFlow.continuous_deformation⟧, so no pasting lemma is needed. It agrees with Milnor's two cases: if $F(x) \le a$, the time is $0$ and $r_t(x) = \varphi_0(x) = x$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsSlabFlow.deformation_of_le⟧; if $F(x) \ge a$, it is $\varphi_{t(a - F(x))}(x)$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · deformation_of_ge⟧. At $t = 0$ it is the identity ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsSlabFlow.deformation_zero⟧.

Four consequences of §II.2 along trajectories carry the whole argument.

- *Linear in the slab.* If $F(x) \in [a,b]$ and $a - F(x) \le t \le b - F(x)$, then $F(\varphi_t(x)) = F(x) + t$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsSlabFlow.apply_eq_add⟧. This is Lemma C at the time $0$, since $\varphi_0(x) = x$.
- *No downward crossing.* If $c \in [a,b]$, $t \ge 0$ and $F(\varphi_t(x)) \le c$, then $F(x) \le c$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsSlabFlow.le_of_apply_le⟧. This is the mirror form of step 3.
- *Rising at most at unit speed.* If $a \le c \le c' \le b$ and $F(x) \le c$, then $F(\varphi_{c'-c}(x)) \le c'$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsSlabFlow.apply_le_of_le⟧. If $F(\varphi_{c'-c}(x)) \le c$ there is nothing to prove. Otherwise the continuous function $u \mapsto F(\varphi_u(x))$ is at most $c$ at $u = 0$ and larger than $c$ at $u = c' - c$, so by the intermediate value theorem it equals $c$ at some $u \in [0, c'-c]$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Order/IntermediateValue · intermediate_value_Icc⟧. From the time $u$ on, step 4 applies: the value $c$ is at least $a$, and $c + (c' - c - u) \le c' \le b$. So at the time $c' - c$ the value is $c + (c' - c - u) = c' - u \le c'$.
- *Backwards.* If $a \le c \le c' \le b$ and $F(y) \le c'$, then $F(\varphi_{c-c'}(y)) \le c$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsSlabFlow.le_of_apply_le'⟧. Put $x = \varphi_{c-c'}(y)$, so that $\varphi_{c'-c}(x) = y$ by the group law. If $F(y) \le c$, no downward crossing, for the time $c' - c \ge 0$, gives $F(x) \le c$. If $F(y) > c$, then $F(y) \in (c, c'] \subseteq [a, b]$, and going back from $y$ for the time $F(y) - c$ reaches the level $c$: $F(\varphi_{c - F(y)}(y)) = c$ by linearity in the slab. Since $\varphi_{c' - F(y)}(x) = \varphi_{c - F(y)}(y)$ and $c' - F(y) \ge 0$, the trajectory of $x$ is at the level $c$ at a later time, and no downward crossing gives $F(x) \le c$.

**Theorem 3.1, first half, for a slab flow.** If $a \le c \le c' \le b$, then $\varphi_{c'-c}(T^c) = T^{c'}$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsSlabFlow.image_sublevel⟧.

*Proof.* The inclusion $\subseteq$ is "rising at most at unit speed". For $\supseteq$, a point $y$ with $F(y) \le c'$ is the image under $\varphi_{c'-c}$ of $\varphi_{c-c'}(y)$, which lies in $T^c$ by the backwards statement. $\square$

With $c = a$ and $c' = b$ this is Milnor's "clearly" (p. 13): $\varphi_{b-a}$ carries $M^a$ onto $M^b$. Both inclusions are needed. A trajectory starting below $a$ must not be above $b$ at the time $b - a$, and a trajectory ending below $b$ must not have started above $a$. Both rest on the facts that a trajectory crosses the slab at most once, upwards, and at unit speed.

**Theorem 3.1, second half, for a slab flow.** $T^a$ is a strong deformation retract of $T^b$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsSlabFlow.isStrongDeformationRetract⟧, and if $a \le b$ the inclusion $T^a \to T^b$ is a homotopy equivalence ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsSlabFlow.exists_homotopyEquiv⟧.

*Proof.*

1. *Where $r_t$ goes.* For $x$ in the slab and $0 \le t \le 1$, $F(r_t(x)) = F(x) + t\,(a - F(x))$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsSlabFlow.apply_deformation⟧. Indeed $r_t(x) = \varphi_{t(a - F(x))}(x)$, and the time $t(a - F(x))$ lies in $[a - F(x), 0]$, inside the interval where linearity in the slab applies. The value lies between $a$ and $F(x)$.
2. Hence $r_t$ maps $T^b$ into $T^b$ for $0 \le t \le 1$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsSlabFlow.deformation_mem⟧, points below $a$ not moving at all, and $r_1$ maps $T^b$ into $T^a$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsSlabFlow.deformation_one_mem⟧, because $F(r_1(x)) = a$ for $x$ in the slab.
3. Milnor's retraction $r_1$ is a continuous self-map of $T^b$ with values in $T^a$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsSlabFlow.retraction⟧ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsSlabFlow.retraction_mem⟧. The map $(t, x) \mapsto r_t(x)$, $0 \le t \le 1$, is a homotopy from the identity of $T^b$ to $r_1$, and it fixes $T^a$ at all times because $r_t(x) = x$ when $F(x) \le a$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsSlabFlow.homotopyRel⟧.
4. The homotopy equivalence follows by the lemma of §II.1, with homotopy inverse $r_1$. $\square$

Milnor's hypothesis $a < b$ is not needed: $a \le b$ suffices, and for $a = b$ the statements are trivial.

### II.4 Theorem 3.1 in a Euclidean space

Let $E$ be a finite-dimensional real inner product space. A point $p$ is a **critical point** of $f : E \to \mathbb R$ if $f$ is differentiable at $p$ with derivative $0$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · IsCriticalPoint⟧ (§0.2). The **gradient** $\nabla f(x)$ is the vector with $\langle \nabla f(x), h\rangle = df_x(h)$ for all $h$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/Gradient/Basic · gradient⟧; it is Milnor's $\operatorname{grad} f$ for the Riemannian metric given by the inner product. Three facts about it are used.

- If $f$ is $C^{m+1}$, then $\nabla f$ is $C^m$ ⟦Chapter2/Regular · contDiff_gradient⟧: it is $x \mapsto df_x$ followed by the inverse of the Riesz isomorphism $E \to E^*$, a continuous linear map.
- $df_x(\nabla f(x)) = \|\nabla f(x)\|^2$ ⟦Chapter2/Regular · fderiv_apply_gradient⟧, from $\langle \nabla f(x), h\rangle = df_x(h)$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/Gradient/Basic · inner_gradient_left⟧ with $h = \nabla f(x)$.
- At a point where $f$ is differentiable, $p$ is critical exactly when $\nabla f(p) = 0$ ⟦Chapter2/Regular · isCriticalPoint_iff_gradient_eq_zero⟧, since the Riesz map is injective. This is Milnor's remark that the gradient vanishes precisely at the critical points (p. 12).

**The cut-off.** Milnor takes a smooth $\rho : M \to \mathbb R$ equal to $1/\langle \operatorname{grad} f, \operatorname{grad} f\rangle$ on $f^{-1}[a,b]$ and vanishing outside a compact neighbourhood of it, and does not show that one exists. The formal proof constructs it in a more general form, which §II.5 and §II.13 reuse. It needs a product rule for functions that are smooth only on part of the space.

*A product lemma.* If $\chi$ is smooth on a normed space and $g$ is smooth on an open set $U$ containing the closed support of $\chi$, then $\chi \cdot g$ is smooth everywhere ⟦Chapter2/Regular · contDiff_smul_of_tsupport⟧. At a point of $U$ it is a product of two functions smooth near that point. A point outside $U$ lies outside the closed support of $\chi$, so $\chi$ vanishes on a neighbourhood of it, and there the product is identically $0$.

**Lemma B.** Let $g$ be smooth on a finite-dimensional real normed space, with no zero on the compact set $K$, and let $O \supseteq K$ be open. There is a smooth $\rho$ with compact support contained in $O$, with $\rho \ge 0$ wherever $g \ge 0$, and with $\rho\, g = 1$ on $K$ ⟦Chapter2/Regular · exists_cutoff_mul⟧.

*Proof.*

1. The set $U = O \cap \{g \ne 0\}$ is open and contains $K$. As the space is locally compact, there is a compact $L$ with $K \subseteq \operatorname{int} L \subseteq L \subseteq U$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Compactness/LocallyCompact · exists_compact_between⟧.
2. A smooth Urysohn function: there is a smooth $\chi$ with values in $[0,1]$, support exactly $\operatorname{int} L$, and $\chi(x) = 1$ exactly for $x \in K$ ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/PartitionOfUnity · exists_contMDiff_support_eq_eq_one_iff⟧. Its closed support lies in $L$.
3. Put $\rho = \chi \cdot g^{-1}$. It is smooth by the product lemma, as $g^{-1}$ is smooth on $U$, which contains $L$. Its closed support lies in that of $\chi$, hence in the compact set $L \subseteq O$. It is $\ge 0$ where $g \ge 0$, since $\chi \ge 0$. On $K$, $\rho\, g = 1 \cdot g^{-1} g = 1$. $\square$

For Milnor's $\rho$ apply Lemma B with $g = \|\nabla f\|^2$, which is smooth and has no zero on $K = f^{-1}[a,b]$ when $K$ contains no critical point, and with $O = E$. This gives a smooth $\rho \ge 0$ with compact support and $\rho\,\|\nabla f\|^2 = 1$ on $K$ ⟦Chapter2/Regular · exists_cutoff⟧. The sign $\rho \ge 0$ is never used.

**The field and its flow.** Milnor's vector field is $X = \rho\,\nabla f$ ⟦Chapter2/Regular · gradField⟧. It is smooth ⟦Chapter2/Regular · contDiff_gradField⟧ with compact support ⟦Chapter2/Regular · hasCompactSupport_gradField⟧. A $C^1$ field with compact support is bounded and globally Lipschitz ⟦Chapter2/Regular · isBddLipschitz_gradField⟧ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.of_contDiff⟧, which is the hypothesis under which Part I constructs the global flow of Lemma 2.4 ⟦Flow/Global · flow⟧ (§0.10). Each $t \mapsto \varphi_t(x)$ is an integral curve of $X$ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.hasDerivAt_flow⟧, and outside the support of $\rho$ the flow is stationary ⟦Chapter2/Regular · flow_gradField_of_notMem⟧ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flow_of_notMem_tsupport⟧. Along $X$, $df(X) = \rho\,\|\nabla f\|^2$ ⟦Chapter2/Regular · fderiv_apply_gradField⟧.

**Lemma (the derivative along a trajectory).** If $\rho\,\|\nabla f\|^2 = 1$ wherever $a \le f \le b$, then the flow of $X$ is a slab flow for $f$ and $[a,b]$ ⟦Chapter2/Regular · isSlabFlow_gradField⟧.

*Proof.* Continuity, $\varphi_0 = \mathrm{id}$ and the group law are proved in Part I ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.continuous_flow⟧ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flow_zero⟧ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flow_add⟧. By the chain rule the derivative of $t \mapsto f(\varphi_t(x))$ is $df(X)$ evaluated at $\varphi_t(x)$, that is $\rho\,\|\nabla f\|^2 = 1$ when $f(\varphi_t(x)) \in [a,b]$. This is Milnor's computation $\langle d\varphi_t(q)/dt, \operatorname{grad} f\rangle = \langle X, \operatorname{grad} f\rangle = +1$. $\square$

In particular $f(\varphi_t(x)) = f(x) + t$ for $x$ in the slab and $a - f(x) \le t \le b - f(x)$ ⟦Chapter2/Regular · apply_flow_gradField⟧, and $\varphi_{c'-c}(M^c) = M^{c'}$ for $a \le c \le c' \le b$ ⟦Chapter2/Regular · flow_gradField_image_sublevel⟧.

**Theorem 3.1 (V2.2).** Let $f : E \to \mathbb R$ be $C^\infty$ and $a \le b$, and suppose that $f^{-1}[a, b]$ is compact and contains no critical point of $f$. Then the following hold ⟦Chapter2/Regular · theorem_3_1⟧:

1. there is a $C^\infty$ diffeomorphism $\Phi$ of $E$ with $\Phi(M^a) = M^b$;
2. $M^a$ is a strong deformation retract of $M^b$;
3. the inclusion $M^a \to M^b$ is a homotopy equivalence;
4. $M^a$ and $M^b$ are homeomorphic.

*Proof.* By the characterization of critical points, $\nabla f$ has no zero on $K = f^{-1}[a,b]$. Lemma B gives $\rho$, and the flow of $X = \rho\nabla f$ is a slab flow for $f$. Its time-$(b - a)$ map is a $C^\infty$ diffeomorphism of $E$, with inverse the time-$(a - b)$ map ⟦Flow/Smooth · flowDiffeomorph⟧; that the flow is smooth is Part I's version of Lemma 2.4. The equality $\Phi(M^a) = M^b$ is the first half for slab flows with $c = a$, $c' = b$; items 2 and 3 are the second half. For item 4, $\Phi$ restricts to a homeomorphism of $M^a$ onto its image, which is $M^b$. $\square$

Read backwards, the inverse $\varphi_{a-b}$ pushes $M^b$ down onto $M^a$ ⟦Chapter2/Regular · exists_diffeomorph_image_sublevel_eq⟧, which is the picture Milnor draws. *Restated:* Milnor's "$M^a$ is diffeomorphic to $M^b$" is formalized as what his proof gives, a diffeomorphism of the whole space carrying $M^a$ onto $M^b$. Here $M^a$ and $M^b$ are manifolds with boundary, since $a$ and $b$ are regular values; recording an ambient diffeomorphism avoids giving them that structure.

### II.5 Theorem 3.1 on a submanifold of Euclidean space

Lemma 2.4 is formalized for compactly supported fields on a vector space (Part I), not on manifolds. So on a submanifold the formal proof runs Milnor's argument with an *ambient* flow which preserves the submanifold.

**Setting.** $V$ is a finite-dimensional real normed space, and $N \subseteq V$ is a slice submanifold modelled on $Z$ with normal model $W$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · IsSliceSubmanifold⟧ (§0.1): every point of $N$ lies in the domain of a *slice chart*, a diffeomorphism $\sigma$ from an open subset of $V$ onto an open subset of $Z \times W$ such that a point $x$ of its domain lies in $N$ exactly when the $W$-component of $\sigma(x)$ is $0$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · SliceChart⟧. $N$ carries the manifold structure of the companion project ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · IsSliceSubmanifold.chartedSpace⟧, and need not be closed in $V$. $F : V \to \mathbb R$ is smooth and $f = F|N$. Critical points of $f$ are critical points of the manifold derivative ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsCriticalPointM⟧, which are the points $q$ at which $dF_q$ vanishes on the tangent space $T_qN \subseteq V$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · tangentSubspace⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · isCriticalPointM_comp_val_iff_forall_tangent⟧ (§0.3). A slice chart $\sigma$ gives a parametrization of $N$, $x(z) = \sigma^{-1}(z, 0)$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · sliceParam⟧, and $T_qN$ is the range of its derivative ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · tangentSubspace_eq_range_fderiv_sliceParam⟧.

**Derivatives of slice charts.** $\sigma$ and $\sigma^{-1}$ are smooth on their domains ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · contDiffOn_sliceChart⟧ ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · contDiffOn_sliceChart_invFun⟧. By the chain rule applied to $\sigma \circ \sigma^{-1} = \mathrm{id}$ near $\sigma(x)$, $d\sigma_x \circ d(\sigma^{-1})_{\sigma(x)}$ is the identity ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · fderiv_sliceChart_fderiv_invFun⟧. The derivative of the parametrization at $z$ is $w \mapsto d(\sigma^{-1})_{(z,0)}(w, 0)$ ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · fderiv_sliceParam_apply⟧.

**Coordinate fields.** For $u \in Z$, the coordinate field of $\sigma$ in the direction $u$ is
$$Y_u(x) = d(\sigma^{-1})_{\sigma(x)}(u, 0),$$
defined on the domain of $\sigma$ ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · chartField⟧. It is smooth there ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · contDiffOn_chartField⟧ and linear in $u$; in particular $Y_{-u} = -Y_u$ ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · chartField_neg⟧. At a point $q \in N$ of the domain, $\sigma(q)$ has $W$-component $0$, so $Y_u(q)$ is the derivative of the parametrization applied to $u$ ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · chartField_eq_fderiv_sliceParam⟧. Hence the coordinate fields are tangent to $N$ along $N$ ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · chartField_mem_tangentSubspace⟧. And if $q$ is not a critical point of $f$, some coordinate field has $dF_q(Y_u(q)) > 0$ ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · exists_chartField_pos⟧: $dF_q$ does not vanish on $T_qN$, so it is nonzero on the image $dx(w)$ of some $w$, and one takes $u = w$ or $u = -w$ according to the sign.

**The chart domain.** Let $O$ be the union of the domains of all slice charts of $N$ ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · chartDomain⟧. It is open ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · isOpen_chartDomain⟧, contains $N$ ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · subset_chartDomain⟧, and $N$ is closed in it: $O \setminus N$ is open ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · isOpen_chartDomain_diff⟧. Indeed, if $x$ lies in the domain of $\sigma$ but not in $N$, then $\sigma(x)$ has nonzero $W$-component; by continuity the same holds on a neighbourhood of $x$ inside the domain, and every point there is outside $N$.

**Step 1: a tangent field along which $F$ increases.** If $K \subseteq N$ is compact and contains no critical point of $f$, there is a smooth field $Y$ on $V$, tangent to $N$ at every point of $N$, with $dF(Y) > 0$ on $K$ ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · exists_tangent_field⟧.

*Proof.* For $q \in K$ choose a slice chart $\sigma_q$ at $q$ and a direction $u_q$ with $dF_q(Y_{u_q}(q)) > 0$. The set of points of the domain of $\sigma_q$ where $dF(Y_{u_q}) > 0$ is open and contains $q$, so it contains the closed support of a smooth bump $\chi_q \ge 0$ with $\chi_q(q) = 1$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/BumpFunction/FiniteDimension · exists_contDiff_tsupport_subset⟧. The open sets $\{\chi_q > 0\}$ cover the compact $K$; choose finitely many points $q_1, \dots, q_m$ whose sets cover it, and put
$$Y = \sum_{i=1}^m \chi_{q_i}\, Y_{u_{q_i}}.$$
Each term is smooth on all of $V$ by the product lemma of §II.4, the coordinate field being smooth on the chart domain, an open set containing the closed support of $\chi_{q_i}$. At a point of $N$ each term is $0$ or a multiple of a tangent vector, because $\chi_{q_i} \ne 0$ forces the point into the chart domain; since $T_qN$ is a linear subspace, $Y$ is tangent. At a point of $K$ every term of $dF(Y)$ is $\ge 0$, because where $\chi_{q_i} \ne 0$ the point lies in the closed support, on which $dF(Y_{u_{q_i}}) > 0$; and at least one term is $> 0$, because the sets $\{\chi_{q_i} > 0\}$ cover $K$. $\square$

**Step 2: normalization.** The function $dF(Y)$ is smooth and positive on $K$. Lemma B of §II.4, with $O$ the chart domain, gives a smooth $\rho$ with compact support inside $O$ and $\rho\, dF(Y) = 1$ on $K$. The field $X = \rho\, Y$ is smooth, has compact support inside $O$, is tangent to $N$ along $N$, and satisfies $dF(X) = 1$ on $K$. It plays the role of Milnor's $\rho\operatorname{grad} f$. For the metric induced by an inner product on $V$, $\operatorname{grad} f$ is the orthogonal projection of $\nabla F$ to $T_qN$, but the proof only uses $df(X) = 1$ on the slab, so no metric is needed.

**Step 3: local integral curves inside $N$.** Let $X$ be a smooth field on $V$ which is tangent to $N$ along $N$, and let $y \in N$. There are $\varepsilon > 0$ and a curve $\gamma$ with $\gamma(0) = y$, $\gamma'(t) = X(\gamma(t))$ and $\gamma(t) \in N$ for $|t| < \varepsilon$ ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · exists_integralCurve_mem⟧.

*Proof.* Take a slice chart $\sigma$ at $y$, with parametrization $x$. Read $X$ in the chart: let $\tilde X(z)$ be the $Z$-component of $d\sigma(X(x(z)))$, taken at the point $x(z)$. For $z$ in the domain of the parametrization, $dx_z(\tilde X(z)) = X(x(z))$. Indeed $X(x(z))$ is tangent, so it equals $dx_z(w) = d(\sigma^{-1})(w, 0)$ for some $w$; applying $d\sigma$ gives $(w, 0)$, so $\tilde X(z) = w$. The field $\tilde X$ is $C^1$ at the $Z$-component $z_0$ of $\sigma(y)$, as a composite of smooth maps. Mathlib's Picard–Lindelöf theorem ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/ODE/ExistUnique · ContDiffAt.exists_forall_mem_closedBall_exists_eq_forall_mem_Ioo_hasDerivAt₀⟧ gives a solution $\alpha$ of $\alpha' = \tilde X(\alpha)$ with $\alpha(0) = z_0$ on an interval $(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$. Shrinking $\varepsilon$, $\alpha$ stays in the open domain of the parametrization, by continuity at $0$. Then $\gamma = x \circ \alpha$ lies in $N$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · sliceParam_mem⟧, starts at $y$, and $\gamma' = dx(\alpha') = dx(\tilde X(\alpha)) = X(\gamma)$. $\square$

**Step 4: invariance of $N$.** Let $X$ be smooth with compact support, tangent to $N$ along $N$, with closed support inside the chart domain $O$. Then $\varphi_t(x) \in N$ for every $x \in N$ and every $t$ ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · flow_mem_of_tangent⟧.

*Proof.* Part I proves an invariance criterion: a closed set through each point of which some local integral curve stays in the set is invariant under the flow ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flow_mem⟧. (Uniqueness makes the flow line agree with the local curve, so the set of times at which the flow line lies in the set is open; it is closed by continuity; it contains $0$.) But $N$ need not be closed, so the criterion is applied to $S = N \cup (V \setminus O)$, which is closed because its complement $O \setminus N$ is open. Every point of $S$ has a local integral curve inside $S$: a point of $N$ by Step 3, a point outside $O$ by the constant curve, since $X$ vanishes there. So $\varphi_t(x) \in S$. If $\varphi_t(x)$ were outside $O$, it would be a zero of $X$ and hence a fixed point of the flow ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flow_of_eq_zero⟧, and then $x = \varphi_{-t}(\varphi_t(x)) = \varphi_t(x)$ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flow_neg_flow⟧ would lie outside $O \supseteq N$: a trajectory cannot reach a zero of $X$ in finite time. So $\varphi_t(x) \in N$. $\square$

**Theorem 3.1 on a submanifold (V2.3).** Let $N \subseteq V$ be a slice submanifold modelled on a finite-dimensional $Z$, $F : V \to \mathbb R$ smooth and $f = F|N$. Let $a \le b$, and suppose that $f^{-1}[a,b] \subseteq N$ is compact and contains no critical point of $f$. Then there is a $C^\infty$ diffeomorphism $\Phi$ of the manifold $N$ with $\Phi(N^a) = N^b$; $N^a$ is a strong deformation retract of $N^b$; and the inclusion $N^a \to N^b$ is a homotopy equivalence ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · theorem_3_1_submanifold⟧.

*Proof.*

1. $K = f^{-1}[a,b]$, viewed in $V$, is compact, as the image of a compact set under the inclusion, and lies in $N$.
2. Steps 1 and 2 give the field $X = \rho Y$: smooth, compactly supported inside the chart domain, tangent to $N$, with $dF(X) = 1$ on $K$. It is bounded and Lipschitz, so it has a global flow, and by Step 4 the flow preserves $N$.
3. The restricted flow $\varphi_t|N$ is a slab flow for $f$. Continuity and the group law are inherited from $V$. When $F(\varphi_t(q)) \in [a,b]$, the point $\varphi_t(q)$ lies in $K$, and the chain rule gives the derivative $dF(X) = 1$.
4. Each $\varphi_t|N$ is smooth as a map of manifolds $N \to N$: the ambient $\varphi_t$ is smooth ⟦Flow/Smooth · contDiff_flow⟧, the inclusion of $N$ is smooth, the composite takes values in $N$, and a smooth map with values in a slice submanifold is smooth into it ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Corestrict · IsSliceSubmanifold.contMDiff_codRestrict⟧. So $\Phi = \varphi_{b-a}|N$ is a diffeomorphism of $N$, with inverse $\varphi_{a-b}|N$.
5. The two halves for slab flows (§II.3) give $\Phi(N^a) = N^b$, the strong deformation retraction and the homotopy equivalence. $\square$

When $N$ is compact, $f^{-1}[a,b]$ is a closed subset of a compact space, and the compactness hypothesis can be dropped ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · theorem_3_1_submanifold_of_isCompact⟧. *Narrower than the book:* $f$ must be the restriction of a smooth function on $V$. On a compact Hausdorff manifold $M$ without boundary this costs nothing: Whitney's embedding makes $M$ diffeomorphic to a compact slice submanifold of some $\mathbb R^{K+1}$, every smooth function on which is such a restriction, and Theorem 3.1 follows on $M$ for any $a \le b$ with $f^{-1}[a,b]$ free of critical points ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · theorem_3_1_of_compactSpace⟧ (V2.4). Part III tells that transfer. For non-compact abstract manifolds the theorem is not formalized: it would need a closed embedding into Euclidean space, which Mathlib does not have.

### II.6 The Remark on p. 13: compactness cannot be omitted

Milnor's Remark after Theorem 3.1 says that the compactness of $f^{-1}[a,b]$ cannot be dropped. His Diagram 3 (p. 14) shows a surface from which a point between the levels $a$ and $b$ has been removed, and for it $M^a$ is "clearly" not a deformation retract of $M^b$. The formal version replaces the pictured surface by a punctured plane, and proves more: the two sublevel sets are not even homotopy equivalent.

**The example (V2.5).** $M = \mathbb C \setminus \{i\}$ ⟦Chapter2/Remark · RemarkP13.M⟧, $f(z) = |z|^2$ ⟦Chapter2/Remark · RemarkP13.F⟧, $a = \frac14$ and $b = 4$. The removed point has $f(i) = 1$.

1. *No critical point in the slab.* If $\frac14 \le |z|^2 \le 4$, then $z$ is not a critical point of $|z|^2$ on $\mathbb C$ ⟦Chapter2/Remark · RemarkP13.remark_p13_not_isCriticalPoint⟧. The derivative of $|z|^2$ at $z$ is $h \mapsto 2\langle z, h\rangle$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Calculus · hasStrictFDerivAt_norm_sq⟧; if it vanished, its value $2|z|^2$ at $h = z$ would be $0$, so $z = 0$, which lies at level $0 < \frac14$. Since $M$ is open in $\mathbb C$, the critical points of $f$ on the surface $M$ are those of $|z|^2$, and the formal statement is made in $\mathbb C$.
2. *The slab is not compact* ⟦Chapter2/Remark · RemarkP13.remark_p13_not_isCompact⟧. If $f^{-1}[\frac14, 4] \subseteq M$ were compact, its image in $\mathbb C$ would be compact, hence closed, and it does not contain $i$; so some disc of radius $\varepsilon$ about $i$ would miss it. But for $\delta = \min(\varepsilon/2, \frac12)$ the point $(1 + \delta) i$ lies in $M$, at distance $\delta < \varepsilon$ from $i$, and $|(1+\delta) i|^2 = (1+\delta)^2 \in [1, \frac94] \subseteq [\frac14, 4]$.
3. *$M^{1/4}$ is contractible.* As $|i| = 1 > \frac12$, $M^{1/4}$ is the closed disc of radius $\frac12$ ⟦Chapter2/Remark · RemarkP13.sublevelHomeomorph⟧, which is convex and nonempty, hence contractible ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Convex/Contractible · Convex.contractibleSpace⟧.
4. *A loop of winding number one is not null-homotopic* ⟦Chapter2/Remark · RemarkP13.not_nullhomotopic⟧. Let $\gamma$ be a loop in a space $X$ and $j : X \to \mathbb C \setminus \{0\}$ a continuous map with $j(\gamma(s)) = e^{2\pi i s}$. Suppose $H$ is a homotopy from $j$ to a constant map ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Homotopy/Contractible · ContinuousMap.Nullhomotopic⟧, and put $G(t, s) = H(t, \gamma(s))$, a map $[0,1]^2 \to \mathbb C \setminus \{0\}$ with $G(0, s) = e^{2\pi i s}$.
   - The exponential map $\mathbb C \to \mathbb C \setminus \{0\}$ is a covering map ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Complex/CoveringMap · Complex.isCoveringMap_exp⟧, so $G$ lifts: there is a continuous $L : [0,1]^2 \to \mathbb C$ with $e^{L} = G$ and $L(0, s) = 2\pi i s$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Homotopy/Lifting · IsCoveringMap.liftHomotopy⟧.
   - Two logarithms of the same number differ by an integer multiple of $2\pi i$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/SpecialFunctions/Complex/Log · Complex.exp_eq_exp_iff_exists_int⟧. Since $G(t, 1) = G(t, 0)$, the winding number $k(t) = \operatorname{Im}\big(L(t,1) - L(t,0)\big)/2\pi$ of the loop $G(t, \cdot)$ is an integer for every $t$, and $k$ is continuous.
   - A continuous integer-valued function on a connected space is constant ⟦Chapter2/Remark · RemarkP13.eq_of_forall_eq_intCast⟧: if it took two integer values $m < n$, it would take the value $m + \frac12$ in between, by the intermediate value theorem.
   - $k(0) = 1$, because $L(0,1) - L(0,0) = 2\pi i$. And $k(1) = 0$: the loop $G(1, \cdot)$ is constant, so $s \mapsto \operatorname{Im}\big(L(1, s) - L(1, 0)\big)/2\pi$ is continuous, integer-valued and $0$ at $s = 0$, hence $0$ at $s = 1$. This contradicts the constancy of $k$.
5. *$M^4$ is not contractible* ⟦Chapter2/Remark · RemarkP13.not_contractibleSpace⟧. Apply step 4 to $j(z) = z - i$, a map $M^4 \to \mathbb C \setminus \{0\}$ ⟦Chapter2/Remark · RemarkP13.j⟧, and to the loop $\gamma(s) = i + e^{2\pi i s}$ around the removed point ⟦Chapter2/Remark · RemarkP13.γ⟧, which lies in $M^4$ because $|\gamma(s)| \le |i| + 1 = 2$ and is a closed loop, $\gamma(0) = \gamma(1)$ ⟦Chapter2/Remark · RemarkP13.γ_zero_eq_one⟧. If $M^4$ were contractible, its identity map would be null-homotopic, and so would $j$; but $j(\gamma(s)) = e^{2\pi i s}$.
6. *Conclusion.* $M^{1/4}$ and $M^4$ are not homotopy equivalent ⟦Chapter2/Remark · RemarkP13.remark_p13⟧, because a space homotopy equivalent to a contractible space is contractible. Hence, by the lemma of §II.1, $M^{1/4}$ is not a strong deformation retract of $M^4$ ⟦Chapter2/Remark · RemarkP13.remark_p13_not_isStrongDeformationRetract⟧, and the two are not homeomorphic either ⟦Chapter2/Remark · RemarkP13.remark_p13_not_homeomorph⟧: the first half of Theorem 3.1 fails as well.
7. *With the point put back*, on all of $\mathbb C$, $f^{-1}[\frac14, 4]$ is a closed bounded annulus, hence compact, still without critical points, and Theorem 3.1 of §II.4 applies: the disc of radius $\frac12$ is a strong deformation retract of the disc of radius $2$ ⟦Chapter2/Remark · RemarkP13.remark_p13_plane⟧.

So Milnor's "clearly" hides the fact that a punctured disc is not contractible, which the formal proof derives from the homotopy lifting property of the exponential map.

### II.7 Milnor's function $\mu$

Theorem 3.2 modifies $f$ near the critical point with the help of a function $\mu$ of one variable. Milnor requires (p. 16) that $\mu$ be $C^\infty$ with
$$\mu(0) > \epsilon, \qquad \mu(r) = 0 \ \text{ for } r \ge 2\epsilon, \qquad -1 < \mu'(r) \le 0 \ \text{ for all } r,$$
and he does not show that such a function exists. These four conditions are the formal definition ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · IsMilnorMu⟧. Everything in the proof of Theorem 3.2 uses $\mu$ only through them, and through three consequences.

- $\mu$ is decreasing, since $\mu' \le 0$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · IsMilnorMu.antitone⟧.
- $\mu \ge 0$, since it decreases to $\mu(2\epsilon) = 0$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · IsMilnorMu.nonneg⟧. Milnor uses this in Assertion 1 without stating it.
- $s \mapsto s + \mu(s)$ is increasing, since its derivative $1 + \mu'$ is positive ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · IsMilnorMu.monotone_add⟧. This is Milnor's $\partial F/\partial \xi < 0$ along $\eta = 0$.

*Why a construction is needed.* $\mu$ must drop by more than $\epsilon$ on $[0, 2\epsilon]$ with a slope of absolute value less than $1$. So its average slope there is below $-\frac12$, and the slope has to be controlled everywhere. The first candidate, $A\,(1 - \psi(r/2\epsilon))$ with a smooth step $\psi$ and $A > \epsilon$, fails: Mathlib's smooth step has slope $2$ at its midpoint, which gives the slope $-A/\epsilon < -1$ at $r = \epsilon$. The formal construction integrates a plateau instead.

**The construction.** Let $\psi$ be Mathlib's smooth transition function: smooth, with values in $[0,1]$, equal to $0$ on $(-\infty, 0]$ and to $1$ on $[1, \infty)$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/SpecialFunctions/SmoothTransition · Real.smoothTransition⟧. Put ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · muBump⟧ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · milnorMu⟧
$$\beta(s) = \psi\Big(4 - \frac{2s}{\epsilon}\Big), \qquad \mu(r) = \frac34 \int_r^{2\epsilon} \beta(s)\, ds.$$
For $\epsilon > 0$, $\beta$ is smooth ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · contDiff_muBump⟧ with values in $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · muBump_nonneg⟧ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · muBump_le_one⟧; it equals $1$ for $s \le \frac32\epsilon$, where $4 - 2s/\epsilon \ge 1$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · muBump_of_le⟧, and $0$ for $s \ge 2\epsilon$, where $4 - 2s/\epsilon \le 0$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · muBump_of_ge⟧.

**Lemma.** For every $\epsilon > 0$ this $\mu$ satisfies Milnor's four conditions ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · isMilnorMu_milnorMu⟧.

*Proof.*

1. By the fundamental theorem of calculus, with the lower limit of integration varying, $\mu'(r) = -\frac34\,\beta(r)$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · hasDerivAt_milnorMu⟧ ⟦Mathlib/MeasureTheory/Integral/IntervalIntegral/FundThmCalculus · intervalIntegral.integral_hasDerivAt_left⟧. Hence $-1 < -\frac34 \le \mu' \le 0$.
2. $\mu'$ is smooth, so $\mu$ is $C^\infty$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · contDiff_milnorMu⟧.
3. For $r \ge 2\epsilon$ the integrand vanishes between $2\epsilon$ and $r$, so $\mu(r) = 0$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · milnorMu_of_ge⟧.
4. Splitting the integral at $\frac32\epsilon$, $\mu(0) = \frac34\big(\int_0^{3\epsilon/2} 1\, ds + \int_{3\epsilon/2}^{2\epsilon} \beta\big) \ge \frac34 \cdot \frac{3\epsilon}{2} = \frac98\,\epsilon > \epsilon$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · lt_milnorMu_zero⟧. $\square$

The exposition normalizes a different plateau. Any function with Milnor's four properties serves, and the formal proof fixes this one.

### II.8 Theorem 3.2 in Morse coordinates: $F$, Assertions 1 and 2, the cell and the handle

Milnor's proof of Theorem 3.2 (pp. 15–19) works in coordinates $u^1, \dots, u^n$ around $p$ in which $f = c - \xi + \eta$, with $\xi = (u^1)^2 + \dots + (u^\lambda)^2$ and $\eta = (u^{\lambda+1})^2 + \dots + (u^n)^2$. All his formulas make sense on the whole coordinate space. The formal proof therefore states and proves every computation once, on a product of two inner product spaces, and transports the results afterwards (§II.10 and §II.13).

**Setting.** $X$ and $Y$ are real inner product spaces, Milnor's $\mathbb R^\lambda$ and $\mathbb R^{n-\lambda}$. A point of $X \times Y$ is written $v = (x, y)$, and Milnor's two sums of squares become ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.xi⟧ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.eta⟧
$$\xi(v) = \|x\|^2, \qquad \eta(v) = \|y\|^2.$$
In these coordinates $f$ becomes $f(v) = c - \xi + \eta$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.morseQuad⟧, and Milnor's modified function (p. 16) is ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.morseF⟧
$$F(v) = c - \xi + \eta - \mu(\xi + 2\eta).$$
With $\mu = 0$ it is $f$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.morseQuad_eq_morseF⟧. Three sets enter:

- the **ellipsoid** $\{\xi + 2\eta \le 2\epsilon\}$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.ellipsoid⟧, which is closed ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.isClosed_ellipsoid⟧ and lies in the ball $\{\xi + \eta \le 2\epsilon\}$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.ellipsoid_subset_ball⟧;
- Milnor's **cell** $e^\lambda = \{\xi \le \epsilon,\ y = 0\}$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.cellModel⟧, which lies in the ellipsoid ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.cellModel_subset_ellipsoid⟧;
- Milnor's **handle** $H$, the closure of $F^{-1}(-\infty, c - \epsilon] \setminus f^{-1}(-\infty, c - \epsilon]$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.handleModel⟧.

Let $\mu$ satisfy Milnor's conditions for $\epsilon$. Then $F \le f$, because $\mu \ge 0$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.morseF_le_morseQuad⟧, and $F = f$ outside the ellipsoid, where $\xi + 2\eta > 2\epsilon$ and therefore $\mu(\xi + 2\eta) = 0$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.morseF_eq_of_notMem_ellipsoid⟧.

**Assertion 1, in coordinates.** $F(v) \le c + \epsilon$ if and only if $f(v) \le c + \epsilon$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.morseF_le_iff⟧.

*Proof.* Outside the ellipsoid $F = f$. Inside it both inequalities hold: $\xi + 2\eta \le 2\epsilon$ gives $\frac12\xi + \eta \le \epsilon$, so $F \le f = c - \xi + \eta \le c + \frac12\xi + \eta \le c + \epsilon$. $\square$

**Assertion 2, in coordinates.** The derivatives of $\xi$ and $\eta$ are $d\xi(h, k) = 2\langle x, h\rangle$ and $d\eta(h, k) = 2\langle y, k\rangle$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.hasFDerivAt_xi⟧ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.hasFDerivAt_eta⟧. Writing $\mu'$ for $\mu'(\xi + 2\eta)$, the chain rule gives ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.hasFDerivAt_morseF⟧
$$dF = -(1 + \mu')\, d\xi + (1 - 2\mu')\, d\eta.$$
This is Milnor's $dF = \frac{\partial F}{\partial\xi}\,d\xi + \frac{\partial F}{\partial\eta}\,d\eta$, with $\partial F/\partial\xi = -1 - \mu' < 0$ and $\partial F/\partial\eta = 1 - 2\mu' \ge 1$. If $\mu$ is differentiable with $-1 < \mu' \le 0$ and $dF = 0$ at $v$, then $v = 0$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.eq_zero_of_hasFDerivAt_morseF⟧: evaluating $dF$ on $(x, 0)$ gives $-2(1 + \mu')\|x\|^2 = 0$ with $1 + \mu' > 0$, so $x = 0$; evaluating it on $(0, y)$ gives $2(1 - 2\mu')\|y\|^2 = 0$ with $1 - 2\mu' \ge 1$, so $y = 0$. Milnor's conclusion, that $dF$ vanishes only where $d\xi$ and $d\eta$ vanish simultaneously, uses that $d\xi$ and $d\eta$ involve disjoint sets of coordinates; the evaluations on $(x, 0)$ and $(0, y)$ make this explicit. The origin is a critical point ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.hasFDerivAt_morseF_zero⟧. So for $\mu$ satisfying Milnor's conditions the only critical point of $F$ is the origin ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.hasFDerivAt_morseF_zero_iff⟧, and the same computation with $\mu = 0$ gives the same for $f$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.hasFDerivAt_morseQuad_zero_iff⟧.

*The bound $\partial F/\partial\eta \ge 1$.* As reproduced in the scan, Milnor's display reads $> 1$, with the "$\partial F$" of the numerator dropped, so the scan may also have lost the bar of a $\ge$. Since $\mu' = 0$ on $[2\epsilon, \infty)$, the correct bound is $\ge 1$, and that is all the proof uses.

**The cell lies below the level $c - \epsilon$ of $F$.** On $e^\lambda$ one has $\eta = 0$, so $F = c - (\xi + \mu(\xi))$, and since $s \mapsto s + \mu(s)$ is increasing and $\xi \ge 0$, $F \le c - \mu(0) < c - \epsilon$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.morseF_lt_of_mem_cellModel⟧. This is Milnor's inequality $F(q) \le F(p) < c - \epsilon$ (p. 18), there justified by $\partial F/\partial\xi < 0$, made into a statement about the whole cell. In particular $F(p) = c - \mu(0) < c - \epsilon$.

**The cell meets $M^{c-\epsilon}$ in its boundary sphere.** On the cell $f = c - \xi$, which is at most $c - \epsilon$ exactly when $\xi \ge \epsilon$; with $\xi \le \epsilon$ this means $\xi = \epsilon$. So $e^\lambda \cap M^{c-\epsilon} = \{\xi = \epsilon,\ y = 0\}$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.cellModel_inter_sublevel⟧, the boundary sphere of the cell, as Milnor notes on p. 16.

**$F^{-1}(-\infty, c - \epsilon] = M^{c-\epsilon} \cup H$.** If $A \subseteq B$ and $B$ is closed, then $B = A \cup \overline{B \setminus A}$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · eq_union_closure_diff⟧: a point of $B$ not in $A$ lies in $B \setminus A$, and the closure of $B \setminus A$ lies in the closed set $B$. Here $A = M^{c-\epsilon}$ is contained in $B = F^{-1}(-\infty, c - \epsilon]$ because $F \le f$, and $B$ is closed because $F$ is continuous ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.sublevel_morseF_eq_union⟧. Milnor writes this equality (p. 17) as notation; it needs exactly these two facts.

**$e^\lambda \subseteq H$** (p. 18). The points of the cell with $\xi < \epsilon$ lie in $F^{-1}(-\infty, c-\epsilon] \setminus M^{c-\epsilon}$: there $F < c - \epsilon$ while $f = c - \xi > c - \epsilon$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.mem_diff_of_mem_cellModel⟧. Every point $(x, 0)$ of the cell is the limit, as $s \to 1$ from below, of the points $(sx, 0)$, which lie in the cell with $\xi = s^2\|x\|^2 < \epsilon$ for $0 < s < 1$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.tendsto_smul_cellModel⟧. So the whole cell lies in the closure $H$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.cellModel_subset_handleModel⟧. The points with $\xi = \epsilon$ are in $M^{c-\epsilon}$ and belong to $H$ only as such limits; Milnor's text does not distinguish them. (The scan's "$f(q) \ge c - e$" on p. 18 reads $c - \epsilon$.)

### II.9 Assertion 4 in Morse coordinates: $r_t$ as one formula

Milnor's Assertion 4 (pp. 18–19, Diagram 7) deforms $M^{c-\epsilon} \cup H$ onto $M^{c-\epsilon} \cup e^\lambda$ by moving points "vertically", shrinking $y$ and keeping $x$. He gives three cases: in Case 1, $\xi \le \epsilon$, $r_t(x, y) = (x, ty)$; in Case 2, $\epsilon \le \xi \le \eta + \epsilon$, $r_t(x, y) = (x, s_t\, y)$ with $s_t = t + (1 - t)\big((\xi - \epsilon)/\eta\big)^{1/2}$; in Case 3, $\eta + \epsilon \le \xi$, $r_t$ is the identity. His $r_1$ is the identity and $r_0$ the retraction. The formal proof lets the parameter run the other way, as in Theorem 3.1 and in Mathlib's homotopies, and writes the three cases as one formula ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.retractScale⟧ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.retractModel⟧:
$$\sigma_t(v) = 1 - t + t\,\sqrt{\min\Big(1,\ \frac{\xi - \epsilon}{\eta}\Big)}, \qquad r_t(x, y) = \big(x,\ \sigma_t(v)\, y\big).$$
Two conventions of the formal language, under which every function is total, make the single formula work: division by $0$ gives $0$, and the square root of a negative number is $0$.

**The three cases.**

- *Case 1*, $\xi \le \epsilon$: $r_t(x, y) = (x, (1 - t)\,y)$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.retractModel_of_xi_le⟧. If $\eta = 0$ then $y = 0$ and nothing moves. Otherwise $(\xi - \epsilon)/\eta \le 0$, so the minimum is $\le 0$, its square root is $0$, and $\sigma_t = 1 - t$.
- *Case 2*, $\xi \le \eta + \epsilon$ with $\eta > 0$: then $(\xi - \epsilon)/\eta \le 1$ and $\sigma_t = 1 - t + t\,\big((\xi - \epsilon)/\eta\big)^{1/2}$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.retractModel_of_case2⟧, which is Milnor's $s_t$ with $t$ replaced by $1 - t$. The hypothesis $\epsilon \le \xi$ is not needed: for $\xi < \epsilon$ the square root is $0$ and this is Case 1.
- *Case 3*, $\eta + \epsilon \le \xi$: $r_t$ is the identity ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.retractModel_of_le_xi⟧. If $\eta > 0$, then $(\xi - \epsilon)/\eta \ge 1$ and the minimum is $1$; if $\eta = 0$ nothing moves.

The single formula agrees with Milnor's three on their overlaps automatically, so the compatibility checks he makes (on $\xi = \epsilon$ Cases 1 and 2 agree, on $\xi = \eta + \epsilon$ Cases 2 and 3 agree) are built in. *The point $\xi = \epsilon$, $\eta = 0$*, where Milnor's Case 2 reads $0/0$ and which he does not discuss, is harmless: there $y = 0$, and a point with $y = 0$ is fixed by every $r_t$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.retractModel_of_snd_eq_zero⟧.

**Elementary properties.** For $0 \le t \le 1$, $0 \le \sigma_t \le 1$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.retractScale_nonneg⟧ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.retractScale_le_one⟧. $\sigma_0 = 1$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.retractScale_zero⟧, so $r_0$ is the identity ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.retractModel_zero⟧. $r_t$ keeps $\xi$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.xi_retractModel⟧ and multiplies $\eta$ by $\sigma_t^2$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.eta_retractModel⟧, so it does not increase $\eta$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.eta_retractModel_le⟧. It fixes $M^{c-\epsilon}$, which in coordinates is $\{f \le c - \epsilon\} = \{\eta + \epsilon \le \xi\}$, Case 3 ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.retractModel_of_morseQuad_le⟧, and it fixes the cell, where $y = 0$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.retractModel_of_mem_cellModel⟧.

**Continuity.** Milnor leaves it to the reader to check that the functions $s_t u^i$ stay continuous as $\xi \to \epsilon$ and $\eta \to 0$ (p. 19). Formally, $(t, v) \mapsto r_t(v)$ is continuous on all of $[0, 1] \times X \times Y$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.continuous_retractModel⟧, not only on $F^{-1}(-\infty, c - \epsilon]$. At a point $(t_0, v_0)$ with $\eta(v_0) \ne 0$, the quotient $(\xi - \epsilon)/\eta$ is continuous nearby, and so are the minimum, the square root, the product with $t$, and the product with $y$. At a point with $\eta(v_0) = 0$, that is $v_0 = (x_0, 0)$, the factor $\sigma_t$ itself is discontinuous. But $r_{t_0}(v_0) = v_0$, and, because $0 \le \sigma_t \le 1$, the distance from $r_t(v)$ to $v_0$ in the maximum norm of $X \times Y$ is at most that from $v$ to $v_0$: the $x$-components are unchanged, and $\|\sigma_t\, y - 0\| \le \|y\|$. So $r_t(v) \to v_0$ as $(t, v) \to (t_0, v_0)$.

**What $r_t$ does.**

- *$r_t$ maps every sublevel set of $F$ into itself* (p. 19): $F(r_t(v)) \le F(v)$ for $0 \le t \le 1$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.morseF_retractModel_le⟧. The deformation keeps $\xi$ and replaces $\eta$ by some $\eta' \le \eta$. As $\mu$ is decreasing, $\mu(\xi + 2\eta') \ge \mu(\xi + 2\eta)$, and therefore $c - \xi + \eta' - \mu(\xi + 2\eta') \le c - \xi + \eta - \mu(\xi + 2\eta)$. This is Milnor's "$F$ is increasing in $\eta$", in integrated form.
- *$r_1$ lands in $M^{c-\epsilon} \cup e^\lambda$* ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.retractModel_one_mem⟧, for every point of $X \times Y$. Case 1 goes to $(x, 0)$ with $\xi \le \epsilon$, a point of the cell. In Case 2 with $\eta > 0$ the new value of $\eta$ is $\frac{\xi - \epsilon}{\eta}\cdot \eta = \xi - \epsilon$, so $f = c - \xi + (\xi - \epsilon) = c - \epsilon$: the point lands on the hypersurface $f^{-1}(c - \epsilon)$, as Milnor says. Case 2 with $\eta = 0$ is the point $\xi = \epsilon$, $y = 0$, already at level $c - \epsilon$. Case 3 is already in $M^{c-\epsilon}$.
- *$r_t$ keeps the ellipsoid in itself* ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.retractModel_mem_ellipsoid⟧, since it keeps $\xi$ and does not increase $\eta$. This holds for the deformation built with any level $\delta$ in place of $\epsilon$.
- *On $F^{-1}(-\infty, c - \delta]$, the deformation of level $\delta$ moves only points of the ellipsoid* ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.retractModel_of_notMem_ellipsoid⟧. Outside the ellipsoid $F = f$, so $f \le c - \delta$, which is Case 3 for the level $\delta$. This is what will make "the identity outside the coordinate neighbourhood" continuous (§II.10).

**Assertion 4, in coordinates.** $M^{c-\epsilon} \cup e^\lambda$ is a strong deformation retract of $F^{-1}(-\infty, c - \epsilon] = M^{c-\epsilon} \cup H$, in $X \times Y$ ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.isStrongDeformationRetract_model⟧. Each $r_t$, $0 \le t \le 1$, maps $F^{-1}(-\infty, c-\epsilon]$ into itself ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.retractModel_mem_sublevel⟧; $r_1$ is the retraction ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.retractOne⟧; and $(t, v) \mapsto r_t(v)$ is a homotopy from the identity to $r_1$ which fixes $M^{c-\epsilon} \cup e^\lambda$ at all times ⟦Chapter2/CellModel · CellModel.retractHomotopy⟧.

### II.10 Theorem 3.2 in a Euclidean space

Let $E$ be a finite-dimensional real inner product space; the inner product is needed only for Theorem 3.1, which is applied to $F$.

**Morse charts in split form.** The lemma of Morse (Lemma 2.2, proved in Part I) gives, for a non-degenerate critical point $p$ of a smooth $f$ on $E$, coordinates $y = (y^0, \dots, y^{n-1})$ near $p$, smooth with smooth inverse, with $y(p) = 0$ and
$$f = f(p) - \sum_{i < \lambda} (y^i)^2 + \sum_{i \ge \lambda} (y^i)^2$$
throughout their domain, where $\lambda$ is the index of the Hessian ⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · morse_lemma⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · hessian⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · index⟧ (§0.2). Milnor's splitting $u = (x, y)$ is the linear isomorphism of $\mathbb R^n$ with the product of the Euclidean spaces of the first $\lambda$ and of the last $n - \lambda$ coordinates ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · splitCoord⟧, under which $\xi = \sum_{i<\lambda} u_i^2$ and $\eta = \sum_{i \ge \lambda} u_i^2$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · norm_sq_splitCoord_fst⟧ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · norm_sq_splitCoord_snd⟧. A **split Morse chart** at $p$ is an open partial homeomorphism $\varphi$ from $E$ to $X \times Y$ with $p$ in its domain, $\varphi(p) = 0$, $\varphi$ smooth on its domain, $\varphi^{-1}$ smooth on its image, and $f = f(p) - \xi\circ\varphi + \eta\circ\varphi$ on its domain ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · IsSplitMorseChart⟧. The Morse lemma followed by the splitting provides one with $X = \mathbb R^\lambda$, $Y = \mathbb R^{n-\lambda}$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · exists_isSplitMorseChart⟧. Critical points depend only on the germ of a function ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · isCriticalPoint_congr⟧, and they correspond under a Morse chart: $g \circ \varphi$ is critical at $q$ if and only if $g$ is critical at $\varphi(q)$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · IsSplitMorseChart.isCriticalPoint_comp_iff⟧, by the chain rule applied with $\varphi$ in one direction and with $\varphi^{-1}$ in the other.

Everything below except the final assembly is stated for an arbitrary split Morse chart, so that it can be reused for charts of a manifold. The standing hypothesis, Milnor's condition (2) on p. 15, is that the image of $\varphi$ contains the ball $\{\xi + \eta \le 2\epsilon\}$. Then the ellipsoid lies in the image ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · ellipsoid_subset_target⟧, its preimage, the ellipsoid in $E$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · ellipsoidE⟧, lies in the domain of $\varphi$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · ellipsoidE_subset_source⟧, and a point $q$ of the domain lies in it exactly when $\varphi(q)$ lies in the ellipsoid ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · mem_ellipsoidE_iff⟧.

**Milnor's $F$ on $E$.** The correction $\mu(\xi + 2\eta)$ is transported by the chart and extended by $0$ outside its domain ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · milnorBump⟧, and $F = f$ minus this correction ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · milnorF⟧. This is Milnor's definition: $F = f - \mu(\xi + 2\eta)$ within the coordinate neighbourhood $U$, and $F = f$ outside it. The cell and the handle in $E$ are $e^\lambda = \varphi^{-1}(\text{cell})$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · cellE⟧ and $H = \overline{F^{-1}(-\infty, c-\epsilon] \setminus M^{c-\epsilon}}$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · handleE⟧.

**$F$ is smooth.** Milnor says this is easily verified. The verification needs the ellipsoid in $E$ to be closed. The ellipsoid in $X \times Y$ is closed and bounded in a finite-dimensional space, hence compact ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · isCompact_ellipsoid⟧; its image under the continuous map $\varphi^{-1}$ is compact ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · isCompact_ellipsoidE⟧, hence closed in the Hausdorff space $E$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · isClosed_ellipsoidE⟧. Outside it the correction vanishes ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · milnorBump_eq_zero⟧. Near a point of the chart domain the correction equals the smooth function $\mu(\xi\circ\varphi + 2\,\eta\circ\varphi)$. Near a point outside the domain, which lies in the open complement of the closed ellipsoid, it is identically $0$. So the correction is smooth ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · contDiff_milnorBump⟧, and so is $F$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · contDiff_milnorF⟧.

Moreover $F = (\text{the model } F) \circ \varphi$ on the chart domain ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · milnorF_eq⟧, $F = f$ outside the ellipsoid ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · milnorF_eq_of_notMem⟧, $F \le f$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · milnorF_le⟧, and $F(p) = c - \mu(0)$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · milnorF_apply_self⟧.

**Assertion 1.** $F^{-1}(-\infty, c + \epsilon] = M^{c+\epsilon}$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · sublevel_milnorF_add⟧: on the chart domain this is Assertion 1 in coordinates, and outside it $F = f$.

**Assertion 2.** $F$ and $f$ have the same critical points ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · isCriticalPoint_milnorF_iff⟧. At a point $q$ of the chart domain, $F$ and $f$ agree near $q$ with the model functions composed with $\varphi$; by the correspondence of critical points under $\varphi$, $q$ is critical for either of them exactly when $\varphi(q) = 0$, by the computation in coordinates. At a point outside the domain, which lies outside the closed ellipsoid, $F = f$ on a neighbourhood.

**The handle and the cell.** $F^{-1}(-\infty, c-\epsilon] = M^{c-\epsilon} \cup H$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · sublevel_milnorF_eq_union⟧, by the closure argument of §II.8. $e^\lambda \subseteq H$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · cellE_subset_handleE⟧: the curve $s \mapsto \varphi^{-1}(sx, 0)$ converges, by the continuity of $\varphi^{-1}$ on its domain, and lies in $F^{-1}(-\infty, c - \epsilon] \setminus M^{c-\epsilon}$ for $s < 1$. $e^\lambda \subseteq F^{-1}(-\infty, c - \epsilon]$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · cellE_subset_sublevel_milnorF⟧. And $e^\lambda \cap M^{c-\epsilon}$ is the image of the sphere $\{\xi = \epsilon,\ y = 0\}$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · cellE_inter_sublevel⟧.

**Assertion 3.** If $f^{-1}[c - \epsilon, c + \epsilon]$ is compact and contains no critical point other than $p$, then $F^{-1}(-\infty, c - \epsilon]$ is a strong deformation retract of $M^{c+\epsilon}$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · isStrongDeformationRetract_milnorF⟧.

*Proof.* First, $F^{-1}[c - \epsilon, c + \epsilon] \subseteq f^{-1}[c - \epsilon, c + \epsilon]$: $F \ge c - \epsilon$ gives $f \ge F \ge c - \epsilon$, and $F \le c + \epsilon$ gives $f \le c + \epsilon$ by Assertion 1. Being closed in a compact set, $F^{-1}[c - \epsilon, c + \epsilon]$ is compact; Milnor needs this closedness and does not mention it. A critical point of $F$ in it would be a critical point of $f$ (Assertion 2, used in the direction from $F$ to $f$), hence $p$; but $F(p) = c - \mu(0) < c - \epsilon$, so $p$ is not in it. Theorem 3.1 (§II.4), applied to $F$ with $a = c - \epsilon$ and $b = c + \epsilon$, makes $F^{-1}(-\infty, c - \epsilon]$ a strong deformation retract of $F^{-1}(-\infty, c + \epsilon]$, which is $M^{c+\epsilon}$ by Assertion 1. $\square$

**Assertion 4 on $E$: the identity outside the chart.** Milnor lets $r_t$ be the identity outside $U$. The formal deformation is ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · handleRetract⟧ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · handleRetract_of_mem⟧ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · handleRetract_of_notMem⟧
$$r_t(q) = \varphi^{-1}\big(r_t(\varphi(q))\big) \ \text{ for } q \text{ in the chart domain}, \qquad r_t(q) = q \ \text{ otherwise}.$$
It is built with a level $\delta$ ($\delta = \epsilon$ here, $\delta = 0$ for Remark 3.4), while the chart, $\mu$ and the ellipsoid keep their $\epsilon$. Milnor does not check that this is well defined and continuous. The formal proof does, from one observation: *on $F^{-1}(-\infty, c - \delta]$, the deformation never leaves the chart*, that is, $r_t(\varphi(q))$ lies in the image of $\varphi$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · retractModel_mem_target⟧. If $\varphi(q)$ is in the ellipsoid, $r_t$ keeps it in the ellipsoid, which lies in the image of $\varphi$; this is a second use of condition (2), which Milnor does not mention. If $\varphi(q)$ is not in the ellipsoid, $r_t$ fixes it (§II.9). From this:

- $r_t$ does not increase $F$ on $F^{-1}(-\infty, c - \delta]$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · milnorF_handleRetract_le⟧, so it maps this set into itself for $0 \le t \le 1$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · handleRetract_mem⟧;
- $r_0$ is the identity ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · handleRetract_zero⟧, and $r_t$ fixes $M^{c-\delta}$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · handleRetract_of_mem_sublevel⟧ and the cell ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · handleRetract_of_mem_cellE⟧;
- on $F^{-1}(-\infty, c - \delta]$, $r_t$ is the identity outside the ellipsoid ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · handleRetract_of_notMem_ellipsoidE⟧;
- $r_1$ maps $F^{-1}(-\infty, c - \delta]$ into $M^{c-\delta}$ together with the cell of radius $\delta$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · handleRetract_one_mem⟧: on the chart domain this is the statement in coordinates, and outside it, hence outside the ellipsoid, $f = F \le c - \delta$;
- *continuity:* $(t, q) \mapsto r_t(q)$ is continuous on $[0,1] \times F^{-1}(-\infty, c - \delta]$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · continuous_handleRetract⟧. Near a point $(t_0, q_0)$ with $q_0$ in the open chart domain, $r_t = \varphi^{-1} \circ r_t \circ \varphi$ there, which is continuous because $\varphi$ is continuous at $q_0$, the deformation in coordinates is continuous, and $\varphi^{-1}$ is continuous at $r_{t_0}(\varphi(q_0))$, a point of the image of $\varphi$ by the observation. Near a point with $q_0$ outside the domain, $q_0$ lies outside the closed ellipsoid; the points of $F^{-1}(-\infty, c - \delta]$ outside the ellipsoid form an open neighbourhood of it, and there $r_t$ is the identity. This step uses the closedness of the ellipsoid, hence that $E$ is Hausdorff.

**Assertion 4.** $M^{c-\epsilon} \cup e^\lambda$ is a strong deformation retract of $F^{-1}(-\infty, c - \epsilon] = M^{c-\epsilon} \cup H$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · isStrongDeformationRetract_handle⟧, with the retraction $r_1$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · handleRetractOne⟧ and the homotopy $(t, q) \mapsto r_t(q)$, which fixes every set that each $r_t$ fixes ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · handleHomotopy⟧.

**The conclusion, given a chart.** If the image of the chart contains $\{\xi + \eta \le 2\epsilon\}$ and $f^{-1}[c-\epsilon, c+\epsilon]$ is compact with no critical point other than $p$, then $M^{c-\epsilon} \cup e^\lambda$ is a strong deformation retract of $M^{c+\epsilon}$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · isStrongDeformationRetract_union_cellE⟧. Indeed $M^{c-\epsilon} \cup e^\lambda \subseteq F^{-1}(-\infty, c - \epsilon] \subseteq M^{c+\epsilon}$, because $F \le f$, the cell lies below $c - \epsilon$ for $F$, and Assertion 1; and Lemma A(i) (§II.1) composes Assertion 3 with Assertion 4. This is the composition behind Milnor's closing sentence of the proof (p. 19), that Assertion 4 together with Assertion 3 completes it.

**"For all sufficiently small $\epsilon$."** Milnor chooses $\epsilon$ so small that (1) $f^{-1}[c - \epsilon, c + \epsilon]$ is compact and contains no critical point other than $p$, and (2) the image of the chart contains the ball $\sum (u^i)^2 \le 2\epsilon$. Both conditions persist for smaller $\epsilon$, and this is how "for all sufficiently small $\epsilon$" is formalized: there is $\epsilon_1 > 0$ such that the conclusion holds for every $\epsilon \in (0, \epsilon_1]$. Condition (2): the image of $\varphi$ is open and contains $0$, so it contains a ball of some radius $r$, and for $\epsilon \le r^2/4$ the set $\{\xi + \eta \le 2\epsilon\}$ lies in that ball, because then $\|x\|^2$ and $\|y\|^2$ are both less than $r^2$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · exists_forall_ball_subset_target⟧. Condition (1): $f^{-1}[c - \epsilon, c + \epsilon]$ is closed in the compact $f^{-1}[c - \epsilon_0, c + \epsilon_0]$ of the hypothesis. Together, with $\epsilon_1 = \min(\epsilon_0, r^2/4)$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · exists_morseChart_forall_small⟧.

**The cell as an embedded disk.** For a linear isometry $\iota$ from $X$ onto a Euclidean space, the map
$$\chi(z) = \varphi^{-1}\big(\sqrt\epsilon\, \iota^{-1}(z),\ 0\big)$$
sends the closed unit ball onto $e^\lambda$, is continuous and injective on the ball, and satisfies $f(\chi(z)) = c - \epsilon|z|^2$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · cellE_parametrization⟧. The point $(\sqrt\epsilon\,\iota^{-1}z, 0)$ has $\xi = \epsilon|z|^2 \le \epsilon$, so it lies in the cell and in the image of $\varphi$, on which $\varphi^{-1}$ is continuous and injective; conversely a point $(x, 0)$ of the cell is $\chi(\iota(x/\sqrt\epsilon))$. In the final statement, the space of the first $\lambda$ coordinates is identified with $\mathbb R^\lambda$ by renumbering ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · negIdxEquiv⟧.

**Theorem 3.2 (V2.6).** Let $f : E \to \mathbb R$ be $C^\infty$, $p$ a non-degenerate critical point of index $\lambda$, $c = f(p)$, and suppose that for some $\epsilon_0 > 0$ the set $f^{-1}[c - \epsilon_0, c + \epsilon_0]$ is compact and contains no critical point other than $p$. Then there is $\epsilon_1 > 0$ such that for every $\epsilon \in (0, \epsilon_1]$ there is a map $\chi$ from the closed unit ball $D^\lambda$ of $\mathbb R^\lambda$ to $E$, continuous and injective on $D^\lambda$, with $\chi(z) \in M^{c-\epsilon}$ exactly when $|z| = 1$ and with $\chi(D^\lambda) \subseteq M^{c+\epsilon}$, such that $M^{c-\epsilon} \cup \chi(D^\lambda)$ is a strong deformation retract of $M^{c+\epsilon}$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · theorem_3_2⟧. In particular the inclusion of $M^{c-\epsilon} \cup \chi(D^\lambda)$ in $M^{c+\epsilon}$ is a homotopy equivalence ⟦Chapter2/CellAttach · theorem_3_2_homotopyEquiv⟧.

*Proof.* Choose the chart and $\epsilon_1$ as above. For $\epsilon \in (0, \epsilon_1]$ take for $\mu$ the function of §II.7 for this $\epsilon$, and $\chi$ as above. For $|z| \le 1$, $\chi(z) \in M^{c-\epsilon}$ means $c - \epsilon|z|^2 \le c - \epsilon$, that is $|z|^2 \ge 1$, that is $|z| = 1$; and $c - \epsilon|z|^2 \le c + \epsilon$ always. The strong deformation retraction is the conclusion given a chart. The homotopy equivalence follows by the lemma of §II.1. $\square$

*Stronger than the book.* Milnor states only that $M^{c+\epsilon}$ has the homotopy type of $M^{c-\epsilon}$ with a $\lambda$-cell attached. The formal statement records what the proof produces, a strong deformation retraction onto $M^{c-\epsilon}$ together with an embedded cell; §II.11 derives Milnor's statement from it. As reproduced in the scan, the statement of Theorem 3.2 ends with "a $k$-cell" and renders the index as "A"; the proof requires a $\lambda$-cell, and both are read so.

### II.11 Lemma D: the subspace is the attached cell

Milnor's theorem speaks of $M^{c-\epsilon}$ *with a $\lambda$-cell attached*: the space obtained from the disjoint union of $M^{c-\epsilon}$ and the closed disk $D^\lambda$ by identifying each $u \in S^{\lambda-1}$ with $\varphi(u)$, for an attaching map $\varphi$ (§0.5). His proof produces the subspace $M^{c-\epsilon} \cup e^\lambda$ of $M$, and the passage from one to the other is a single remark, that $e^\lambda \cap M^{c-\epsilon}$ is the boundary of the cell, "so that" the cell is attached as required (p. 16). The exposition states the missing lemma as Lemma D.

In the formal proof the attaching space is Mathlib's pushout, in the category of topological spaces, of $\varphi : S^{\lambda-1} \to M^{c-\epsilon}$ and the inclusion $S^{\lambda-1} \to D^\lambda$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Category/TopCat/Sphere · TopCat.diskBoundaryInclusion⟧, where $D^\lambda$ and $S^{\lambda-1}$ are the closed unit ball and the unit sphere of $\mathbb R^\lambda$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Category/TopCat/Sphere · TopCat.disk⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Category/TopCat/Sphere · TopCat.diskBoundary⟧, the basic cell of Mathlib's CW complexes. A commutative square is a pushout when it has the universal property of one ⟦Mathlib/CategoryTheory/Limits/Shapes/Pullback/IsPullback/Defs · CategoryTheory.IsPushout⟧.

**Lemma D (V2.7).** Let $W$ be a Hausdorff space, $j : A \to W$ a closed embedding, $D$ a compact space, $\chi : D \to W$ continuous and injective, and $i : S \to D$, $\varphi : S \to A$ continuous with $j \circ \varphi = \chi \circ i$. Suppose that $W = j(A) \cup \chi(D)$ and that $\chi(d) \in j(A)$ only for $d \in i(S)$. Then the square formed by $\varphi$, $i$, $j$ and $\chi$ is a pushout: $W$ is $A$ with $D$ attached along $\varphi$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAdjunction · isPushout_of_cell⟧.

*Proof.* The exposition argues with a closed quotient map; the formal proof checks the universal property directly.

1. $\chi$ is a continuous injection of a compact space into a Hausdorff space, hence a closed embedding, a homeomorphism onto its closed image $\chi(D)$. Likewise $j$ is a homeomorphism onto its closed image $j(A)$.
2. Let $a : A \to P$ and $d : D \to P$ be continuous with $a \circ \varphi = d \circ i$. Define $g : W \to P$ by $g(w) = a(j^{-1}(w))$ if $w \in j(A)$, and $g(w) = d(\chi^{-1}(w))$ otherwise; such $w$ lie in $\chi(D)$ by the covering hypothesis.
3. $g \circ j = a$ by definition. Also $g \circ \chi = d$: if $\chi(x) \notin j(A)$ this is the definition; if $\chi(x) \in j(A)$, then $x = i(s)$ for some $s$, so $\chi(x) = j(\varphi(s))$ and $g(\chi(x)) = a(\varphi(s)) = d(i(s)) = d(x)$.
4. $g$ is continuous by the pasting lemma for the closed cover $W = j(A) \cup \chi(D)$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/ContinuousOn · ContinuousOn.union_of_isClosed⟧: on $j(A)$ it is $a \circ j^{-1}$, and on $\chi(D)$ it is $d \circ \chi^{-1}$ by step 3, both continuous.
5. Uniqueness: a map which agrees with $a$ on $j(A)$ and with $d$ on $\chi(D)$ agrees with $g$ everywhere, since these sets cover $W$. $\square$

**Lemma D for a cell in a normed space.** Let $A$ be a closed subset of a normed space $E$, and $\chi : \mathbb R^n \to E$ continuous and injective on $D^n$ with $\chi(z) \in A$ exactly when $|z| = 1$, for $z \in D^n$. Then $A \cup \chi(D^n)$ is the pushout of $\varphi = \chi|_{S^{n-1}} : S^{n-1} \to A$ and $S^{n-1} \to D^n$, and $\varphi$ is injective ⟦Chapter2/CellAdjunction · exists_isPushout_union_cell⟧. This is Lemma D with $W = A \cup \chi(D^n)$, which is Hausdorff as a subspace of $E$; $j$ the inclusion of $A$, a closed embedding because $A$ is closed; and $\chi$ and $i$ the evident maps. $W$ is covered by $A$ and $\chi(D^n)$, and $\chi(z) \in A$ forces $|z| = 1$, that is $z \in S^{n-1}$. The same proof works in any Hausdorff space ⟦Chapter2/CellPatch · exists_isPushout_union_cell_of_t2⟧. *Hidden hypotheses:* the ambient space is Hausdorff and $A$ is closed, so that the cell is closed and $\chi$ is a homeomorphism onto it.

**Theorem 3.2 with an attaching space (V2.6).** Under the hypotheses of Theorem 3.2, there is $\epsilon_1 > 0$ such that for every $\epsilon \in (0, \epsilon_1]$ there is an injective continuous $\varphi : S^{\lambda-1} \to M^{c-\epsilon}$ for which $M^{c+\epsilon}$ is homotopy equivalent to the pushout of $D^\lambda \leftarrow S^{\lambda-1} \rightarrow M^{c-\epsilon}$, that is, to $M^{c-\epsilon} \cup_\varphi e^\lambda$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAdjunction · theorem_3_2_attach⟧.

*Proof.* Theorem 3.2 gives $\chi$ and the homotopy equivalence of $M^{c-\epsilon} \cup \chi(D^\lambda)$ with $M^{c+\epsilon}$. The set $M^{c-\epsilon}$ is closed, as $f$ is continuous. Lemma D identifies $M^{c-\epsilon} \cup \chi(D^\lambda)$ with the pushout up to the canonical isomorphism of two pushouts of the same diagram ⟦Mathlib/CategoryTheory/Limits/Shapes/Pullback/IsPullback/Defs · CategoryTheory.IsPushout.isoPushout⟧, which in the category of topological spaces is a homeomorphism. Composing gives the homotopy equivalence. $\square$

The same deduction, for any continuous function on a Hausdorff space and levels $a \le b$, is ⟦Chapter2/CellPatch · exists_homotopyEquiv_pushout_of_cell⟧: if $M^a \cup \chi(D^n)$ is a strong deformation retract of $M^b$, for a cell $\chi(D^n) \subseteq M^b$ meeting $M^a$ exactly in $\chi(S^{n-1})$, then $M^b$ is homotopy equivalent to $M^a \cup_\varphi e^n$ with $\varphi$ injective.

### II.13 Theorem 3.2 on a submanifold, through one chart

The proof of Theorem 3.2 has two kinds of steps. Assertions 2 and 3 use the smooth structure: critical points of $F$, and Theorem 3.1 applied to $F$. Everything else (the definition of $F$, Assertion 1, the cell, the deformation of Assertion 4 and Lemma D) takes place in the coordinate neighbourhood of $p$ and uses only topology. The formal proof first redoes the topological part in a coordinate patch of an arbitrary Hausdorff space, then supplies Assertions 2 and 3 on a submanifold.

**Morse patches.** Let $M$ be a topological space, $f : M \to \mathbb R$ and $p \in M$. A **Morse patch** at $p$ is an open partial homeomorphism $\varphi$ from $M$ to $X \times Y$ with $p$ in its domain, $\varphi(p) = 0$, and $f = f(p) - \xi\circ\varphi + \eta\circ\varphi$ on its domain ⟦Chapter2/CellPatch · IsMorsePatch⟧. No smoothness is required. The constructions of §II.10 carry over word for word: the correction ⟦Chapter2/CellPatch · patchBump⟧, Milnor's $F$ ⟦Chapter2/CellPatch · patchF⟧, the ellipsoid ⟦Chapter2/CellPatch · patchEllipsoid⟧ and the cell ⟦Chapter2/CellPatch · patchCell⟧ in $M$, and the deformation, $\varphi^{-1} \circ r_t \circ \varphi$ on the patch and the identity elsewhere ⟦Chapter2/CellPatch · patchRetract⟧. So do the proofs, with $E$ replaced by $M$: Assertion 1 ⟦Chapter2/CellPatch · sublevel_patchF_add⟧; the cell lies below $c - \epsilon$ for $F$ ⟦Chapter2/CellPatch · patchCell_subset_sublevel_patchF⟧; on $F^{-1}(-\infty, c-\delta]$ the deformation never leaves the patch ⟦Chapter2/CellPatch · retractModel_mem_target_of_patchF_le⟧; Assertion 4 ⟦Chapter2/CellPatch · isStrongDeformationRetract_patch_handle⟧; the choice of $\epsilon$ ⟦Chapter2/CellPatch · exists_forall_ball_subset_patch_target⟧; and the parametrization of the cell ⟦Chapter2/CellPatch · patchCell_parametrization⟧.

*Hausdorffness* is used twice. The ellipsoid in $M$ is compact, as the image of a compact set under $\varphi^{-1}$ ⟦Chapter2/CellPatch · isCompact_patchEllipsoid⟧, and closed when $M$ is Hausdorff ⟦Chapter2/CellPatch · isClosed_patchEllipsoid⟧; that makes the deformation continuous ⟦Chapter2/CellPatch · continuous_patchRetract⟧, and, on a manifold, $F$ smooth. And Lemma D needs a Hausdorff space. A manifold in Milnor's sense, a subset of some $\mathbb R^K$, is Hausdorff; an abstract manifold need not be.

**Theorem 3.2 in a patch, given Assertion 3 (V2.8 (i)).** Let $M$ be Hausdorff, $\varphi$ a Morse patch at $p$ whose image contains $\{\xi + \eta \le 2\epsilon\}$, $\epsilon > 0$, $\mu$ a function with Milnor's four properties, and $\iota$ a linear isometry from $X$ onto $\mathbb R^k$. Suppose that $F^{-1}(-\infty, c - \epsilon]$ is a strong deformation retract of $M^{c+\epsilon}$. Then $\chi(z) = \varphi^{-1}(\sqrt\epsilon\,\iota^{-1}z, 0)$ is continuous and injective on $D^k$, meets $M^{c-\epsilon}$ exactly in $\chi(S^{k-1})$, lies in $M^{c+\epsilon}$, and $M^{c-\epsilon} \cup \chi(D^k)$ is a strong deformation retract of $M^{c+\epsilon}$ ⟦Chapter2/CellPatch · patch_theorem_3_2⟧. The proof is that of §II.10: Lemma A(i) composes the hypothesis with Assertion 4.

**On a submanifold.** Now $N \subseteq V$ is a slice submanifold, $F : V \to \mathbb R$ is smooth and $f = F|N$, so $f$ is smooth on $N$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · contMDiff_comp_val⟧. Three things remain to be supplied: a Morse patch of $N$, the smoothness of Milnor's $F$ on $N$, and Assertion 3.

*The Morse chart of a manifold.* Milnor applies Lemma 2.2 in an unspecified coordinate system. The formal proof applies the lemma of Morse on the manifold $N$ (Part I) ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · morse_lemmaM⟧ inside the domain of one slice chart $\sigma$ of $N$, and then splits the coordinates. The result is a Morse patch $\psi$ of $f$ at $p$, with values in $\mathbb R^\lambda \times \mathbb R^{n-\lambda}$, where $\lambda$ is the index of $f$ at $p$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · indexM⟧ and $p$ is a non-degenerate critical point in the sense of §0.2 ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsNondegenerateCriticalPointM⟧. The patch is a smooth coordinate system of $N$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord⟧ whose domain lies inside the domain of $\sigma$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttachSubmanifold · exists_isMorsePatch_submanifold⟧. So the whole construction takes place where $N$ is the graph described by $\sigma$.

*$F$ is smooth on $N$* ⟦Chapter2/CellAttachSubmanifold · contMDiff_patchBump⟧. On the domain of $\psi$ the correction is $\mu(\xi + 2\eta) \circ \psi$, smooth because $\psi$ is a smooth coordinate system; on the open complement of the closed ellipsoid it is $0$.

*Assertion 2 on $N$* ⟦Chapter2/CellAttachSubmanifold · isCriticalPointM_patchF_iff⟧. In a smooth coordinate system, the critical points of a function on $N$ are those of the function read in the coordinates ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.isCriticalPointM_iff⟧. Read in $\psi$, Milnor's $F$ and $f$ are the model functions of §II.8 near $\psi(q)$, and the only critical point of either is the origin. This transports Milnor's computation of $dF$ in the coordinates $u^1, \dots, u^n$ (p. 17). Outside the domain of $\psi$, and hence outside the closed ellipsoid, $F = f$ near $q$, and their manifold derivatives agree.

*Extending $F$ to $V$.* Milnor's Theorem 3.1 is intrinsic. The formal Theorem 3.1 on a submanifold is for restrictions of smooth functions on $V$ (§II.5), so Assertion 3 needs Milnor's $F$ on $N$ to be such a restriction, a step Milnor does not need.

**Lemma.** Let $b : N \to \mathbb R$ be smooth and zero outside a compact set $K \subseteq N$ contained in the domain of a slice chart $\sigma$. Then $b$ is the restriction of a smooth function $B$ on $V$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttachSubmanifold · exists_contDiff_comp_val_eq_of_chart⟧.

*Proof.* Let $O$ be the set of points $x$ of the domain of $\sigma$ such that $(\sigma(x)_Z, 0)$ lies in the image of $\sigma$, where $\sigma(x)_Z$ is the $Z$-component; $O$ is open by continuity, and it contains $K$, because for $q \in N$ in the domain of $\sigma$ the point $\sigma(q)$ is itself $(\sigma(q)_Z, 0)$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · SliceChart.mk_zero_mem_target⟧. On $O$ the function $g(x) = b\big(\sigma_N^{-1}(\sigma(x)_Z)\big)$ is defined, where $\sigma_N$ is the chart of $N$ induced by $\sigma$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · SliceChart.toChart⟧. It is smooth: $b$ is smooth, $\sigma_N^{-1}$ is smooth because $\sigma_N$ belongs to the maximal atlas of $N$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · toChart_mem_maximalAtlas⟧, and $x \mapsto \sigma(x)_Z$ is smooth. Lemma B of §II.4 with $g = 1$ gives a smooth cut-off $\chi$ with closed support in $O$ and $\chi = 1$ on $K$. Then $B = \chi\, g$ is smooth on $V$ by the product lemma. On $N$: for $q$ in the domain of $\sigma$, $\sigma_N^{-1}(\sigma(q)_Z) = q$, so $B(q) = \chi(q)\, b(q)$, which is $b(q)$ if $q \in K$ and $0 = b(q)$ if $q \notin K$; for $q$ outside the domain, $q \notin K$ and $\chi(q) = 0 = b(q)$. $\square$

**Theorem 3.2 on a submanifold (V2.8 (ii)).** Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional real normed space, $N \subseteq V$ a slice submanifold modelled on a finite-dimensional $Z$, $F : V \to \mathbb R$ smooth, $f = F|N$, $p \in N$ a non-degenerate critical point of $f$ of index $\lambda$, and $c = f(p)$. Suppose that for some $\epsilon_0 > 0$ the set $f^{-1}[c - \epsilon_0, c + \epsilon_0] \subseteq N$ is compact and contains no critical point of $f$ other than $p$. Then there is $\epsilon_1 > 0$ such that for every $\epsilon \in (0, \epsilon_1]$ there is a map $\chi : D^\lambda \to N$, continuous and injective on $D^\lambda$, meeting $N^{c-\epsilon}$ exactly in $\chi(S^{\lambda-1})$ and contained in $N^{c+\epsilon}$, such that $N^{c-\epsilon} \cup \chi(D^\lambda)$ is a strong deformation retract of $N^{c+\epsilon}$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttachSubmanifold · theorem_3_2_submanifold⟧.

*Proof.*

1. Take the Morse patch $\psi$ inside a slice chart $\sigma$, and let $\epsilon_1 = \min(\epsilon_0, \epsilon_2)$, where $\epsilon_2$ is given by the ball condition for $\psi$. Fix $\epsilon \in (0, \epsilon_1]$ and let $\mu$ be the function of §II.7 for $\epsilon$.
2. The correction of $\psi$ vanishes outside the ellipsoid, which is compact and lies in the domain of $\psi$, hence of $\sigma$. By the lemma it is the restriction of a smooth $B$ on $V$. So $G = F - B$ is smooth on $V$, and $G|N$ is Milnor's $F$ on $N$.
3. By Assertion 2 on $N$, $G|N$ and $f$ have the same critical points.
4. Assertion 3: $(G|N)^{-1}[c - \epsilon, c + \epsilon]$ lies in $f^{-1}[c - \epsilon_0, c + \epsilon_0]$ ($G \ge c - \epsilon$ gives $f \ge c - \epsilon$, and $G \le c + \epsilon$ gives $f \le c + \epsilon$ by Assertion 1), so it is compact; a critical point of $G|N$ in it would be $p$, which lies at the level $c - \mu(0) < c - \epsilon$. Theorem 3.1 on $N$ (§II.5), applied to $G$, makes $(G|N)^{-1}(-\infty, c - \epsilon]$ a strong deformation retract of $(G|N)^{-1}(-\infty, c + \epsilon]$, which is $N^{c+\epsilon}$ by Assertion 1.
5. Theorem 3.2 in a patch, for the Hausdorff space $N$, with $\iota$ the renumbering isometry, which exists because the index is at most the dimension ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · indexM_le_finrank⟧, gives the conclusion. $\square$

With Lemma D in a Hausdorff space (§II.11), $N^{c+\epsilon}$ is homotopy equivalent to $N^{c-\epsilon} \cup_\varphi e^\lambda$ for an injective $\varphi : S^{\lambda-1} \to N^{c-\epsilon}$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttachSubmanifold · theorem_3_2_attach_submanifold⟧. *Narrower than the book:* $f$ is the restriction of a smooth function on $V$, and abstract manifolds are not treated.

### II.14 Remark 3.3: several critical points on one level

Milnor's Remark 3.3 (p. 19) says that if $f^{-1}(c)$ contains $k$ non-degenerate critical points $p_1, \dots, p_k$, of indices $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k$, a similar proof shows that $M^{c+\epsilon}$ has the homotopy type of $M^{c-\epsilon} \cup e^{\lambda_1} \cup \dots \cup e^{\lambda_k}$. The hypotheses of Theorem 3.2 are understood: $f^{-1}[c - \epsilon_0, c + \epsilon_0]$ is compact and the $p_i$ are all its critical points. The exposition proves it by redoing Assertions 1–4 chart by chart for $F = f - \sum_i \mu(\xi_i + 2\eta_i)$. The formal proof redoes nothing: it subtracts the bumps one at a time and applies the single-patch statements of §II.13 at each step.

**Extending a deformation by the identity.** If $A \subseteq B$ is a strong deformation retract of $B$, $B$ and $C$ are closed, and $B \cap C \subseteq A$, then $A \cup C$ is a strong deformation retract of $B \cup C$ ⟦Chapter2/SeveralCritical · IsStrongDeformationRetract.union_of_isClosed⟧. The deformation $H$ of $B$, extended by the identity on $C$, is well defined because the two agree on $B \cap C \subseteq A$, where $H$ fixes every point; it is continuous by the pasting lemma for the closed cover of $[0,1] \times (B \cup C)$ by $[0,1] \times B$ and $[0,1] \times C$; its end map takes values in $A \cup C$; and it fixes $A \cup C$. A set which is the whole space is trivially a strong deformation retract ⟦Chapter2/SeveralCritical · isStrongDeformationRetract_of_eq_univ⟧.

**The functions $F_s$.** Given smooth functions $B_i$ on $V$, the extensions of the corrections of the patches, put $F_s = F - \sum_{i \in s} B_i$ for every finite set $s$ of indices ⟦Chapter2/SeveralCritical · multiF⟧; it is smooth ⟦Chapter2/SeveralCritical · contDiff_multiF⟧.

**Lemma (Assertions 1, 2 and 4 for several patches).** Let $\psi_i$ be Morse patches of $f$ at points $p_i$ with $f(p_i) = c$, smooth coordinate systems of $N$ with pairwise disjoint domains, each image containing $\{\xi + \eta \le 2\epsilon\}$; let $\mu$ have Milnor's properties; and let $B_i$ be smooth on $V$ with $B_i|N$ the correction of $\psi_i$. Then for every finite set $s$: (1) $(F_s|N)^{-1}(-\infty, c + \epsilon] = N^{c+\epsilon}$; (2) $F_s|N$ and $f$ have the same critical points; (3) $F_s(p_i) = c - \mu(0)$ for $i \in s$; (4) the cells $e_i$, $i \in s$, lie in $(F_s|N)^{-1}(-\infty, c - \epsilon]$; (5) $N^{c-\epsilon} \cup \bigcup_{i \in s} e_i$ is a strong deformation retract of $(F_s|N)^{-1}(-\infty, c - \epsilon]$ ⟦Chapter2/SeveralCritical · multi_induction⟧.

*Proof,* by induction on $s$. For $s = \emptyset$, $F_\emptyset = F$, and (5) holds because $N^{c-\epsilon}$ is the whole space $F^{-1}(-\infty, c-\epsilon]$. Suppose the claims hold for $s$, let $i \notin s$, and write $g = F_s|N$.

- Every $B_j$, $j \in s$, vanishes on the domain of $\psi_i$, by disjointness; so $g = f$ there. Hence $\psi_i$ is a Morse patch of $g$ at $p_i$ with $g(p_i) = c$, and $F_{s \cup \{i\}}|N$ is Milnor's modification of $g$ in the patch $\psi_i$.
- (1) is Assertion 1 for $g$ and $\psi_i$, followed by (1) for $s$. (2) is Assertion 2 on $N$ for $g$ and $\psi_i$, followed by (2) for $s$. (3): at $p_i$ the new value is $g(p_i) - \mu(0) = c - \mu(0)$; a point $p_j$ with $j \in s$ lies outside the domain of $\psi_i$, where the new correction vanishes, so (3) for $s$ applies. (4): the new cell lies below $c - \epsilon$ for the modification of $g$; the old cells lie below $c - \epsilon$ for $g$, hence for the smaller function $F_{s \cup \{i\}}$.
- (5) in two steps. First, extend the deformation of claim (5) for $s$ by the identity over the new cell $e_i$. This is allowed: $g^{-1}(-\infty, c - \epsilon]$ is closed, $e_i$ is compact, hence closed, and a point of $e_i$ with $g \le c - \epsilon$ lies in $N^{c-\epsilon}$, because $g = f$ on the domain of $\psi_i$. So $N^{c-\epsilon} \cup \bigcup_{j \in s} e_j \cup e_i$ is a strong deformation retract of $g^{-1}(-\infty, c - \epsilon] \cup e_i$. Second, Assertion 4 in the patch $\psi_i$ for the function $g$: $g^{-1}(-\infty, c - \epsilon] \cup e_i$ is a strong deformation retract of $(F_{s \cup \{i\}}|N)^{-1}(-\infty, c - \epsilon]$. Lemma A(i) composes the two. $\square$

**Remark 3.3 (V2.9).** Let $N \subseteq V$, $F$ and $f = F|N$ be as in §II.13, and $S$ a finite set of non-degenerate critical points of $f$, all at the level $c$. Suppose that for some $\epsilon_0 > 0$ the set $f^{-1}[c - \epsilon_0, c + \epsilon_0]$ is compact and all its critical points lie in $S$. Then there is $\epsilon_1 > 0$ such that for every $\epsilon \in (0, \epsilon_1]$ there are maps $\chi_p : D^{\lambda_p} \to N$, $p \in S$, with $\lambda_p$ the index of $p$, each continuous and injective on the ball, with $\chi_p(z) \in N^{c-\epsilon}$ exactly when $|z| = 1$ and $\chi_p(D^{\lambda_p}) \subseteq N^{c+\epsilon}$, the cells pairwise disjoint, such that $N^{c-\epsilon} \cup \bigcup_{p} \chi_p(D^{\lambda_p})$ is a strong deformation retract of $N^{c+\epsilon}$ ⟦Chapter2/SeveralCritical · remark_3_3_submanifold⟧.

*Proof.*

1. *Disjoint Morse charts.* $N$ is Hausdorff, so the finite set $S$ has pairwise disjoint open neighbourhoods $U_p$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Separation/Hausdorff · Set.Finite.t2_separation⟧. Morse patches can be taken inside given open neighbourhoods ⟦Chapter2/SeveralCritical · exists_isMorsePatch_submanifold_subset⟧, so patches $\psi_p$ with domains inside the $U_p$ have pairwise disjoint domains. Milnor's "similar proof" needs these disjoint charts, hence Hausdorffness.
2. *One $\epsilon$ for all patches:* $\epsilon_1$ is the minimum of $\epsilon_0$ and the radii given by the ball condition for the finitely many patches.
3. Extend each correction to a smooth $B_p$ on $V$ (the lemma of §II.13), apply the multi-patch lemma with $s = S$, and let $G = F_S$, smooth on $V$ with $G \le F$ on $N$.
4. *Assertion 3:* $(G|N)^{-1}[c - \epsilon, c + \epsilon]$ lies in $f^{-1}[c - \epsilon_0, c + \epsilon_0]$, so it is compact; a critical point of $G|N$ in it is a critical point of $f$ by claim (2), hence a point of $S$, but there $G = c - \mu(0) < c - \epsilon$ by claim (3). Theorem 3.1 on $N$ applied to $G$, with claim (1), makes $(G|N)^{-1}(-\infty, c - \epsilon]$ a strong deformation retract of $N^{c+\epsilon}$.
5. The cells are parametrized as before ⟦Chapter2/SeveralCritical · patchCell_props⟧; they are pairwise disjoint because each lies in the domain of its own patch. Lemma A(i) composes step 4 with claim (5). $\square$

*Narrower than the book:* only on slice submanifolds. That the union is $N^{c-\epsilon}$ with the cells attached one at a time is part of the proof of Theorem 3.5 (§II.16).

### II.15 Lemmas 3.6 and 3.7: attaching along homotopic maps, and to equivalent spaces

**The attaching space.** For a space $X$ and a continuous $\varphi : S^{n-1} \to X$, the space $X \cup_\varphi e^n$ is the pushout of $X \leftarrow S^{n-1} \rightarrow D^n$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · AttachCell⟧ (§0.5), with the inclusion of $X$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · cellInl⟧ and the characteristic map of the cell ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · cellInr⟧, which agree on the sphere: the point $u \in S^{n-1}$ of the cell is the point $\varphi(u)$ of $X$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · cellInl_apply_eq⟧.

**Maps and homotopies out of $X \cup_\varphi e^n$.** Milnor uses two facts silently. (Q1) A map given on $X$ and on the cell, compatibly on $S^{n-1}$, is a continuous map out of $X \cup_\varphi e^n$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · cellDesc⟧; this is the universal property of the pushout, and two maps agreeing on $X$ and on the cell are equal ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · attachCell_hom_ext⟧. (Q2) A *homotopy* $[0,1] \times (X \cup_\varphi e^n) \to Z$ given on $[0,1] \times X$ and on $[0,1] \times D^n$, compatibly, is continuous too ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · cellHomotopy⟧. This is not the universal property: it needs the product of $[0,1]$ with a quotient map to be a quotient map, which holds because $[0,1]$ is locally compact (a lemma of J. H. C. Whitehead). The formal proof uses the exponential law instead: because $[0,1]$ is locally compact, a continuous map $[0,1] \times P \to Z$ is the same as a continuous map from $P$ to the space $C([0,1], Z)$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/CompactOpen · ContinuousMap.curry⟧. The two pieces of the homotopy become maps $X \to C([0,1], Z)$ and $D^n \to C([0,1], Z)$, compatible on $S^{n-1}$; they glue by (Q1), and uncurrying gives the homotopy. Its values at times $0$ and $1$ are checked on $X$ and on the cell.

**Milnor's formulas on the cell.** Write a point of $D^n$ as $z = tu$, with $t = |z|$ and $u = z/|z|$. All Milnor's maps and homotopies have the same shape on the cell: for a radius $a \in (0, 1]$ and a map $\Psi$ with values in $X$ such that $\Psi(a, u) = \varphi(u)$,
$$tu \ \mapsto\ \text{the point } (t/a)\,u \text{ of the cell, for } t \le a; \qquad tu \ \mapsto\ \Psi(t, u) \in X, \text{ for } t \ge a,$$
with $a$ and $\Psi$ possibly depending on a parameter ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · radial⟧. The formal definition uses the first formula for $t \le a$ and the second for $t > a$; at $t = a$ the two agree, because $\Psi(a, u) = \varphi(u)$ is the boundary point $u$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · radial_of_le⟧ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · radial_of_ge⟧. On the boundary sphere, when $a \le 1$, the formula is $\Psi(1, u)$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · radial_inclusion⟧. It is jointly continuous in the parameter and the point when $a$ and $\Psi$ are ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · continuous_radial⟧: each piece is continuous on its closed set, $\{t \le a\}$ or $\{t \ge a\}$, where $u = z/|z|$ is continuous because $t \ge a > 0$, and they agree on $t = a$, so the pasting lemma applies ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · continuous_dite_le⟧. Two instances with the same $a$ and $\Psi$ agree ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · radial_congr⟧, and with $a = 1$ the formula is the identity of the cell ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · radial_of_eq_one⟧. The case $n = 0$, where $S^{-1}$ is empty, needs no separate treatment. Homotopy parameters given by formulas are clamped to $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · clampI⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Order/Interval/Set/ProjIcc · Set.projIcc⟧; in every use the formula already lies in $[0,1]$.

**Lemma 3.6 (V2.13).** If $\varphi_0, \varphi_1 : S^{n-1} \to X$ are homotopic, there is a homotopy equivalence $k : X \cup_{\varphi_0} e^n \to X \cup_{\varphi_1} e^n$ which is the identity on $X$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · lemma_3_6⟧.

*Proof.* Let $\Phi$ be a homotopy from $\varphi_0$ to $\varphi_1$, and $\varphi_s = \Phi(s, \cdot)$.

1. *Milnor's $k$* (p. 21) is the identity on $X$ and, on the cell, $k(tu) = 2tu$ for $t \le \frac12$ and $k(tu) = \varphi_{2-2t}(u)$ for $t \ge \frac12$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · lemma36Map⟧: the formula above with $a = \frac12$ and $\Psi(t, u) = \varphi_{2-2t}(u)$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · kOuter⟧ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · kCell⟧. At $t = \frac12$, $\Psi = \varphi_1(u)$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · kOuter_half⟧, which is identified with $u$ in the target. At $t = 1$, $\Psi = \varphi_0(u)$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · kOuter_one⟧, as the identification $u \sim \varphi_0(u)$ in the source requires. So (Q1) applies, and $k$ is the identity on $X$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · lemma36Map_inl⟧.
2. *Milnor's $\ell$*, "defined by similar formulas", is formally $k$ for the reversed homotopy, from $\varphi_1$ to $\varphi_0$. Then $\ell k$ is the identity on $X$, and on the cell ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · lemma36_zero⟧
$$\ell k(tu) = 4tu \ (t \le \tfrac14), \qquad \varphi_{4t-1}(u) \ (\tfrac14 \le t \le \tfrac12), \qquad \varphi_{2-2t}(u) \ (t \ge \tfrac12).$$
Along each radius, the outer three quarters run out along the homotopy to $\varphi_1$ and back.
3. *$\ell k$ is homotopic to the identity*, which Milnor calls not difficult to verify. The homotopy $H_\tau$, $\tau \in [0, 1]$, is the identity on $X$ and, on the cell, the radial formula with $a = (1 + 3\tau)/4$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · aH⟧ and ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · hOuter⟧
$$\Psi(t, u) = \varphi_{\min(4(t - a),\ 2 - 2t)}(u).$$
The two terms of the minimum cross at $t = (1+\tau)/2$, where both equal $1 - \tau$: so along each radius the subscript rises from $0$ to $1 - \tau$ and returns to $0$, and the excursion shrinks as $\tau$ grows. The checks: $\Psi(a, u) = \varphi_0(u)$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · hOuter_aH⟧, because $\min(0, 2 - 2a) = 0$ for $a \le 1$; on the boundary sphere $\Psi(1, u) = \varphi_0(u)$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · hOuter_one⟧, which is the identity of $X$ at $\varphi_0(u)$ under $u \sim \varphi_0(u)$; and $\Psi$ is continuous ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · continuous_hOuter⟧. So (Q2) applies. At $\tau = 0$, $a = \frac14$ and the formula is $\ell k$ (step 2); at $\tau = 1$, $a = 1$ and it is the identity. So $H$ is a homotopy from $\ell k$ to the identity ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · lemma36Homotopy⟧. The exposition writes $H_\tau$ in three pieces, with a break at $(1+\tau)/2$; the minimum is the same function.
4. $k\ell$ is homotopic to the identity by step 3 for the reversed homotopy, whose reverse is $\Phi$. So $k$ is a homotopy equivalence with homotopy inverse $\ell$. $\square$

**Lemma 3.7 (V2.14).** Let $\varphi : S^{n-1} \to X$ be continuous and $f : X \to Y$ a homotopy equivalence. The map $F : X \cup_\varphi e^n \to Y \cup_{f\varphi} e^n$ which is $f$ on $X$ and the identity on the cell is a homotopy equivalence ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · lemma_3_7⟧.

*Proof* (Milnor's, after Hilton).

1. $F$ is well defined: the point $u \sim \varphi(u)$ goes to $u \sim f\varphi(u)$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · attachMap⟧ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · attachMap_inl⟧ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · attachMap_inr⟧. Let $g$ be a homotopy inverse of $f$, $h_t$ a homotopy from $gf$ to the identity of $X$, and $G : Y \cup_{f\varphi} e^n \to X \cup_{gf\varphi} e^n$ the analogous map, $g$ on $Y$ and the identity on the cell.
2. $h_t \circ \varphi$ is a homotopy from $gf\varphi$ to $\varphi$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · homotopyAttach⟧, so Lemma 3.6 provides $k : X \cup_{gf\varphi} e^n \to X \cup_\varphi e^n$. Lemma 3.7 needs the formula for $k$, not only the statement of Lemma 3.6: Milnor's next step uses the specific definitions of $k$, $G$ and $F$ (p. 22).
3. *$kGF$ is homotopic to the identity* ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · lemma37Homotopy⟧. Milnor's homotopy $q_\tau$ is $h_\tau$ on $X$ and, on the cell, the radial formula with $a = (1 + \tau)/2$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · aQ⟧ and $\Psi(t, u) = h_{2 - 2t + \tau}(\varphi(u))$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · qOuter⟧, that is, $tu \mapsto \frac{2}{1+\tau}\,tu$ for $t \le \frac{1+\tau}2$ and $tu \mapsto h_{2-2t+\tau}(\varphi(u))$ for $t \ge \frac{1+\tau}2$. The checks: $\Psi(a, u) = h_1(\varphi(u)) = \varphi(u)$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · qOuter_aQ⟧; on the sphere $\Psi(1, u) = h_\tau(\varphi(u))$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · qOuter_one⟧, which is $q_\tau$ on $X$ at $\varphi(u)$; the subscript $2 - 2t + \tau$ lies in $[\tau, 1]$. At $\tau = 0$: on $X$, $h_0 = gf$, which is $kGF$ on $X$; on the cell, $a = \frac12$ and $\Psi(t, u) = h_{2-2t}(\varphi(u))$, which is $k$'s formula for the homotopy $h_t\varphi$, and $kGF = k$ on the cell. At $\tau = 1$: $a = 1$, the identity on the cell, and $h_1$ is the identity on $X$.
4. *Milnor's Assertion* (p. 22): if $LF \simeq \mathrm{id}$ and $FR \simeq \mathrm{id}$, then $RF \simeq \mathrm{id}$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · homotopic_of_leftInv_rightInv⟧. Indeed $L \simeq L(FR) = (LF)R \simeq R$, so $RF \simeq LF \simeq \mathrm{id}$, homotopy being compatible with composition on either side.
5. *$G$ has a left homotopy inverse*, by "a similar proof": step 3 for $(Y, f\varphi, g, f)$ in place of $(X, \varphi, f, g)$, with a homotopy from $fg$ to the identity of $Y$, gives maps $G'$ and $k'$ with $k'G'G \simeq \mathrm{id}$.
6. Milnor's three steps. $k$ has the left homotopy inverse $\ell$ (Lemma 3.6) and the right homotopy inverse $GF$ (step 3), so $(GF)k \simeq \mathrm{id}$ by step 4. Then $G$ has the left homotopy inverse $k'G'$ and the right homotopy inverse $Fk$, so $(Fk)G \simeq \mathrm{id}$. Finally $(kG)F \simeq \mathrm{id}$ by step 3 and $F(kG) = (Fk)G \simeq \mathrm{id}$: $F$ is a homotopy equivalence with homotopy inverse $kG$. $\square$

### II.16 Theorem 3.5: one cell for each critical point

**Iterated adjunction spaces (V2.11).** A space $K$ is an *iterated adjunction space with cells* $(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k)$ if it is obtained from the empty space by attaching a $\lambda_1$-cell, then a $\lambda_2$-cell, and so on, each along an arbitrary continuous map of its boundary sphere into the space built so far ⟦Chapter2/CWType · IsCellAttachment⟧. A space $X$ *has the homotopy type of a finite cell complex with cells* $(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k)$ if it is homotopy equivalent to such a $K$ ⟦Chapter2/CWType · IsFiniteCellComplexType⟧ (§0.5). The empty space has this property with no cells ⟦Chapter2/CWType · IsFiniteCellComplexType.of_isEmpty⟧, the property is invariant under homotopy equivalence ⟦Chapter2/CWType · IsFiniteCellComplexType.of_homotopyEquiv⟧, and it survives attaching a cell: if $X \simeq K$ by a homotopy equivalence $h$, then $X \cup_\varphi e^n \simeq K \cup_{h\varphi} e^n$ by Lemma 3.7 ⟦Chapter2/CWType · IsFiniteCellComplexType.attach⟧. This is Milnor's appeal to Lemmas 3.6 and 3.7 on p. 23; only Lemma 3.7 is needed, because the attaching maps are not made cellular.

**Why this is not literally a CW complex.** Milnor's statement is about a CW complex in J. H. C. Whitehead's sense. The formal statement differs in two ways. First, the attaching maps are arbitrary continuous maps, not cellular ones. Milnor makes them cellular with the cellular approximation theorem: the attaching map of a $\lambda$-cell is homotopic to a map into the $(\lambda - 1)$-skeleton, and Lemma 3.6 then replaces it. Mathlib has no cellular approximation theorem. Second, the cells are attached in order of increasing critical value, not of dimension. An iterated adjunction space whose cells come in non-decreasing dimension, each attached along a map into the union of the cells of smaller dimension, is a finite CW complex. Without that condition it is still homotopy equivalent to a CW complex with the same number of cells in each dimension, by cellular approximation and Lemmas 3.6 and 3.7, but it need not itself carry a CW structure with these cells: a $1$-cell attached after a $2$-cell may have its ends inside the $2$-cell. So Theorem 3.5 is verified with "CW-complex" replaced by "iterated adjunction space with exactly $C_\lambda$ cells of dimension $\lambda$"; the passage to a CW complex is not formalized.

**A minimum is a critical point.** If $f(p) \le f(x)$ for every $x$, then $p$ is a critical point ⟦Chapter2/CWType · isCriticalPointM_of_forall_le⟧. The function read in the preferred chart at $p$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · localRep⟧ takes the value $f(p)$ at the image of $p$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · localRep_self⟧ and is nowhere smaller, so its derivative vanishes there ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/LocalExtr/Basic · IsLocalMin.fderiv_eq_zero⟧, which is criticality ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · isCriticalPointM_iff_fderiv⟧. Milnor's remark that $M^a$ is vacuous below the first critical value (p. 23) rests on this.

**Setting.** $N \subseteq V$ is a *compact* slice submanifold modelled on a finite-dimensional $Z$, $F : V \to \mathbb R$ is smooth, $f = F|N$, and every critical point of $f$ is non-degenerate. Then $f$ is a Morse function (§0.2) ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsMorseFunction⟧, and as $N$ is compact it has finitely many critical points ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · IsMorseFunction.finite_setOf_isCriticalPointM⟧ (Part I).

**Passing one critical level.** Let $p$ be a non-degenerate critical point, $c = f(p)$, and $a' < c < a$ such that every other critical point has its value below $a'$ or above $a$. If $N^{a'}$ has the homotopy type of a cell complex with cells $(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_j)$, then $N^a$ has that of one with cells $(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_j, \lambda)$, $\lambda$ the index of $p$ ⟦Chapter2/CWType · IsFiniteCellComplexType.sublevel_step⟧.

*Proof.* Put $\epsilon_0 = \min(c - a', a - c)$. Then $f^{-1}[c - \epsilon_0, c + \epsilon_0]$ is compact, being closed in the compact $N$, and contains no critical point other than $p$. Theorem 3.2 with an attaching space on $N$ (§II.13) gives $\epsilon_1$; take $\epsilon = \min(\epsilon_1, \epsilon_0/2)$, so that $a' < c - \epsilon$ and $c + \epsilon < a$, and an attaching map $\varphi$ with $N^{c+\epsilon} \simeq N^{c-\epsilon} \cup_\varphi e^\lambda$. No critical value lies in $[a', c - \epsilon]$ or in $[c + \epsilon, a]$, so Theorem 3.1 for compact $N$ (§II.5) makes the inclusions $N^{a'} \to N^{c-\epsilon}$ and $N^{c+\epsilon} \to N^a$ homotopy equivalences. Hence $N^{c-\epsilon}$ has the homotopy type of the complex with cells $(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_j)$, $N^{c-\epsilon} \cup_\varphi e^\lambda$ that of the complex with one more cell (attaching), and $N^a \simeq N^{c+\epsilon} \simeq N^{c-\epsilon} \cup_\varphi e^\lambda$. $\square$

**Regular sublevel sets, distinct critical values.** Suppose moreover that distinct critical points have distinct values, and let $a$ be a regular value. Then the critical points with value below $a$ can be listed without repetition as $p_1, \dots, p_j$ with $f(p_1) < \dots < f(p_j)$, so that $N^a$ has the homotopy type of a cell complex with cells $(\lambda(p_1), \dots, \lambda(p_j))$ ⟦Chapter2/CWType · isFiniteCellComplexType_sublevel⟧.

*Proof,* by strong induction on the number of critical points below $a$. If there is none, $N^a$ is empty: otherwise the minimum of $f$ on the compact $N$, attained at a critical point, would be at most $a$, and not equal to $a$ as $a$ is regular, so below $a$. The empty space has the homotopy type of the complex without cells. Otherwise, let $p$ be the critical point with the largest value below $a$, and choose a regular value $a' < f(p)$ above every smaller critical value: the midpoint between $f(p)$ and the largest critical value below it, or $f(p) - 1$ if there is none. Every critical point other than $p$ then lies below $a'$ or above $a$ (values are distinct, and none lies in $(f(p), a]$ by the maximality of $p$ and the regularity of $a$). There is one critical point fewer below $a'$; the induction hypothesis applies to $N^{a'}$, and passing one critical level adds the cell of $p$ at the end of the list. $\square$

**Theorem 3.5 (V2.12).** Let $N \subseteq V$ be a compact slice submanifold, $F : V \to \mathbb R$ smooth, and suppose that all critical points of $f = F|N$ are non-degenerate.

- If distinct critical points have distinct values, the critical points can be listed without repetition as $p_1, \dots, p_k$ with $f(p_1) < \dots < f(p_k)$, so that $N$ has the homotopy type of the iterated adjunction space with cells $(\lambda(p_1), \dots, \lambda(p_k))$ ⟦Chapter2/CWType · theorem_3_5⟧; in particular $N$ has the homotopy type of one in which, for every $\lambda$, the number of cells of dimension $\lambda$ is the number $C_\lambda$ of critical points of index $\lambda$ ⟦Chapter2/CWType · theorem_3_5_count⟧.
- In general, the critical points can be listed without repetition with $f(p_1) \le \dots \le f(p_k)$, with the same conclusion ⟦Chapter2/CWType · theorem_3_5'⟧, and in particular with exactly $C_\lambda$ cells of dimension $\lambda$ ⟦Chapter2/CWType · theorem_3_5_count'⟧.

*Proof of the first case.* $f$ is bounded on the compact $N$, say by $M_0$; the value $a = M_0 + 1$ is regular and $N^a = N$, so the previous result applies, and $N$ is homeomorphic to $N^a$. For the count, the list of critical points has no repetition and contains each critical point exactly once, so the number of entries $\lambda$ in the list of indices is the number of critical points of index $\lambda$. $\square$

*The general case* replaces Theorem 3.2 by Remark 3.3. Two further steps are needed.

1. *Attaching disjoint cells one at a time* ⟦Chapter2/CWType · isFiniteCellComplexType_union_cells⟧. Let $A$ be a closed subset of a Hausdorff space, and $\chi_i$, for $i$ in a list, maps from closed unit balls, continuous and injective, with pairwise disjoint images, each meeting $A$ exactly in the image of the unit sphere. If $A$ has the homotopy type of a complex with cells $(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_j)$, then $A \cup \bigcup_i \chi_i(D)$ has that of one with the dimensions of the $\chi_i$ appended. The proof is by induction on the list. Lemma D (§II.11) makes $A \cup \chi_{i_1}(D)$ the space $A$ with a cell attached, so it has the homotopy type of the complex with one more cell; it is closed, the image of a compact ball being closed; and, because the cells are disjoint, each later $\chi_i$ still meets $A \cup \chi_{i_1}(D)$ exactly in the image of its sphere. The induction hypothesis applies to it.
2. *Passing a critical level with several critical points* ⟦Chapter2/CWType · IsFiniteCellComplexType.sublevel_step_finset⟧. If $S$ is the set of critical points with value $c$ and every other critical point lies below $a'$ or above $a$, then, for some enumeration of $S$, $N^a$ has the homotopy type of the complex with the indices of $S$ appended. The proof is that of the single-point step, with Remark 3.3 (§II.14) in place of Theorem 3.2 and step 1 in place of attaching one cell.

The induction over regular values then runs as before, now removing all the critical points of the highest critical value below $a$ at once ⟦Chapter2/CWType · isFiniteCellComplexType_sublevel'⟧, and $a$ above the maximum of $f$ gives the theorem. $\square$

**The Remark on p. 24 (V2.15).** Milnor remarks at the end of §3 that each $M^a$ has the homotopy type of a finite complex with one cell of dimension $\lambda$ for each critical point of index $\lambda$ in $M^a$, even when $a$ is a critical value. For $a$ not a critical value this is the induction itself ⟦Chapter2/CWType · isFiniteCellComplexType_sublevel'⟧, with the critical points below $a$ listed by non-decreasing value. At a critical value it would need Remark 3.4 on submanifolds, which is not formalized.

*What Milnor's proof uses that the formal one supplies.* The emptiness of $M^a$ below the first critical value needs the minimum to be a critical point. The induction runs over regular values with exactly one critical value between consecutive ones, which needs the finiteness of the set of critical points. The $\epsilon$ of Theorem 3.2 must also be smaller than the distances to the neighbouring regular values. *Narrower than the book:* $N$ is compact and $f$ extends to $V$. The non-compact case, with infinitely many critical points, needs Whitehead's theorem and the fact that a manifold has the homotopy type of a CW complex, neither of which Mathlib has; it is not formalized, and neither is the passage from an iterated adjunction space to a CW complex.

### II.17 What the formalization taught us

- **One crossing.** Milnor's "clearly" in Theorem 3.1 rests on the fact that a trajectory crosses the slab $f^{-1}[a,b]$ only once, upwards and at unit speed. It is a continuity argument about one real function (§II.2), and once isolated it yields both inclusions of $\varphi_{b-a}(M^a) = M^b$ and the properties of $r_t$ for any slab flow (§II.3).
- **Two auxiliary functions exist.** The cut-off $\rho$ of Theorem 3.1 comes from a smooth Urysohn function on a compact neighbourhood (§II.4). Milnor's $\mu$ of Theorem 3.2 needs a slope bound everywhere, and the obvious candidate violates it; an integrated plateau satisfies it (§II.7).
- **Hausdorffness, used three times.** The ellipsoid $\xi + 2\eta \le 2\epsilon$ must be closed for $F$ to be smooth and for "the identity outside $U$" to be continuous (§II.10, §II.13); Lemma D needs the cell to be closed (§II.11); Remark 3.3 needs disjoint Morse charts (§II.14).
- **The deformation of Assertion 4 is one formula**, continuous on the whole coordinate space; it never leaves the chart on $F^{-1}(-\infty, c - \epsilon]$, and the point $\xi = \epsilon$, $\eta = 0$, where Milnor's Case 2 reads $0/0$, is fixed (§II.9).
- **Unstated lemmas.** The composition of strong deformation retractions and its converse with a retraction (Lemma A, §II.1); the identification of $M^{c-\epsilon} \cup e^\lambda$ with an attaching space (Lemma D, §II.11); the fact that $[0,1]$ times a quotient map is a quotient map, needed for homotopies on attaching spaces (§II.15); and the fact that a global minimum is a critical point (§II.16).
- **Lemma 3.7 uses the proof of Lemma 3.6**, namely the formula for $k$, not only its statement (§II.15).
- **Small corrections.** The bound in Assertion 2 is $\partial F/\partial\eta \ge 1$, not $> 1$ (§II.8); the scan's "$k$-cell" in Theorem 3.2 is a $\lambda$-cell (§II.10).
- **On submanifolds** the intrinsic flow is replaced by an ambient flow tangent to $N$, whose invariance needs a closed set larger than $N$ (§II.5), and Assertion 3 needs Milnor's $F$ to be extended to the ambient space (§II.13).
- **What is not done.** Theorem 3.1 on non-compact abstract manifolds (§II.5); Theorems 3.2 and 3.5 and Remarks 3.3 and 3.4 on abstract manifolds; Remark 3.4 on submanifolds, and with it the Remark of p. 24 at a critical value; Theorem 3.5 for non-compact manifolds; the CW structure, which needs the cellular approximation theorem (§II.16); and the remark on Smale's handles (p. 17), that $M^{c-\epsilon} \cup H$ is diffeomorphic to $M^{c+\epsilon}$, which is Theorem 3.1 for $F$ with its diffeomorphism kept, a step the formal proof of Assertion 3 discards.
