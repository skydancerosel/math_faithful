## Part V. Lefschetz, connections and curvature (Milnor §§7–9)

Milnor's §7 applies the Morse theory of the distance function $L_p = \|\cdot - p\|^2$ to complex submanifolds of $\mathbb C^n$: a closed complex submanifold of complex dimension $k$ has the homotopy type of a CW-complex of dimension at most $k$ (Theorem 7.2), a non-singular affine variety of real dimension $2k$ has no homology above dimension $k$ (Theorem 7.1), and Lefschetz's theorems on hyperplane sections follow (Corollary 7.3, Theorem 7.4). The only new mathematics in the section is algebraic: at a critical point of $L_p$ on a complex submanifold of complex dimension $k$, the Hessian has index at most $k$. The formal proof establishes that algebraic core in a form stronger than Milnor's: at every point of a holomorphic chart, critical or not, degenerate or not. It does so by the identity of Andreotti and Frankel, not by counting focal points. Milnor's Assertions 1 and 2 are proved beside it, read as statements about quadratic forms and about Hessians in a holomorphic chart. **The topological results of §7, Theorems 7.1, 7.2 and 7.4 and Corollary 7.3, are not formalized.** Complex submanifolds are not formalized as manifolds, Theorem 3.5 is available only for compact manifolds and produces adjunction spaces rather than CW-complexes, and cellular homology, relative homology, Lefschetz duality, relative homotopy groups and projective varieties are all missing; §V.6 lists what each statement would need.

Milnor's §§8–9 are a rapid course on connections on an abstract Riemannian manifold. Mathlib has covariant derivatives on vector bundles, their torsion and the notion of a metric connection. It has no curvature tensor, no Christoffel symbols, no covariant derivative along a curve, no parallel transport and no Levi-Civita connection. The formal proof therefore works on three levels. *At a point* (§§V.7–V.8), any trilinear map on an inner product space that satisfies (1)–(3) of Lemma 9.3 also satisfies (4), by Milnor's octahedron, and the theory of sectional curvature is developed for it: independence of the basis of the plane, polarization, determination of $R$, constant curvature. *On an abstract manifold* (§V.9), Milnor's notions are matched with Mathlib's: his rules for a connection, symmetric connections as torsion-free ones, compatible connections as metric ones, the uniqueness half of Lemma 8.6, and the sign of his curvature tensor. *On a submanifold $N$ of a Euclidean space with the induced metric* (§§V.10–V.21), the whole substance of §§8–9 is proved. There the Levi-Civita connection is $X \vdash Y = P(dY(X))$, with $P_q$ the orthogonal projection onto $T_qN$, and $q \mapsto P_q$ is smooth by the explicit formula $A(A^{\mathsf T}A)^{-1}A^{\mathsf T}$. On that basis the formalization proves the second fundamental form and its symmetry, Lemma 8.6 with both halves, Lemmas 8.1–8.3, parallel transport as a linear isometry, Lemmas 8.7 and 9.2, the formula $R(X, Y)Z = \mathrm{II}(Y, \mathrm{II}(X, Z)) - \mathrm{II}(X, \mathrm{II}(Y, Z))$, the Gauss equation, Lemmas 9.1 and 9.3, and the constant curvature $1$ of the round sphere. Every Riemannian manifold is isometric to such an $N$ by Nash's embedding theorem, which the Lean files quote but neither prove nor use. The embedded results are therefore statements about embedded manifolds with the induced metric. The bridge between the second and the third level, the induced connection as a Mathlib covariant derivative on the tangent bundle of $N$, is not built.

The embedded route also changes the proofs. Milnor computes in local coordinates throughout, and several of his proofs are a computation in coordinates left to the reader. The formal proofs use no coordinates. Everything is reduced to the calculus of one smooth operator field $q \mapsto P_q$ and of its derivative $D_v$, whose values $D_vw$ are the second fundamental form. Several steps that the book treats as evident turn out to need an argument, and the sections below point them out. The symmetry of the induced connection is the symmetry of the second fundamental form, a genuine second-order property of the embedding. Milnor's remark that the linear equations of a parallel field have solutions for all $t$ needs a different equation, whose coefficient is skew-adjoint. The tensor property of $R$ in its third argument is a different computation from the one in the first.

### V.1 Quadratic forms on a complex vector space

The setting of §7, as formalized, is linear algebra. $V$ is a complex normed space: Milnor's $\mathbb C^k$, the domain of a holomorphic chart, finite-dimensional wherever dimensions are counted. $V$ is also regarded as a real vector space, by restriction of scalars. $F$ is a complex inner product space (Milnor's $\mathbb C^n$), and $w : V \to F$ is a map that is complex $C^2$ at a point $q$, hence near it: Milnor's inclusion $M \to \mathbb C^n$, read in holomorphic coordinates. "Bilinear form" means a continuous real-bilinear form $H : V \times V \to \mathbb R$.

The notions of §0.2 are used unchanged. The Hessian of $f : E \to \mathbb R$ at $q$ is the second Fréchet derivative, $d^2f_q(v, w) = d(x \mapsto df_x(w))_q(v)$, defined at every point ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · hessian⟧. The null space of a bilinear form $H$ is $\{v : H(v, w) = 0 \text{ for all } w\}$ and its nullity is the dimension of the null space ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · nullSpace⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · nullity⟧. The index is the negative index of inertia of $v \mapsto H(v, v)$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · index⟧ ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/QuadraticForm/Signature · sigNeg⟧. On a finite-dimensional space it is the largest dimension of a subspace on which $H$ is negative definite ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · index_isGreatest⟧, and such a subspace of dimension $\operatorname{index} H$ exists ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · exists_negDef_finrank_eq_index⟧.

Two facts about the nullity are proved in the file itself. *The nullity does not change under a linear change of variables*: if $L$ is a linear isomorphism, the form $H(L\cdot, L\cdot)$ has the same nullity as $H$ ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · nullity_bilinearComp_equiv⟧. Indeed $v$ is in the null space of $H(L\cdot, L\cdot)$ iff $H(Lv, Lw) = 0$ for all $w$, iff $Lv$ is in the null space of $H$, because $Lw$ runs through all vectors as $w$ does. So the null space of the new form is $L^{-1}$ of the old one, and $L^{-1}$ preserves dimension. *The nullity of $-H$ is that of $H$*, the two null spaces being equal ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · nullity_neg⟧.

A *complex structure* on a real normed space $E$ is a continuous real-linear map $J$ with $J(Jv) = -v$. It is an isomorphism, with inverse $-J$ ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · complexStructureEquiv⟧. On a complex vector space the complex structure is multiplication by $i$, regarded as a real-linear map ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · realMulI⟧, with $i(iv) = -v$ ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · realMulI_realMulI⟧ and injective ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · realMulI_injective⟧. A bilinear form $G$ is *$J$-invariant* if $G(Jv, Jw) = G(v, w)$, and $L$ is *$J$-anti-invariant* if $L(Jv, Jw) = -L(v, w)$.

The two prototypes come from Milnor's p. 39. For a continuous complex-bilinear $B : V \times V \to \mathbb C$, the real part $\operatorname{Re} B$ is a real bilinear form ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · reBilin⟧, with $(\operatorname{Re}B)(v, w) = \operatorname{Re}(B(v, w))$ ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · reBilin_apply⟧. If $Q(z) = \sum b_{hj}z^hz^j$ with $b_{hj} = b_{jh}$ and $B(z, z') = \sum b_{hj}z^hz'^j$, then Milnor's real form $Q'(x, y)$, obtained by substituting $z = x + iy$ and taking real parts, is $\operatorname{Re}B(z, z)$. Milnor's identity $Q(iz) = -Q(z)$ becomes, polarized, $\operatorname{Re}B(iv, iw) = -\operatorname{Re}B(v, w)$, because $B(iv, iw) = i^2B(v, w)$ ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · reBilin_I_smul⟧. So $\operatorname{Re}B$ is $J$-anti-invariant for $J = i\cdot$. When $V$ is a complex inner product space it carries the real inner product $\operatorname{Re}\langle v, w\rangle$ ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · reInner⟧ ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · reInner_apply⟧. This is the Euclidean inner product of $\mathbb R^{2k}$ when $V = \mathbb C^k$: it satisfies $\operatorname{Re}\langle v, v\rangle = \|v\|^2$ ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · reInner_self⟧, it is symmetric ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · reInner_comm⟧, and it is homogeneous for real scalars ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · re_inner_real_smul_left⟧. Mathlib's Hermitian product is conjugate-linear in the first slot, Milnor's $\sum_\alpha a_\alpha\bar b_\alpha$ in the second. Their real parts agree, and only real parts enter the proofs. Multiplication by $i$ is orthogonal for the real inner product, since $\langle iv, iw\rangle = \bar\imath\,i\,\langle v, w\rangle = \langle v, w\rangle$ ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · reInner_I_smul⟧. So $\operatorname{Re}\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ is $J$-invariant.

### V.2 Assertion 1

Milnor's Assertion 1 (p. 39) says that if $e$ is an eigenvalue of $Q'$ of multiplicity $\mu$, then so is $-e$. His proof observes that an orthogonal change of variables carries $Q'$ into $-Q'$, and says that the assertion "clearly follows". The formal proof splits the statement in two: a version relative to an arbitrary invariant form, which is what §7 actually needs, and the matrix version Milnor states.

**Assertion 1, relative form (V5.4).** Let $J$ be a complex structure on a real normed space $E$, $G$ a $J$-invariant and $L$ a $J$-anti-invariant bilinear form. Then for every real $e$ the forms $L - eG$ and $L + eG$ have the same nullity ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · nullity_sub_smul_eq_nullity_add_smul'⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Pull $L - eG$ back by $J$:
$$(L - eG)(Jv, Jw) = -L(v, w) - eG(v, w) = -(L + eG)(v, w).$$
So the change of variables $J$ carries $L - eG$ into $-(L + eG)$. The nullity is unchanged by a linear change of variables ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · nullity_bilinearComp_equiv⟧ and by a change of sign ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · nullity_neg⟧.

When $G$ is positive definite, the nullity of $L - eG$ is the multiplicity of $e$ as an eigenvalue of $L$ *relative to* $G$: the dimension of the space of $v$ with $L(v, \cdot) = eG(v, \cdot)$.

**Assertion 1 (V5.3).** Let $V$ be a complex inner product space, $B$ a continuous complex-bilinear form on $V$ and $e$ real. Then $\operatorname{Re}B - e\operatorname{Re}\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ and $\operatorname{Re}B + e\operatorname{Re}\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ have the same nullity ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · nullity_reBilin_sub_smul_reInner_eq⟧. This is the relative form with $J = i\cdot$, $G = \operatorname{Re}\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$, invariant by §V.1, and $L = \operatorname{Re}B$, anti-invariant by §V.1.

Milnor speaks of the eigenvalues of $Q'$, that is, of its matrix. The formal statement takes a real-linear operator $A$ on $V$ with $\operatorname{Re}\langle Au, w\rangle = \operatorname{Re}B(u, w)$ for all $u, w$: the symmetric matrix of $Q'$ in orthonormal real coordinates. The $e$-eigenspace of such an $A$ is the null space of $\operatorname{Re}B - e\operatorname{Re}\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · eigenspace_eq_nullSpace_sub_smul_reInner⟧. If $Au = eu$, then $\operatorname{Re}B(u, w) - e\operatorname{Re}\langle u, w\rangle = \operatorname{Re}\langle Au - eu, w\rangle = 0$ for every $w$. Conversely, if $u$ is in the null space, test against $w = Au - eu$: this gives $\|Au - eu\|^2 = 0$.

**Assertion 1, matrix form (V5.3).** With $A$ as above, the $e$-eigenspace and the $(-e)$-eigenspace of $A$ have the same dimension ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · finrank_eigenspace_neg_eq⟧. Both are null spaces, by the previous lemma. Since $\operatorname{Re}B - (-e)\operatorname{Re}\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle = \operatorname{Re}B + e\operatorname{Re}\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$, Assertion 1 gives the equality. The multiplicity is read as the dimension of the eigenspace, which for a symmetric matrix is the usual multiplicity.

*What "clearly follows" contains.* The orthogonal map $J$ with $Q'(Ju) = -Q'(u)$ anticommutes with $A$, so it maps the $e$-eigenspace injectively into the $(-e)$-eigenspace and back. The formal proof performs the same exchange on null spaces, without mentioning commutation. *Why the relative form matters.* Assertion 1 concerns eigenvalues for the coordinate inner product of $\mathbb R^{2k}$. Focal points are governed by the eigenvalues of the second fundamental form $(v \cdot \ell_{ij})$ relative to the first fundamental form $(g_{ij})$ (Assertion 6.4 and Lemma 6.3, Part III). The two agree only if the real coordinates are orthonormal at $q$, which Milnor assumes in §6 (p. 34) but not on p. 40. What Assertion 2 needs is the relative form, with $G = (g_{ij})$, which is $J$-invariant by the Cauchy–Riemann equations.

### V.3 The Hessian of $L_p$ in holomorphic coordinates

On p. 40 Milnor takes holomorphic coordinates centred at $q$ and a unit normal $v$. The Hermitian product $\sum_\alpha w_\alpha(z)\bar v_\alpha$ is a constant plus $Q(z)$ plus higher terms, the linear terms vanishing because $v$ is orthogonal to $M$. Its real part, the real inner product $w \cdot v$, is a constant plus $Q'(x, y)$ plus higher terms, and Milnor says that the quadratic terms "clearly" determine the second fundamental form in the direction $v$. The formal proof replaces the remark by an explicit formula for the whole Hessian of $L_p \circ w$ in the chart.

**Theorem (V5.6).** Let $V$ be a complex normed space, $F$ a complex inner product space, $w : V \to F$ complex $C^2$ at $q$, and $p \in F$. For all real directions $\xi, \xi' \in V$,
$$d^2\big(\|w(\cdot) - p\|^2\big)_q(\xi, \xi') = 2\Big(\operatorname{Re}\langle dw_q\xi,\, dw_q\xi'\rangle + \operatorname{Re}\big\langle w(q) - p,\ d^2w_q(\xi, \xi')\big\rangle\Big),$$
where $dw_q$ and $d^2w_q$ are the complex first and second derivatives of $w$ ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · hessian_normSq_sub_holomorphic⟧.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. Write $\|w(z) - p\|^2 = \operatorname{Re}\langle w(z) - p, w(z) - p\rangle$ ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · reInner_self⟧.
2. A map that is $C^2$ at a point, for a finite order, is $C^2$ on a neighbourhood. So $w$ is complex differentiable at every $z$ near $q$, and its complex derivative $dw_z$, restricted to real scalars, is its real derivative.
3. Near $q$, the real derivative of $z \mapsto \operatorname{Re}\langle w(z) - p, w(z) - p\rangle$ is $\xi \mapsto 2\operatorname{Re}\langle w(z) - p, dw_z\xi\rangle$. The rule for a bilinear form evaluated on two differentiable maps gives $\operatorname{Re}\langle dw_z\xi, w(z) - p\rangle + \operatorname{Re}\langle w(z) - p, dw_z\xi\rangle$, and the two terms are equal by symmetry of the real inner product ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · reInner_comm⟧.
4. The Hessian at $q$ is the derivative at $q$ of $z \mapsto df_z$. Near $q$ this map coincides with $z \mapsto 2\operatorname{Re}\langle w(z) - p, \cdot\rangle \circ dw_z$, a product of two operator-valued maps, and the Hessian depends only on the germ. The product rule differentiates it. The factor $z \mapsto \operatorname{Re}\langle w(z) - p, \cdot\rangle$ has derivative $\xi \mapsto \operatorname{Re}\langle dw_q\xi, \cdot\rangle$ at $q$. The factor $z \mapsto dw_z$ has derivative $\xi \mapsto d^2w_q(\xi, \cdot)$ at $q$. Here the second complex derivative enters: $C^2$ at $q$ makes $z \mapsto dw_z$ of class $C^1$ at $q$, hence differentiable there. Evaluating the derivative in the direction $\xi$ at $\xi'$ gives the formula.

The two terms behave oppositely under $J = i\cdot$. The first is unchanged by $(\xi, \xi') \mapsto (i\xi, i\xi')$, because $dw_q(i\xi) = i\,dw_q\xi$ and multiplication by $i$ is orthogonal ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · reInner_I_smul⟧. It is the first fundamental form of $w$, and its $J$-invariance is the Cauchy–Riemann equations in disguise. The second changes sign, because $d^2w_q$ is complex bilinear and $\langle w(q) - p, \cdot\rangle$ is complex-linear in its second slot: it is the real part of a complex bilinear form. For $p = w(q) + tv$ with $v$ a unit normal, the second term on the diagonal is $-2t\,Q'(\xi)$, $Q$ being the quadratic part of Milnor's $\sum_\alpha w_\alpha\bar v_\alpha$. This is the precise content of "the quadratic terms determine the second fundamental form".

The formula holds at every point, and it never uses the vanishing of the linear terms of Milnor's expansion. The fact behind that vanishing is nevertheless proved. **Real orthogonality to a complex subspace is Hermitian orthogonality (V5.5):** if $T$ is a complex subspace and $\operatorname{Re}\langle a, v\rangle = 0$ for every $a \in T$, then $\langle a, v\rangle = 0$ for every $a \in T$ ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · inner_eq_zero_of_re_inner_eq_zero⟧. The real part vanishes by hypothesis. For the imaginary part, apply the hypothesis to $ia \in T$: $\langle ia, v\rangle = \bar\imath\langle a, v\rangle = -i\langle a, v\rangle$, whose real part is $\operatorname{Im}\langle a, v\rangle$. Milnor's normal vector is orthogonal to $TM_q$ in the real sense, while the linear coefficients are Hermitian products; since $TM_q$ is a complex subspace, the lemma with $T = TM_q$ closes the gap. Nothing below uses it, since the formal route avoids power series.

### V.4 Assertion 2

Milnor's Assertion 2 (p. 40) says that the focal points of $(M, q)$ along a normal line occur in pairs symmetric about $q$, with equal multiplicities. The formal proof gives its algebraic content and its form in a holomorphic chart.

**Assertion 2, algebraic content (V5.4).** Let $J$ be a complex structure, $G$ a $J$-invariant and $L$ a $J$-anti-invariant bilinear form. For every real $t$, $G - tL$ and $G + tL$ have the same nullity ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · nullity_sub_smul_eq_nullity_add_smul⟧.

*Proof.* $(G - tL)(Jv, Jw) = G(v, w) + tL(v, w)$, so $J$ carries $G - tL$ into $G + tL$, and the nullity is invariant ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · nullity_bilinearComp_equiv⟧. With $G = (g_{ij})$ and $L = (v \cdot \ell_{ij})$ these two nullities are the multiplicities of $q + tv$ and $q - tv$ as focal points, by Assertion 6.4, which is formalized for real slice submanifolds ⟦Chapter4/Focal · isFocalPointMult_add_smul_iff⟧ (Part III).

**Assertion 2 in a holomorphic chart (V5.7).** Let $w : V \to F$ be complex $C^2$ at $q$, $n \in F$ and $t$ real. The Hessians at $q$ of $\|w(\cdot) - (w(q) + tn)\|^2$ and of $\|w(\cdot) - (w(q) - tn)\|^2$ have the same nullity ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · nullity_hessian_normSq_sub_add_eq_sub⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Call the two Hessians $H_+$ and $H_-$, and let $J = i\cdot$, an isomorphism ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · complexStructureEquiv⟧. By §V.3,
$$H_+(i\xi, i\xi') = 2\Big(\operatorname{Re}\langle dw_q(i\xi), dw_q(i\xi')\rangle + \operatorname{Re}\big\langle w(q) - (w(q) + tn),\ d^2w_q(i\xi, i\xi')\big\rangle\Big).$$
The first term is $\operatorname{Re}\langle dw_q\xi, dw_q\xi'\rangle$, by complex linearity of $dw_q$ and orthogonality of $i$. In the second, $w(q) - (w(q) + tn) = -tn$ and $d^2w_q(i\xi, i\xi') = -d^2w_q(\xi, \xi')$, so it is $\operatorname{Re}\langle tn, d^2w_q(\xi, \xi')\rangle$. That is exactly the second term of $H_-(\xi, \xi')$, since $w(q) - (w(q) - tn) = tn$. So $H_+(J\cdot, J\cdot) = H_-$, and the nullity is invariant under the change of variables $J$ ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · nullity_bilinearComp_equiv⟧.

The formal statement assumes nothing of $n$, which need be neither normal nor a unit vector, nor of $q$, which need not be critical. For a unit normal $n$ at $w(q)$, the two nullities are the multiplicities of $w(q) \pm tn$ as focal points, by Assertion 6.4 and the formula of p. 36 for the Hessian of $L_p$. That translation is not formalized, because a holomorphic chart of a complex submanifold is not identified with a chart of the real slice submanifold (§V.6). Milnor's route through the eigenvalues of $Q'$ is not used.

### V.5 The Andreotti–Frankel identity and the index bound

Milnor ends the argument on p. 41 by counting. There are at most $2k$ focal points on the normal line, symmetric about $q$, so at most $k$ lie between $q$ and $p$, and by Lemma 6.9 the index of $L_p$ at $q$ is at most $k$. The formal proof uses instead the argument of Andreotti and Frankel, to whom Milnor credits the proof of §7 (p. 39). It needs no focal points.

**The Andreotti–Frankel identity (V5.8).** Let $G$, $L$, $H$ be bilinear forms on a real normed space $E$ and $J : E \to E$ any map such that, for all $v$,
$$H(v, v) = G(v, v) - L(v, v), \qquad G(Jv, Jv) = G(v, v), \qquad L(Jv, Jv) = -L(v, v).$$
Then $H(Jv, Jv) = 2G(v, v) - H(v, v)$ ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · apply_J_apply_J_eq⟧.

*Proof.* $H(Jv, Jv) = G(Jv, Jv) - L(Jv, Jv) = G(v, v) + L(v, v) = 2G(v, v) - H(v, v)$. Neither linearity of $J$ nor anything off the diagonal is used.

On a complex vector space, with $J = i\cdot$ and $L = \operatorname{Re}B$ for a complex bilinear $B$, the condition on $L$ is automatic ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · apply_I_smul_apply_I_smul_eq⟧. On a complex inner product space with $G(v, v) = \|v\|^2$ it reads $Q(iv) = 2\|v\|^2 - Q(v)$ for $Q(v) = \|v\|^2 - \operatorname{Re}B(v, v)$ ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · apply_I_smul_apply_I_smul_eq_two_mul_norm_sq_sub⟧.

**The index bound (V5.9).** Let $E$ be a finite-dimensional real normed space, $H$ a bilinear form and $J$ an injective linear map of $E$ with $H(v, v) + H(Jv, Jv) \ge 0$ for all $v$. Then $2\cdot\operatorname{index}H \le \dim E$ ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · two_mul_index_le_finrank⟧.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. Choose a subspace $W$ of dimension $\operatorname{index}H$ on which $H$ is negative definite ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · exists_negDef_finrank_eq_index⟧.
2. $J(W)$ has the same dimension as $W$, because $J$ is injective.
3. $W \cap J(W) = 0$. Let $v \in W \cap J(W)$, say $v = Jw$ with $w \in W$, and suppose $v \ne 0$. Then $w \ne 0$, since $J0 = 0$. So $H(w, w) < 0$, and $H(Jw, Jw) = H(v, v) < 0$ because $v \in W$. This contradicts $H(w, w) + H(Jw, Jw) \ge 0$.
4. By the dimension formula $\dim(W + JW) + \dim(W \cap JW) = \dim W + \dim JW$ ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/FiniteDimensional/Lemmas · Submodule.finrank_sup_add_finrank_inf_eq⟧, $\dim(W + JW) = 2\dim W$, which is at most $\dim E$.

On a finite-dimensional complex vector space $V$: if $H(v, v) + H(iv, iv) \ge 0$ for all $v$, then $\operatorname{index}H \le \dim_{\mathbb C}V$ ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · index_le_finrank_complex⟧. Apply the theorem to $J = i\cdot$ and use $\dim_{\mathbb R}V = 2\dim_{\mathbb C}V$ ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/Complex/FiniteDimensional · finrank_real_of_complex⟧.

**The index bound for Milnor's shape (V5.10).** Let $V$ be a complex vector space of finite dimension $k$, $G$ a bilinear form with $G(v, v) \ge 0$ and $G(iv, iv) = G(v, v)$, and $B$ a continuous complex-bilinear form. If $H(v, v) = G(v, v) - \operatorname{Re}B(v, v)$ for all $v$, then $\operatorname{index}H \le k$ ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · index_le_finrank_of_eq_sub_re⟧.

*Proof.* The identity, with $L = \operatorname{Re}B$, whose anti-invariance on the diagonal is §V.1, gives $H(v, v) + H(iv, iv) = 2G(v, v) \ge 0$; then apply the bound on a complex space. Two cases are recorded: every form whose quadratic form is $\|v\|^2 - \operatorname{Re}B(v, v)$ has index at most $k$ ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · index_le_finrank_of_hermitian⟧; and so does the Hessian at $q$ of any $f : V \to \mathbb R$ whose Hessian has, on the diagonal, the shape $G - \operatorname{Re}B$ ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · index_hessian_le_finrank_complex⟧. Neither criticality, nor non-degeneracy, nor any smoothness of $f$ is assumed: only the shape of the Hessian matters.

**Milnor's index bound in holomorphic coordinates (V5.11).** Let $V$ be a complex vector space of finite dimension $k$, $F$ a complex inner product space, $w : V \to F$ complex $C^2$ at $q$, and $p \in F$. Then the Hessian of $z \mapsto \|w(z) - p\|^2$ at $q$ has index at most $k$ ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · index_hessian_normSq_sub_holomorphic_le⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Let $H$ be the Hessian. By §V.3 at $(\xi, \xi)$ and at $(i\xi, i\xi)$,
$$H(\xi, \xi) = 2\big(\|dw_q\xi\|^2 + \operatorname{Re}\langle w(q) - p, d^2w_q(\xi, \xi)\rangle\big), \qquad H(i\xi, i\xi) = 2\big(\|dw_q\xi\|^2 - \operatorname{Re}\langle w(q) - p, d^2w_q(\xi, \xi)\rangle\big).$$
The first terms agree because $dw_q(i\xi) = i\,dw_q\xi$ and $i$ is orthogonal; the second terms are opposite because $d^2w_q(i\xi, i\xi) = i^2d^2w_q(\xi, \xi)$. So $H(\xi, \xi) + H(i\xi, i\xi) = 4\|dw_q\xi\|^2 \ge 0$, and the bound on a complex space applies ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · index_le_finrank_complex⟧.

This is the formal counterpart of Milnor's "the index of $L_p$ at $q$ is at most $k$", and it is stronger: $q$ need not be a critical point, the Hessian need not be non-degenerate, and $dw_q$ need not be injective. When $w$ parametrizes a complex submanifold $M$ near a critical point $w(q)$ of $L_p|M$, this Hessian is the Hessian of $L_p|M$ in the real coordinates $(x, y)$. Its index is then the index of $L_p$ at $w(q)$, by the chart independence of the index in a parametrization ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · indexM_normSq_sub_eq⟧ (Part I). Making that identification formal needs $M$ as a real slice submanifold with $w^{-1}$ among its charts, which is not done (§V.6). Milnor's count also needs facts he does not state: that neither $q$ nor $p$ is focal ($p$ is not, by Lemma 6.5, since $q$ is non-degenerate), and the case $p = q$. The formal route needs none of them.

**No negative semidefinite Hessian (V5.12).** If in V5.10 the form $G$ is moreover positive definite and $V \ne 0$, then $H(v, v) > 0$ for some $v$ ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · exists_pos_apply_of_eq_sub_re⟧. Take $v \ne 0$. If $H(v, v) \le 0$, then $H(iv, iv) = 2G(v, v) - H(v, v) > 0$. With $G(\xi, \xi) = 2\|dw_q\xi\|^2$ (§V.3), positive definite where $dw_q$ is injective, this says that the Hessian of $L_p$ in a holomorphic chart of positive dimension is never negative semidefinite. The consequence drawn in the exposition, that $L_p$ has no local maximum on a complex submanifold of positive dimension, is not formalized.

### V.6 What is not formalized in §7

None of the four topological statements of §7 is formalized. Each needs something that neither this project nor Mathlib provides.

- **Theorem 7.2** (p. 39), the homotopy type of a closed complex submanifold, is missing three things. (i) *Complex submanifolds as manifolds.* Holomorphic charts would have to be charts of a real slice submanifold of $\mathbb R^{2n}$. This is the bridge that would carry V5.7 and V5.11 to focal points and to the index of $L_p : M \to \mathbb R$, which the project computes in a real parametrization ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · indexM_normSq_sub_eq⟧ and relates to focal points by Lemma 6.9 ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · indexM_normSq_sub_eq_finsum_focalMult⟧. (ii) *Theorem 3.5 for non-compact manifolds.* The formal Theorem 3.5 assumes the submanifold compact ⟦Chapter2/CWType · theorem_3_5⟧ (Part II). A closed complex submanifold of $\mathbb C^n$ of positive dimension is never compact, and the non-compact case needs the Appendix and Whitehead's theorem, which Mathlib has only in an abstract model-category form. (iii) *CW-complexes.* Even for compact manifolds the formal Theorem 3.5 produces an iterated adjunction space, not a CW-complex, for lack of cellular approximation. The remaining ingredient is available: for almost every $p$, $L_p$ is a Morse function with compact sublevel sets on a closed slice submanifold ⟦Chapter4/Existence · ae_isMorseFunction_normSq_sub_and_isCompact_sublevel⟧ (Theorem 6.6, Part III).
- **Theorem 7.1** (p. 39) needs Theorem 7.2 and the vanishing of the homology of a CW-complex above its dimension. Cellular homology is not available.
- **Corollary 7.3** (p. 41) needs relative singular homology, the exact sequence of a pair and Lefschetz duality.
- **Theorem 7.4** (pp. 41–42) needs relative homotopy groups and a deformation of a neighbourhood of $V \cap P$ into $V \cap P$, which Milnor obtains from the triangulability of algebraic varieties (quoted). It also needs Theorems 3.1–3.2 for $1/L_p$ on $V$ in $\mathbb CP_n$. Algebraic varieties and complex projective space are not formalized here.

The statement of Theorem 7.2 also says more than its proof gives: the proof yields a CW-complex of dimension *at most* $k$ ($\mathbb C$ is contractible). Assertion 2 as a statement about focal points, and Milnor's count on p. 41, are replaced by V5.7–V5.11.

### V.7 Curvature tensors at a point and Milnor's octahedron

By Lemma 9.1, the curvature of a connection at a point $p$ is a trilinear map $TM_p^3 \to TM_p$. The fourth identity of Lemma 9.3 and the whole theory of sectional curvature are therefore linear algebra on the inner product space $TM_p$. The formal text develops them once, for any real inner product space $E$ and any map $R : E \times E \times E \to E$ that is linear in each argument, $R(X, Y)Z$ standing for Milnor's notation. For submanifolds, Lemma 9.1 itself is proved in §V.16, which then applies everything here.

**Definition.** $R$ is a *curvature tensor* if it satisfies (1)–(3) of Lemma 9.3 (p. 53): (1) $R(X, Y)Z + R(Y, X)Z = 0$; (2) $R(X, Y)Z + R(Y, Z)X + R(Z, X)Y = 0$; (3) $\langle R(X, Y)Z, W\rangle + \langle R(X, Y)W, Z\rangle = 0$ ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · IsCurvatureTensor⟧.

At this level the three identities are hypotheses. Milnor proves (2) and (3) on a manifold by extending vectors to fields with vanishing brackets, which is differential geometry; for submanifolds the formal proofs of (2) and (3) are in §V.16. The definition has immediate consequences: $R(Y, X) = -R(X, Y)$ ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · IsCurvatureTensor.apply_swap⟧, and $R(X, X) = 0$, because (1) gives $2R(X, X)Z = 0$ ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · IsCurvatureTensor.apply_self⟧. Also $\langle R(X, Y)W, Z\rangle = -\langle R(X, Y)Z, W\rangle$ ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · IsCurvatureTensor.inner_swap⟧, $\langle R(X, Y)Z, Z\rangle = 0$ ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · IsCurvatureTensor.inner_apply_self⟧, and (2) paired with $W$, $\langle R(X, Y)Z, W\rangle + \langle R(Y, Z)X, W\rangle + \langle R(Z, X)Y, W\rangle = 0$ ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · IsCurvatureTensor.inner_bianchi⟧.

Milnor calls (3) "clearly equivalent" to $\langle R(X, Y)Z, Z\rangle = 0$ (p. 53). The formal statement: for a linear map $T$, the form $\langle TZ, W\rangle$ is skew-symmetric iff $\langle TZ, Z\rangle = 0$ for every $Z$ ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · inner_add_inner_eq_zero_iff⟧. Taking $W = Z$ gives $2\langle TZ, Z\rangle = 0$. Conversely, expand $\langle T(Z + W), Z + W\rangle = 0$ by linearity of $T$; the terms $\langle TZ, Z\rangle$ and $\langle TW, W\rangle$ vanish and what remains is $\langle TZ, W\rangle + \langle TW, Z\rangle = 0$. The equivalence uses linearity of $R$ in $Z$.

**Milnor's octahedron (V6.21).** Let $\alpha$ be any set and $T : \alpha^4 \to \mathbb R$ any function that is skew-symmetric in its first two arguments, skew-symmetric in its last two, and satisfies the cyclic identity $T(X, Y, Z, W) + T(Y, Z, X, W) + T(Z, X, Y, W) = 0$. Then $T(X, Y, Z, W) = T(Z, W, X, Y)$ ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · octahedron⟧.

*The proof as formalized* is Milnor's (p. 54), with the signs he leaves to the reader written out. Write $[XYZW]$ for $T(X, Y, Z, W)$ and name the six vertices of the octahedron
$$T = [XYZW],\quad B = [ZWXY],\quad L = [XWYZ],\quad K = [YWZX],\quad Q = [YZXW],\quad F = [ZXYW].$$
Each shaded face is an instance of the cyclic identity, brought into shape by the two skew-symmetries.

- *Face $W$:* the cyclic identity for $X, Y, Z$ with last argument $W$ is $T + Q + F = 0$ as it stands.
- *Face $Z$:* the cyclic identity for $X, Y, W$ with last argument $Z$ reads $[XYWZ] + [YWXZ] + [WXYZ] = 0$. Skew-symmetry in the last pair gives $[XYWZ] = -T$ and $[YWXZ] = -K$; skew-symmetry in the first pair gives $[WXYZ] = -L$. So $T + K + L = 0$.
- *Face $X$:* the cyclic identity for $Y, Z, W$ with last argument $X$ reads $[YZWX] + [ZWYX] + [WYZX] = 0$, where $[YZWX] = -Q$ and $[ZWYX] = -B$ by the last pair and $[WYZX] = -K$ by the first. So $Q + B + K = 0$.
- *Face $Y$:* the cyclic identity for $Z, W, X$ with last argument $Y$ reads $[ZWXY] + [WXZY] + [XZWY] = 0$. Here $[ZWXY] = B$; $[WXZY] = -[XWZY] = [XWYZ] = L$, using both skew-symmetries; and likewise $[XZWY] = -[ZXWY] = [ZXYW] = F$. So $B + L + F = 0$.

The two faces at the top vertex $T$ are added and the two at the bottom vertex $B$ subtracted:
$$(T + Q + F) + (T + K + L) - (Q + B + K) - (B + L + F) = 2T - 2B = 0.$$
No linearity of $T$ is used, nor any structure on $\alpha$; the formal proof is the list of the fourteen instances above, four of the cyclic identity and ten of the skew-symmetries, followed by linear arithmetic.

**Lemma 9.3 (4) (V6.21).** Every curvature tensor satisfies $\langle R(X, Y)Z, W\rangle = \langle R(Z, W)X, Y\rangle$ ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · IsCurvatureTensor.inner_comm_pairs⟧. Apply the octahedron to $T(X, Y, Z, W) = \langle R(X, Y)Z, W\rangle$. Skew-symmetry in the first pair is (1) paired with $W$. Skew-symmetry in the last pair is (3). The cyclic identity is (2) paired with $W$.

### V.8 Sectional curvature, polarization and constant curvature

Milnor defines the sectional curvature only on p. 100, in a remark after Lemma 19.1. For orthonormal $A, B \in TM_p$, $\langle R(A, B)A, B\rangle$ is the sectional curvature "determined by $A$ and $B$". He does not say that it depends only on the plane of $A$ and $B$, nor that these numbers determine $R$. Both are proved here, as Lemmas 7.1 and 7.2 of the exposition's Chapter 6.

**Definition.** For vectors $X, Y$,
$$K(X, Y) = \frac{\langle R(X, Y)X, Y\rangle}{\|X\|^2\|Y\|^2 - \langle X, Y\rangle^2}$$
⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · sectionalCurvature⟧. For an orthonormal pair this is Milnor's $\langle R(A, B)A, B\rangle$. For a dependent pair the denominator vanishes, and the formal convention for division makes $K = 0$.

**Lemma (V6.22).** If $R$ is a curvature tensor and $ad - bc \ne 0$, then $K(aX + bY, cX + dY) = K(X, Y)$ ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · IsCurvatureTensor.sectionalCurvature_smul_add⟧.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. The Gram determinant of $(aX + bY, cX + dY)$ is $(ad - bc)^2$ times that of $(X, Y)$, by expanding the inner products ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · gram_smul_add⟧.
2. $R(aX + bY, cX + dY) = (ad - bc)R(X, Y)$, by bilinearity, $R(X, X) = R(Y, Y) = 0$ and $R(Y, X) = -R(X, Y)$ ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · IsCurvatureTensor.apply_smul_add⟧.
3. Hence the numerator is $(ad - bc)\langle R(X, Y)(aX + bY), cX + dY\rangle$. Of the four terms of the expansion, $\langle R(X, Y)X, X\rangle$ and $\langle R(X, Y)Y, Y\rangle$ vanish, and $\langle R(X, Y)Y, X\rangle = -\langle R(X, Y)X, Y\rangle$, by (3). What remains is $(ad - bc)^2\langle R(X, Y)X, Y\rangle$ ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · IsCurvatureTensor.inner_apply_smul_add⟧ ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · IsCurvatureTensor.inner_apply_self_eq⟧.
4. Numerator and denominator are both multiplied by $(ad - bc)^2 \ne 0$.

The Gram determinant of an independent pair is positive ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · gram_pos⟧. $Y \ne 0$, and $u = \langle Y, Y\rangle X - \langle X, Y\rangle Y$ is not $0$ by independence; then $\langle u, u\rangle = \langle Y, Y\rangle\,(\|X\|^2\|Y\|^2 - \langle X, Y\rangle^2)$ is positive, and so is $\langle Y, Y\rangle$. For a dependent pair the numerator vanishes as well, because $R(X, Y) = 0$ when one vector is a multiple of the other ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · IsCurvatureTensor.inner_apply_self_eq_zero_of_not_linearIndependent⟧.

*Polarization.* The *second mixed difference* of a function $k$ of two vectors is
$$\Delta_{A,C}\Delta_{B,D}k = k(A{+}C, B{+}D) - k(A, B{+}D) - k(C, B{+}D) - k(A{+}C, B) + k(A, B) + k(C, B) - k(A{+}C, D) + k(A, D) + k(C, D)$$
⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · mixedDiff⟧. For $k(A, B) = \langle R(A, B)A, B\rangle$, with $R$ a curvature tensor,
$$\Delta_{A,C}\Delta_{B,D}k = 2\big(\langle R(A, B)C, D\rangle + \langle R(A, D)C, B\rangle\big)$$
⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · IsCurvatureTensor.mixedDiff_eq⟧. The function $k$ is quadratic in its first argument, which fills the first and third slots, and in its second, which fills the second and fourth. Of the sixteen terms of $k(A + C, B + D)$, the mixed difference keeps the four in which $A$ and $C$ both occur among the first and third slots and $B$ and $D$ both among the second and fourth: $\langle R(A, B)C, D\rangle + \langle R(A, D)C, B\rangle + \langle R(C, B)A, D\rangle + \langle R(C, D)A, B\rangle$. Pair symmetry (Lemma 9.3 (4)) identifies the last two with the first two.

**Polarization (V6.23).** For a curvature tensor,
$$6\langle R(X, Y)Z, W\rangle = 2\,\Delta_{X,Z}\Delta_{Y,W}k + \Delta_{Y,X}\Delta_{Z,W}k$$
⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · IsCurvatureTensor.polarization⟧.

*Proof.* Write $T = \langle R(X, Y)Z, W\rangle$, $Q = \langle R(Y, Z)X, W\rangle$, $F = \langle R(Z, X)Y, W\rangle$. By the previous lemma the right side is $4(T + \langle R(X, W)Z, Y\rangle) + 2(Q + \langle R(Y, W)X, Z\rangle)$. Pair symmetry and (1) give $\langle R(X, W)Z, Y\rangle = \langle R(Z, Y)X, W\rangle = -Q$ and $\langle R(Y, W)X, Z\rangle = \langle R(X, Z)Y, W\rangle = -F$. So the right side is $4T - 2Q - 2F$, which is $6T$ by (2) in the form $T + Q + F = 0$.

**The sectional curvatures determine $R$ (V6.23).** Two curvature tensors with the same $\langle R(X, Y)X, Y\rangle$ for all $X, Y$ are equal ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · IsCurvatureTensor.ext⟧. By polarization the two have the same $\langle R(X, Y)Z, W\rangle$ for every $W$, hence the same $R(X, Y)Z$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Basic · ext_inner_right⟧. The same holds if they have the same sectional curvature on all independent pairs ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · IsCurvatureTensor.ext_of_sectionalCurvature⟧: on an independent pair the Gram determinant is positive, so the numerators agree, and on a dependent pair both numerators are $0$. Finally, it holds for Milnor's own definition: if $\langle R(A, B)A, B\rangle = \langle R'(A, B)A, B\rangle$ for all orthonormal $A, B$, then $R = R'$ ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · IsCurvatureTensor.ext_of_orthonormal⟧. This rests on Gram–Schmidt for the sectional numerator ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · inner_apply_self_eq_zero_or_exists_orthonormal⟧. If $X = 0$, or $Y$ is a multiple of $X$, then $\langle R(X, Y)X, Y\rangle = 0$ for every curvature tensor. Otherwise put $A = X/\|X\|$, $Y' = Y - \langle A, Y\rangle A$ and $B = Y'/\|Y'\|$. These are orthonormal, $X = \|X\|A$ and $Y = \langle A, Y\rangle A + \|Y'\|B$, so $\langle R(X, Y)X, Y\rangle = (\|X\|\,\|Y'\|)^2\langle R(A, B)A, B\rangle$ for every curvature tensor at once.

**Constant curvature (V6.24).** Put $R_1(X, Y)Z = \langle X, Z\rangle Y - \langle Y, Z\rangle X$ ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · curvatureOne⟧ ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · curvatureOne_apply⟧. It is a curvature tensor ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · isCurvatureTensor_curvatureOne⟧. (1) is visible. In (2) the six terms cancel in pairs by symmetry of the inner product. (3) is $\langle X, Z\rangle\langle Y, W\rangle - \langle Y, Z\rangle\langle X, W\rangle + \langle X, W\rangle\langle Y, Z\rangle - \langle Y, W\rangle\langle X, Z\rangle = 0$. Its sectional numerator is $\langle R_1(X, Y)X, Y\rangle = \|X\|^2\|Y\|^2 - \langle X, Y\rangle^2$ ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · inner_curvatureOne_self⟧, so $\kappa R_1$, again a curvature tensor ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · IsCurvatureTensor.smul⟧, has sectional curvature $\kappa$ on every independent pair ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · sectionalCurvature_smul_curvatureOne⟧. Conversely, let $R$ be a curvature tensor. If $\langle R(A, B)A, B\rangle = \kappa$ for every orthonormal pair, then $R = \kappa R_1$ ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · IsCurvatureTensor.eq_smul_curvatureOne_of_orthonormal⟧: compare with $\kappa R_1$, whose value on an orthonormal pair is $\kappa(1 \cdot 1 - 0^2) = \kappa$. If $K(X, Y) = \kappa$ for every independent pair, then
$$R(X, Y)Z = \kappa\big(\langle X, Z\rangle Y - \langle Y, Z\rangle X\big)$$
⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · IsCurvatureTensor.apply_eq_of_sectionalCurvature_eq⟧.

### V.9 Milnor's §8 in Mathlib's language, and the sign of $R$

On an abstract manifold the formal text matches Milnor's definitions with Mathlib's covariant derivatives. Let $M$ be a smooth manifold in Mathlib's sense, modelled on a real normed space $E$. A Mathlib *covariant derivative* on the tangent bundle is a function $\nabla$ that assigns to every vector field $Y$ and every point $x$ a continuous linear map $\nabla Y(x) : T_xM \to T_xM$ ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/VectorBundle/CovariantDerivative/Basic · CovariantDerivative⟧. It must be additive in $Y$ and satisfy the Leibniz rule at every point where the sections involved are differentiable ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/VectorBundle/CovariantDerivative/Basic · IsCovariantDerivativeOn⟧. Milnor's $X_p \vdash Y$ (p. 43) is the value of $\nabla Y(p)$ at $X_p$; Mathlib's argument order is the reverse of the usual one.

*Milnor's rules (p. 44; V6.3).* Since $\nabla Y(x)$ is a linear map on $T_xM$, three things are built into the definition: dependence on $X_p$ only, linearity in $X_p$, and rule (3), $(fX) \vdash Y = f\,(X \vdash Y)$ ⟦Chapter6/Connection · cov_smul_left⟧. Rule (2), additivity in $Y$, holds at every point where both summands are differentiable ⟦Chapter6/Connection · cov_add_right⟧. Rule (4), $X \vdash (fY) = (Xf)\,Y + f\,(X \vdash Y)$, holds at every point where $f$ and $Y$ are differentiable ⟦Chapter6/Connection · cov_smul_right⟧. Here $Xf$ is the derivative of $f$ at $x$, read as a linear form on $T_xM$, applied to $X_x$ ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/MFDeriv/NormedSpace · mvfderiv⟧. Rule (1), smoothness of $X \vdash Y$ for smooth $X$ and $Y$, is a separate class ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/VectorBundle/CovariantDerivative/Basic · ContMDiffCovariantDerivative⟧. The hidden hypothesis is differentiability at the point. A Mathlib covariant derivative is defined on all sections and is unconstrained on sections that are not differentiable at $x$. Christoffel symbols, the covariant derivative along a curve and parallel transport do not exist in Mathlib, so Lemmas 8.1–8.3 are not stated at this level.

**Symmetric connections (Definition 8.5).** $\nabla$ is *symmetric* if $X \vdash Y - Y \vdash X = [X, Y]$ at every point where $X$ and $Y$ are differentiable ⟦Chapter6/Connection · IsSymmetric⟧. Here $[X, Y]$ is Mathlib's Lie bracket of vector fields on a manifold ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/VectorField/LieBracket · VectorField.mlieBracket⟧, which has Milnor's sign, $[X, Y]f = X(Yf) - Y(Xf)$. Mathlib's torsion is $T(X, Y) = \nabla_XY - \nabla_YX - [X, Y]$ ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/VectorBundle/CovariantDerivative/Torsion · CovariantDerivative.torsion⟧. For a finite-dimensional model space, Milnor's symmetric connections are exactly the torsion-free ones ⟦Chapter6/Connection · isSymmetric_iff_torsion_eq_zero⟧, by Mathlib's characterization of vanishing torsion ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/VectorBundle/CovariantDerivative/Torsion · CovariantDerivative.torsion_eq_zero_iff⟧.

**Compatible connections (Corollary 8.4).** Milnor defines compatibility by "parallel translation preserves inner products" (p. 47). That cannot be stated without parallel transport. Its consequence, Corollary 8.4 (p. 48), is taken as the definition instead. With a Riemannian metric on the tangent bundle ⟦Mathlib/Topology/VectorBundle/Riemannian · RiemannianBundle⟧, $\nabla$ is *compatible* if
$$X_x\langle Y, Y'\rangle = \langle X_x \vdash Y, Y'_x\rangle + \langle Y_x, X_x \vdash Y'\rangle$$
at every point $x$, for all vector fields differentiable there ⟦Chapter6/Connection · IsCompatible⟧. For a finite-dimensional model space and a $C^1$ metric ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/VectorBundle/Riemannian · IsContMDiffRiemannianBundle⟧ this is Mathlib's notion of a metric connection, `IsMetricCompatible` ⟦Chapter6/Connection · isCompatible_iff_isMetricCompatible⟧.

*The Koszul formula.* Put
$$\kappa(X, Y, Z) = X\langle Y, Z\rangle + Y\langle X, Z\rangle - Z\langle X, Y\rangle + \langle[X, Y], Z\rangle - \langle[X, Z], Y\rangle - \langle[Y, Z], X\rangle$$
at $x$ ⟦Chapter6/Connection · koszul⟧. It involves only the metric and the bracket. For a symmetric compatible connection and $X, Y, Z$ differentiable at $x$, $2\langle X \vdash Y, Z\rangle = \kappa(X, Y, Z)$ ⟦Chapter6/Connection · two_mul_inner_cov_eq_koszul⟧. *Proof.* Rewrite the three derivative terms by compatibility and the three brackets by symmetry. Twelve inner products result. Ten cancel in pairs, and two copies of $\langle X \vdash Y, Z\rangle$ remain. For coordinate fields, whose brackets vanish, this is Milnor's first Christoffel identity (p. 49), without his linear system. That system has a misprint: of the three unknowns as printed, the second equals the first by the symmetry he quotes; they should be $\langle\partial_i \vdash \partial_j, \partial_k\rangle$, $\langle\partial_i \vdash \partial_k, \partial_j\rangle$ and $\langle\partial_j \vdash \partial_k, \partial_i\rangle$.

**Lemma 8.6, uniqueness, on an abstract manifold (V6.14).** Two symmetric connections compatible with the metric agree on every vector field $Y$ differentiable at $x$: $\nabla Y(x) = \nabla' Y(x)$ ⟦Chapter6/Connection · cov_eq_of_isSymmetric_of_isCompatible⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Fix $X_0 \in T_xM$ and put $Z_0 = \nabla Y(x)X_0 - \nabla'Y(x)X_0$. Mathlib extends any vector at $x$ to a section that is constant in the trivialization of the tangent bundle at $x$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/FiberBundle/Basic · FiberBundle.extend⟧. That section is differentiable at $x$ ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/VectorBundle/MDifferentiable · mdifferentiableAt_extend⟧ and takes the given value there ⟦Mathlib/Topology/FiberBundle/Basic · FiberBundle.extend_apply_self⟧. Extend $X_0$ and $Z_0$ to fields $X$ and $Z$ in this way. The Koszul formula for both connections gives $2\langle\nabla Y(x)X_0, Z_0\rangle = \kappa(X, Y, Z)(x) = 2\langle\nabla' Y(x)X_0, Z_0\rangle$, the middle term not involving the connection. So $\langle Z_0, Z_0\rangle = 0$. With Mathlib's predicates: two torsion-free metric connections agree on fields differentiable at the point ⟦Chapter6/Connection · cov_eq_of_torsion_eq_zero_of_isMetricCompatible⟧.

Uniqueness holds only on sections differentiable at the point: as functions on all sections, two Levi-Civita connections need not agree, and Milnor never uses them elsewhere. *Existence* is not proved at this level. Mathlib has no Levi-Civita connection, and Milnor's "one can verify" (p. 49) hides two things: a converse of Lemma 8.3, which he does not state, and the patching of the local connections defined by the Christoffel symbols. In the embedded setting both halves are proved (§V.15).

**The curvature tensor and its sign (V6.16).** Milnor's curvature of vector fields (p. 51) is
$$R(X, Y)Z = -X \vdash (Y \vdash Z) + Y \vdash (X \vdash Z) + [X, Y] \vdash Z$$
⟦Chapter6/Connection · milnorCurvature⟧. Nomizu's, which most modern texts use, is $\nabla_X\nabla_YZ - \nabla_Y\nabla_XZ - \nabla_{[X, Y]}Z$ ⟦Chapter6/Connection · nomizuCurvature⟧. Milnor's footnote on p. 51, that Nomizu gives $R$ the opposite sign, is the identity $R_{\mathrm{Milnor}} = -R_{\mathrm{Nomizu}}$, proved by rearranging the terms ⟦Chapter6/Connection · milnorCurvature_eq_neg⟧. Lemma 9.3 (1) holds for vector fields with no hypothesis, $R(Y, X)Z = -R(X, Y)Z$ ⟦Chapter6/Connection · milnorCurvature_swap⟧, because $[Y, X] = -[X, Y]$ ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/VectorField/LieBracket · VectorField.mlieBracket_swap⟧. The tensor property (Lemma 9.1) and (2)–(4) are not proved at this level.

Mathlib defines no curvature, so there is no Mathlib sign to reconcile. Three statements record how the convention enters. Identities (1)–(3) are invariant under $R \mapsto -R$ ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · IsCurvatureTensor.neg⟧. The sectional curvature is the same number in both conventions: Milnor writes $\langle R(X, Y)X, Y\rangle$, the standard convention writes $\langle R^{\mathrm{std}}(X, Y)Y, X\rangle$, and by (3) the two are equal when $R^{\mathrm{std}} = -R$ ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · IsCurvatureTensor.inner_neg_apply_swap⟧. Constant curvature $\kappa$ reads $R(X, Y)Z = \kappa(\langle X, Z\rangle Y - \langle Y, Z\rangle X)$ with Milnor's sign and $\kappa(\langle Y, Z\rangle X - \langle X, Z\rangle Y)$ with the standard one ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · neg_smul_curvatureOne_apply⟧. The Gauss equation of §V.16 confirms that Milnor's convention gives the unit sphere curvature $+1$ (§V.21).

### V.10 Submanifolds of a Euclidean space: derivatives along $N$

From here on, $V$ is a finite-dimensional real inner product space (Milnor's $\mathbb R^K$) and $N \subseteq V$ a slice submanifold in the sense of the companion project (§0.1), modelled on $Z$ with complementary space $W$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · IsSliceSubmanifold⟧ ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · SliceChart⟧. $N$ carries the induced metric: tangent vectors at $q$ are vectors of $V$, with the inner product of $V$. $Z$ is finite-dimensional throughout, and $W$ wherever the symmetry of the second fundamental form is used; when $N$ is non-empty both hold automatically, since $Z \times W$ is isomorphic to $V$. No closedness or compactness of $N$ is assumed anywhere. A map $F : N \to E$ into a normed space is smooth, or differentiable at $q$, as a map of manifolds.

*Tangent spaces and parametrizations* (§0.3). $T_qN \subseteq V$ is the range of the derivative at $q$ of the inclusion $\iota : N \to V$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · tangentSubspace⟧ ⟦Milnor/Chapter5/Orient · mfd⟧. A slice chart $\sigma$ has the parametrization $x(u) = \sigma^{-1}(u, 0)$, Milnor's $u \mapsto x(u)$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · sliceParam⟧, smooth on its domain ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · contDiffOn_sliceParam⟧. For any slice chart $\sigma$ around $q$, $T_qN$ is the range of $dx_u$ at $u = (\sigma q)_1$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · tangentSubspace_eq_range_fderiv_sliceParam⟧.

*Chart coordinates of an ambient vector.* For each $q$ the formal text uses the slice chart chosen at $q$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · sliceAt⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · mem_sliceAt_source⟧, whose induced chart is the preferred chart of the manifold $N$ at $q$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · chartAt_eq_sliceAt⟧. With $\sigma$ this chart, the chart coordinates of $v \in V$ are $\tau_q(v) = (d\sigma_q v)_1 \in Z$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · tanCoord⟧. They invert the derivative of the inclusion: $\tau_q(d\iota_q z) = z$ for $z \in Z$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · tanCoord_mfderiv_val⟧, because the preferred chart of $N$ at $q$ is $x \mapsto (\sigma x)_1$ restricted to $N$, whose derivative at $q$ is $\tau_q \circ d\iota_q$ by the chain rule, while the derivative of a chart at its own base point, read in that chart, is the identity. Hence $d\iota_q(\tau_qv) = v$ for $v \in T_qN$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · mfderiv_val_tanCoord⟧.

**Definition (V6.1).** For $F : N \to E$ the linear map $d^NF_q = dF_q \circ \tau_q : V \to E$ is *the derivative of $F$ at $q$ on ambient vectors* ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_apply⟧. On $v \in T_qN$ it is the manifold derivative of $F$ applied to the chart coordinates of $v$; we write it $dF_q(v)$. Off $T_qN$ its values depend on the chart, and no statement uses them. It depends only on the germ of $F$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_congr⟧ and vanishes on constants ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_const⟧.

The bridge to ambient calculus is the chain rule. If $G : V \to E$ is differentiable at $q$, then $d(G|_N)_q(v) = dG_q(v)$ for $v \in T_qN$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_comp_val⟧, by the manifold chain rule and $d\iota_q(\tau_qv) = v$. Hence if $F = G$ on $N$ near $q$, then $dF_q(v) = dG_q(v)$ for tangent $v$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_eq_fderiv⟧. In particular, an ambient map that vanishes on $N$ near $q$ has zero derivative at $q$ in tangent directions ⟦Chapter6/Projection · fderiv_eq_zero_of_eventually⟧. Every $F$ differentiable at $q$ has such a representative: with $\sigma$ the slice chart chosen at $q$, put $G(y) = F(\text{the point of } N \text{ with chart coordinate } (\sigma y)_1)$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · exists_differentiableAt_rep⟧. It is differentiable at $q$, as $F$ read in the chart composed with $y \mapsto (\sigma y)_1$, and it equals $F$ on $N$ near $q$, because the chart is inverted by its inverse. If $F$ is smooth, the same formula is smooth on the open set of $y$ in the domain of $\sigma$ with $(\sigma y)_1$ in the image of the chart ⟦Chapter6/Projection · exists_contDiffOn_rep⟧.

All the calculus of $d^N$ is reduced to ambient calculus in this way. Sums and differences behave as expected ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_add⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_neg⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_sub⟧. Continuous linear maps commute with $d^N$, on all of $V$: $d(L \circ F) = L \circ dF$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_clm_comp⟧. The key rule is the Leibniz rule for a continuous bilinear map $\beta$:
$$d\big(\beta(F_1, F_2)\big)_q(v) = \beta\big(dF_{1,q}(v), F_2(q)\big) + \beta\big(F_1(q), dF_{2,q}(v)\big) \qquad (v \in T_qN)$$
⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_bilinear⟧. Choose representatives $G_1, G_2$; then $\beta(G_1, G_2)$ represents $\beta(F_1, F_2)$, the ambient product rule differentiates it, and the chain rule translates back. It specializes to the rules for a function times a vector ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_smul⟧, for inner products ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_inner⟧, for an operator-valued map applied to a vector-valued one ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_clm_apply⟧, and for a composition of operator-valued maps ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_clm_comp'⟧. The derivative of the inclusion is the identity on $T_qN$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_val⟧.

### V.11 The tangent projection $P_q = A(A^{\mathsf T}A)^{-1}A^{\mathsf T}$

Let $P_q$ be the orthogonal projection of $V$ onto $T_qN$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · tanProj⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Projection/Basic · Submodule.starProjection⟧. It takes values in $T_qN$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · tanProj_apply_mem⟧, fixes exactly the tangent vectors ⟦Chapter6/Projection · tanProj_eq_self⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · tanProj_eq_self_iff⟧, is idempotent ⟦Chapter6/Projection · tanProj_tanProj⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · tanProj_comp_tanProj⟧ and self-adjoint ⟦Chapter6/Projection · inner_tanProj_left⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · inner_tanProj_of_mem⟧. It kills exactly the normal space ⟦Chapter6/Projection · tanProj_eq_zero_iff⟧, $v - P_qv$ is normal ⟦Chapter6/Projection · sub_tanProj_mem_orthogonal⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · tanProj_sub_tanProj⟧, and a vector both tangent and normal is $0$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · eq_zero_of_mem_of_mem_orthogonal⟧.

In the embedded setting every smoothness question of §8 reduces to one fact: $q \mapsto P_q$ is smooth. Milnor checks the smoothness of what he builds chart by chart (condition (1), p. 44) and never needs this statement. It is not automatic, because the projection onto the range of an operator $A$ is a smooth function of $A$ only where the rank of $A$ does not drop.

*The Gram formula.* Let $Y$ be a finite-dimensional normed space and $A : Y \to V$ linear. Its transpose is $A^{\mathsf T} : V \to Y^*$, $v \mapsto \langle v, A\cdot\rangle$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · transposeL⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · transposeL_apply⟧, and its Gram operator is $A^{\mathsf T}A : Y \to Y^*$, $y \mapsto \langle Ay, A\cdot\rangle$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · gramL⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · gramL_apply⟧. If $A$ is injective, so is $A^{\mathsf T}A$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · gramL_injective⟧: $A^{\mathsf T}A(y - y') = 0$ gives $\|A(y - y')\|^2 = 0$. As $\dim Y^* = \dim Y$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · finrank_clmDual_eq⟧, it is then bijective ⟦Chapter6/Projection · gramL_bijective⟧, an isomorphism ⟦Chapter6/Projection · gramEquiv⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · coe_gramEquiv⟧. The formal inverse of a linear map is total, $0$ where there is no inverse, but at an injective $A$ it is the true inverse ⟦Chapter6/Projection · gramL_inverse_apply⟧. Put $\Pi(A) = A(A^{\mathsf T}A)^{-1}A^{\mathsf T}$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · gramProj⟧.

**Lemma.** If $A$ is injective, $\Pi(A)$ is the orthogonal projection onto the range of $A$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · gramProj_eq_starProjection⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · gramProj_eq_starProjection'⟧. *Proof.* $y = (A^{\mathsf T}A)^{-1}A^{\mathsf T}v$ satisfies $\langle Ay, Ay'\rangle = \langle v, Ay'\rangle$ for every $y'$. So $v - Ay$ is orthogonal to the range of $A$, and $Ay$ lies in it: $Ay$ is the projection of $v$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Projection/Basic · Submodule.eq_starProjection_of_mem_of_inner_eq_zero⟧.

**Lemma.** $\Pi$ is smooth near every injective $A$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · contDiffAt_gramProj⟧. $A \mapsto A^{\mathsf T}$ is linear, $A \mapsto A^{\mathsf T}A$ is a product of smooth maps, inversion is smooth near the isomorphism $A^{\mathsf T}A$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/ContDiff/Operations · contDiffAt_map_inverse⟧, and $\Pi$ is a composite of these.

*In a slice chart.* The parametrization of a slice chart has injective derivative at every point of its domain ⟦Chapter6/Projection · injective_fderiv_sliceParam⟧. Near $u$, $\sigma(x(u')) = (u', 0)$, so by the chain rule $d\sigma \circ dx_u$ is the inclusion $Z \to Z \times W$, which is injective. Since $T_qN$ is the range of $dx_u$, $P_q = \Pi(dx_u)$ at $u = (\sigma q)_1$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · tanProj_eq_gramProj⟧.

**Theorem (V6.2).** The map $q \mapsto P_q$ from $N$ to the operators on $V$ is smooth ⟦Chapter6/Projection · contMDiff_tanProj⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* A slice chart $\sigma$ gives the ambient map $y \mapsto \Pi(dx_{(\sigma y)_1})$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · projRep⟧. It equals $P$ at the points of $N$ in the domain of $\sigma$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · projRep_eq⟧. It is smooth at those points ⟦Chapter6/Projection · contDiffAt_projRep⟧, as a composite of three smooth maps: $y \mapsto (\sigma y)_1$, smooth because $\sigma$ is smooth on its open domain; $u \mapsto dx_u$, the derivative of a map smooth on an open set; and $\Pi$, smooth at the injective $dx_u$. Near $q$, with $\sigma$ the chart chosen at $q$, $P$ coincides with this ambient map composed with the smooth inclusion. In particular $P$ is differentiable at every point ⟦Chapter6/Projection · mdifferentiableAt_tanProj⟧, and its derivative along $N$ in a tangent direction is the ambient derivative of the extension ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_tanProj_eq_fderiv⟧.

For compact $N$ the project also has the nearest-point retraction $r$ of the companion project, with $dr_q = P_q$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · dret_eq_starProjection⟧ (Part III). The construction here needs no compactness and applies to every slice submanifold.

*Every tangent vector extends to a smooth vector field.* For $u \in V$ the field $q \mapsto P_qu$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · projField⟧ is tangent ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · isTangentField_projField⟧, smooth ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · contMDiff_projField⟧, and equal to $u$ at every $q$ with $u \in T_qN$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · projField_apply_of_mem⟧. These global fields replace Milnor's coordinate fields cut off by bump functions in all the uniqueness arguments below.

### V.12 The derivative of the projection

For $v \in V$ write $D_v = d^NP_q\,v$, the derivative of $P$ at $q$ in the direction $v$, an operator on $V$. Differentiating the identities $P^* = P$ and $P^2 = P$ along $N$ gives its structure.

**Lemma.** $D_v$ is self-adjoint, for every $v \in V$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · inner_dN_tanProj_comm⟧. *Proof.* Fix $x, y$. The linear functional $\ell(T) = \langle Tx, y\rangle - \langle x, Ty\rangle$ on operators vanishes on every $P_q$. Since $d(\ell \circ P) = \ell \circ dP$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_clm_comp⟧ and the derivative of the zero function is $0$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_const⟧, $\ell(D_v) = 0$.

**Lemma.** For $v \in T_qN$, $D_vP_q + P_qD_v = D_v$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_tanProj_comp_add⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_tanProj_apply_add⟧, by the Leibniz rule for compositions ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_clm_comp'⟧ applied to $P \circ P = P$.

Multiplying by $P_q$ on the left gives $P_qD_vP_q = 0$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · tanProj_dN_tanProj⟧. So $D_v$ maps tangent vectors to normal vectors ⟦Chapter6/Projection · tanProj_dN_tanProj_of_mem⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_tanProj_mem_orthogonal_of_mem⟧. It also maps normal vectors to tangent vectors ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_tanProj_mem_of_mem_orthogonal⟧: for $\nu$ normal, $P_q\nu = 0$ and the identity becomes $P_qD_v\nu = D_v\nu$.

### V.13 The Levi-Civita connection of the induced metric

A *vector field* on $N$ is a map $X : N \to V$ with $X_q \in T_qN$ for every $q$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · IsTangentField⟧; it is smooth if it is smooth as a map of manifolds $N \to V$.

**Definition.** For $v \in T_qN$ and $Y : N \to V$, $v \vdash Y = P_q(dY_q(v))$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · covAt⟧, and $(X \vdash Y)_q = P_q(dY_q(X_q))$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · leviCivita⟧. Both are tangent ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · covAt_mem⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · leviCivita_mem⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · isTangentField_leviCivita⟧.

**Milnor's rules (V6.4).** (1) $X \vdash Y$ is smooth when $X$ is a smooth vector field and $Y$ a smooth map ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · contMDiff_leviCivita⟧. First, $q \mapsto dY_q(X_q)$ is smooth ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · contMDiff_dN_apply⟧: near $q$ it equals $x \mapsto dG_x(X_x)$ for a smooth representative $G$ of $Y$ on an open set $U$, and $x \mapsto dG_x$ is smooth on $U$. Then apply the smooth $P$. (2) $X \vdash Y$ is additive in $X$ with no hypothesis ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · leviCivita_add_left⟧, and additive in $Y$ at points where both summands are differentiable ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · leviCivita_add_right⟧. (3) $(fX) \vdash Y = f\,(X \vdash Y)$ for every function $f$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · leviCivita_smul_left⟧, since $dY_q$ is linear. (4) $X \vdash (fY) = (Xf)\,Y + f\,(X \vdash Y)$ at $q$, with $Xf = df_q(X_q)$, when $f$ and $Y$ are differentiable at $q$ and $X_q$, $Y_q$ are tangent ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · leviCivita_smul_right⟧. Indeed $d(fY)(X) = df(X)\,Y + f\,dY(X)$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_smul⟧, and $P_q$ fixes $Y_q$. The hypotheses of (2) and (4) are Milnor's tacit ones: his fields are smooth.

**Corollary 8.4 for the induced metric (V6.11).** Let $Y, Y'$ be differentiable at $q$ with $Y_q, Y'_q$ tangent, and $v \in T_qN$. Then
$$v\langle Y, Y'\rangle = \langle v \vdash Y, Y'_q\rangle + \langle Y_q, v \vdash Y'\rangle$$
⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · dN_inner_eq_covAt⟧. *Proof.* The Leibniz rule for inner products ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_inner⟧ gives $\langle dY_q(v), Y'_q\rangle + \langle Y_q, dY'_q(v)\rangle$. Since $Y'_q$ is tangent and $P_q$ is self-adjoint, $\langle dY_q(v), Y'_q\rangle = \langle P_qdY_q(v), Y'_q\rangle$, and likewise for the other term. For the induced connection, compatibility is a one-line computation.

### V.14 The second fundamental form

**Definition.** $\mathrm{II}_q(v, w) = D_vw$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · secondFundForm⟧. It is bilinear ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · secondFundForm_add_left⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · secondFundForm_smul_left⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · secondFundForm_add_right⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · secondFundForm_smul_right⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · secondFundForm_sub_right⟧.

Milnor meets the second fundamental form only in §6, as the normal component $\ell_{ij}$ of $\partial^2x/\partial u^i\partial u^j$ (p. 34), and never mentions it in Part II. In the embedded setting it is the tool for symmetry and curvature.

**Lemma (V6.12).** For a vector field $W$ differentiable at $q$ and $v \in T_qN$, $\mathrm{II}_q(v, W_q) = dW_q(v) - P_q\,dW_q(v)$, the normal part of $dW_q(v)$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · secondFundForm_eq_sub_tanProj⟧. *Proof.* $W = PW$ as maps on $N$, since $W$ is tangent. The Leibniz rule ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_clm_apply⟧ gives $dW_q(v) = D_vW_q + P_qdW_q(v)$.

So the normal part of $dW_q(v)$ depends only on $v$ and $W_q$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · sub_tanProj_dN_eq_of_eq⟧: this is the tensoriality of the second fundamental form. For tangent $v, w$, $\mathrm{II}_q(v, w)$ is normal ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · secondFundForm_mem_orthogonal⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · tanProj_secondFundForm⟧, and $\mathrm{II}_q(v, \cdot)$ is self-adjoint on all of $V$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · inner_secondFundForm_comm⟧. One more identity is used in the curvature computation: for a vector field $W$ differentiable at $q$ and $u, v \in T_qN$,
$$P_q\,\mathrm{II}_q\big(u, dW_q(v)\big) = \mathrm{II}_q\big(u, \mathrm{II}_q(v, W_q)\big)$$
⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · tanProj_secondFundForm_dN⟧. Split $dW_q(v) = P_qdW_q(v) + \mathrm{II}_q(v, W_q)$. The first part contributes $P_qD_uP_q(\cdots) = 0$. The second is normal, so $D_u$ maps it to a tangent vector, which $P_q$ fixes.

**Theorem (symmetry of $\mathrm{II}$, V6.12).** For $v, w \in T_qN$, $\mathrm{II}_q(v, w) = \mathrm{II}_q(w, v)$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · secondFundForm_comm⟧.

*The idea.* $T_xN$ is the kernel of the derivative of the normal coordinate of a slice chart. Differentiating that statement along $N$ expresses the normal coordinate of $\mathrm{II}(v, w)$ through a second derivative, which is symmetric.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. Let $\sigma$ be the slice chart chosen at $q$ and $g(y) = (\sigma y)_2 \in W$ its normal coordinate. Then $dg_y = \mathrm{pr}_2 \circ d\sigma_y$ on the domain of $\sigma$, and $g$ is smooth near $q$, because $\sigma$ is smooth on its open domain ⟦Milnor/Chapter8/TangentBundle · Problems.sliceChart_contDiffOn⟧.
2. For $x \in N$ in the domain of $\sigma$, $T_xN = \ker dg_x$, a lemma of the companion project ⟦Milnor/Chapter8/TangentBundle · Problems.sliceChart_mem_tangentSpace_iff⟧. Hence $dg_x \circ P_x = 0$ for such $x$.
3. Replace $P$ near $q$ by its smooth ambient extension $\Pi(dx_{(\sigma y)_1})$ of §V.11 ⟦Chapter6/Projection · projRep_eq⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · contDiffAt_projRep⟧. The ambient map $y \mapsto dg_y \circ \Pi(\ldots)$ is differentiable at $q$ and vanishes on $N$ near $q$, so its derivative in a tangent direction $a$ is $0$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · fderiv_eq_zero_of_eventually⟧. By the product rule, applied to a tangent $b$ with $P_qb = b$: $d^2g_q(a, b) + dg_q(D_ab) = 0$, where the derivative of the extension in the direction $a$ is $D_a$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_tanProj_eq_fderiv⟧. So $dg_q(\mathrm{II}_q(a, b)) = -d^2g_q(a, b)$ for all tangent $a, b$.
4. $d^2g_q$ is symmetric, $g$ being smooth ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/FDeriv/Symmetric · ContDiffAt.isSymmSndFDerivAt⟧. Hence $dg_q\big(\mathrm{II}_q(v, w) - \mathrm{II}_q(w, v)\big) = 0$, so the difference lies in $\ker dg_q = T_qN$.
5. The difference is also normal, as a difference of normal vectors. So it is $0$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · eq_zero_of_mem_of_mem_orthogonal⟧.

### V.15 The bracket, symmetry, and Lemma 8.6

**Definition.** For maps $X, Y : N \to V$, $[X, Y]_q = dY_q(X_q) - dX_q(Y_q)$, the bracket computed in $V$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · bracket⟧.

Milnor defines $[X, Y]$ by its action on functions. That for fields on $N$ it is computed by the ambient formula, and that this ambient vector is tangent to $N$, are both statements that need proof.

**Lemma.** If $X, Y$ are vector fields differentiable at $q$, then $[X, Y]_q \in T_qN$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · bracket_mem⟧. *Proof.* By §V.14 the normal part of $dY_q(X_q)$ is $\mathrm{II}_q(X_q, Y_q)$ and that of $dX_q(Y_q)$ is $\mathrm{II}_q(Y_q, X_q)$. They are equal by the symmetry of $\mathrm{II}$. So the bracket equals its own projection $P_qdY_q(X_q) - P_qdX_q(Y_q)$.

**Definition 8.5: the induced connection is symmetric (V6.13).** For vector fields differentiable at $q$, $X \vdash Y - Y \vdash X = [X, Y]$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · leviCivita_sub_leviCivita⟧: the left side is $P_q[X, Y]_q$, and the bracket is tangent.

**Lemma (the bracket is Milnor's Poisson bracket).** For a smooth function $f$ and smooth vector fields $X, Y$, $X(Yf) - Y(Xf) = [X, Y]f$ at every $q$, where $Xf = df(X)$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · dN_bracket⟧.

*Proof.* Take a smooth representative $G$ of $f$ on an open $U \ni q$. Near $q$, $Af = (x \mapsto dG_x(A_x))$ for any smooth vector field $A$. So, by the Leibniz rule for an operator applied to a vector ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_clm_apply⟧ and the chain rule ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_comp_val⟧, $B(Af)$ at $q$ is $d^2G_q(B_q, A_q) + dG_q(dA_q(B_q))$ for $B_q$ tangent. Therefore $X(Yf) - Y(Xf) = d^2G_q(X_q, Y_q) - d^2G_q(Y_q, X_q) + dG_q([X, Y]_q)$. The second derivative is symmetric ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/FDeriv/Symmetric · ContDiffAt.isSymmSndFDerivAt⟧, and $dG_q = df_q$ on the tangent vector $[X, Y]_q$.

**Lemma 8.6 in the embedded setting (V6.14).** Call an operation $D$ on maps $N \to V$ *symmetric and compatible* if, for all smooth vector fields $X, Y, Y'$, $D(X, Y)$ is tangent, $D(X, Y) - D(Y, X) = [X, Y]$, and $X\langle Y, Y'\rangle = \langle D(X, Y), Y'\rangle + \langle Y, D(X, Y')\rangle$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · IsSymmCompat⟧. Then:

- *Existence.* $P(dY(X))$ is symmetric and compatible ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · isSymmCompat_leviCivita⟧, by §V.13 and the symmetry just proved.
- *The Koszul formula.* Every symmetric compatible $D$ satisfies, on smooth vector fields,
$$2\langle D(X, Y), Z\rangle = X\langle Y, Z\rangle + Y\langle X, Z\rangle - Z\langle X, Y\rangle + \langle[X, Y], Z\rangle - \langle[X, Z], Y\rangle - \langle[Y, Z], X\rangle$$
⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · IsSymmCompat.two_mul_inner⟧, by the same rewriting as in §V.9.
- *Uniqueness.* Every symmetric compatible $D$ agrees with $P(dY(X))$ on smooth vector fields ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · IsSymmCompat.eq_leviCivita⟧. Put $u = D(X, Y)_q - (X \vdash Y)_q$, a tangent vector, and let $Z$ be the smooth field $x \mapsto P_xu$, equal to $u$ at $q$ (§V.11). The Koszul formula for $D$ and for $P(dY(X))$ has the same right side, so $2\langle D(X, Y)_q, u\rangle = 2\langle(X \vdash Y)_q, u\rangle$, that is, $\langle u, u\rangle = 0$.

The uniqueness assumes no linearity or tensoriality of $D$, only the three properties on smooth fields. Milnor proves uniqueness with the Christoffel identities (p. 49) and asserts the converse, that the connection they define is symmetric and compatible, with "one can verify". In the embedded setting the converse is the computation of §V.13 together with the symmetry of $\mathrm{II}$. The abstract uniqueness of §V.9 and this embedded statement are parallel results, not one theorem, since the induced connection is not packaged as a Mathlib covariant derivative.

### V.16 The curvature of $N$: Lemma 9.1, the Gauss equation, Lemma 9.3

**Definition.** With the induced connection and the ambient bracket, Milnor's curvature of maps $X, Y, Z : N \to V$ is
$$R(X, Y)Z = -X \vdash (Y \vdash Z) + Y \vdash (X \vdash Z) + [X, Y] \vdash Z$$
⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvature⟧. At a point, put $R_q(x, y) = D_yD_x - D_xD_y$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvOp⟧, so that $R_q(x, y)z = \mathrm{II}_q(y, \mathrm{II}_q(x, z)) - \mathrm{II}_q(x, \mathrm{II}_q(y, z))$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvOp_apply⟧.

**Theorem (Lemma 9.1 made explicit, V6.17).** For smooth vector fields $X, Y, Z$ and every $q$,
$$R(X, Y)Z\,(q) = \mathrm{II}_q\big(Y_q, \mathrm{II}_q(X_q, Z_q)\big) - \mathrm{II}_q\big(X_q, \mathrm{II}_q(Y_q, Z_q)\big)$$
⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvature_eq⟧.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. Take a smooth representative $G$ of $Z$ on an open $U \ni q$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · exists_contDiffOn_rep⟧. For $x \in N \cap U$ and $v \in T_xN$, $dZ_x(v) = dG_x(v)$.
2. For smooth vector fields $A$ and $B$, at $q$,
$$B \vdash (A \vdash Z) = \mathrm{II}_q\big(B_q, \mathrm{II}_q(A_q, Z_q)\big) + P_q\,d^2G_q(B_q, A_q) + P_q\,dG_q\big(dA_q(B_q)\big).$$
By definition $A \vdash Z = x \mapsto P_x(dZ_x(A_x))$, and $x \mapsto dZ_x(A_x)$ is smooth ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · contMDiff_dN_apply⟧. The Leibniz rule for $P$ applied to it gives $d(A \vdash Z)_q(B_q) = D_{B_q}(dZ_q(A_q)) + P_q\,d(x \mapsto dZ_x(A_x))_q(B_q)$. Near $q$ the map $x \mapsto dZ_x(A_x)$ equals $x \mapsto dG_x(A_x)$, whose derivative in the direction $B_q$ is $d^2G_q(B_q, A_q) + dG_q(dA_q(B_q))$, by the Leibniz rule for the operator field $x \mapsto dG_x$ applied to $A$. Apply $P_q$ and use $P_q^2 = P_q$ and $P_q\,\mathrm{II}_q(B_q, dZ_q(A_q)) = \mathrm{II}_q(B_q, \mathrm{II}_q(A_q, Z_q))$ (§V.14).
3. The bracket is tangent (§V.15), so $[X, Y] \vdash Z = P_qdG_q(dY_q(X_q)) - P_qdG_q(dX_q(Y_q))$.
4. In $-X \vdash (Y \vdash Z) + Y \vdash (X \vdash Z) + [X, Y] \vdash Z$, step 2 for $(A, B) = (Y, X)$ and $(X, Y)$ and step 3 leave
$$\mathrm{II}(Y, \mathrm{II}(X, Z)) - \mathrm{II}(X, \mathrm{II}(Y, Z)) + P_q\big(d^2G_q(Y_q, X_q) - d^2G_q(X_q, Y_q)\big),$$
the first-derivative terms of $G$ cancelling against the bracket. The last term vanishes by symmetry of $d^2G_q$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/FDeriv/Symmetric · ContDiffAt.isSymmSndFDerivAt⟧.

**The Gauss equation (V6.18).** For all $x, y, z, w \in V$,
$$\langle R_q(x, y)z, w\rangle = \langle\mathrm{II}_q(x, z), \mathrm{II}_q(y, w)\rangle - \langle\mathrm{II}_q(x, w), \mathrm{II}_q(y, z)\rangle$$
⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · inner_curvOp⟧, by self-adjointness of $\mathrm{II}_q(y, \cdot)$ and $\mathrm{II}_q(x, \cdot)$. Hence, for smooth vector fields $X, Y, Z$ and any $W : N \to V$, the same identity holds for $\langle R(X, Y)Z, W\rangle$ at every point ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · gauss_equation⟧. With $Z = X$, $W = Y$ and the symmetry of $\mathrm{II}$,
$$\langle R(X, Y)X, Y\rangle = \langle\mathrm{II}(X, X), \mathrm{II}(Y, Y)\rangle - \|\mathrm{II}(X, Y)\|^2,$$
Gauss's curvature for a surface in $\mathbb R^3$. With Milnor's sign of $R$, his sectional curvature $\langle R(A, B)A, B\rangle$ is therefore Gauss's curvature, positive on the sphere (§V.21). The Gauss equation is not in Milnor's text.

**Lemma 9.1 (V6.17).** $R_q$ maps tangent vectors to tangent vectors ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvOp_mem⟧, since $\mathrm{II}_q(x, z)$ is normal and $D_y$ maps normal vectors to tangent ones. It is linear in $x$ and in $y$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvOp_add_left⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvOp_smul_left⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvOp_add_right⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvOp_smul_right⟧. So it defines a trilinear map $T_qN \times T_qN \times T_qN \to T_qN$, the curvature tensor of $N$ at $q$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvTensor⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · coe_curvTensor_apply⟧. By the theorem, $R(X, Y)Z$ at $q$ is this tensor applied to $X_q, Y_q, Z_q$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvature_eq_curvTensor⟧, and so depends only on those values ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvature_congr⟧. Milnor proves tensoriality in $X$ and says the identities for $Y$ and $Z$ are "easily obtained by similar computations" (p. 51). For $Z$ the computation is a different one: the terms with two derivatives of $f$ cancel only by $[X, Y]f = X(Yf) - Y(Xf)$. In the formal proof this shows up as the symmetry of $d^2G$ in step 4.

**Lemma 9.3 (1)–(3) (V6.20).** The curvature tensor of $N$ at each point is a curvature tensor in the sense of §V.7 ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · isCurvatureTensor_curvTensor⟧. (1) is visible from the formula. (2) is the symmetry of $\mathrm{II}$: the six terms of $R(x, y)z + R(y, z)x + R(z, x)y$ cancel in pairs once $\mathrm{II}(y, x)$, $\mathrm{II}(z, y)$, $\mathrm{II}(x, z)$ are replaced by $\mathrm{II}(x, y)$, $\mathrm{II}(y, z)$, $\mathrm{II}(z, x)$. (3) is the Gauss equation, whose right side is visibly skew in $(z, w)$. For vector fields the identities read: (1) with no hypothesis at all ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvature_add_swap⟧, since $[Y, X] = -[X, Y]$; (2) ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvature_bianchi⟧ and (3) ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · inner_curvature_add_inner⟧ for smooth vector fields. Milnor proves (2) and (3) for extensions of the given vectors with vanishing brackets, which must be constructed: coordinate fields cut off by a bump function. No extension is needed here.

**Lemma 9.3 (4) (V6.21).** For smooth vector fields, $\langle R(X, Y)Z, W\rangle = \langle R(Z, W)X, Y\rangle$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · inner_curvature_comm_pairs⟧, by the octahedron of §V.7 applied to the tensor at $q$. The theory of §V.8 (sectional curvature, polarization, constant curvature) applies to the curvature tensor of $N$ at every point.

### V.17 Covariant derivatives along curves: Lemmas 8.1 and 8.3

A curve in $N$ is a map $c : \mathbb R \to N$ such that $t \mapsto c(t) \in V$ is smooth. It is then smooth as a map into the manifold $N$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · contMDiff_curve⟧. Its velocity $c'(t)$ ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · velocity⟧ is tangent to $N$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · deriv_mem_tangentSubspace⟧: it is the image under $d\iota$ of the manifold derivative of $c$. The chain rule along $c$ reads $(F \circ c)'(t) = dF_{c(t)}(c'(t))$ for $F$ differentiable at $c(t)$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · hasDerivAt_comp_curve⟧, proved with an ambient representative of $F$. Smooth maps compose with $c$ to smooth functions of $t$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · contDiff_comp_curve⟧. In particular $t \mapsto P_{c(t)}$ is smooth ⟦Chapter6/Projection · contDiff_tanProj_comp⟧, with derivative $D_{c'(t)}$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · hasDerivAt_tanProj_comp⟧.

**Definition (V6.5).** A *vector field along $c$* (p. 45) is a map $X : \mathbb R \to V$ with $X(t) \in T_{c(t)}N$ for every $t$ ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · IsFieldAlong⟧. Its *covariant derivative* is $DX/dt\,(t) = P_{c(t)}(X'(t))$ ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · covDeriv⟧, a tangent vector ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · covDeriv_mem⟧. Smoothness of a field along $c$ is smoothness of $t \mapsto X(t) \in V$.

Milnor characterizes $D/dt$ by three axioms (p. 46), and $P(X')$ satisfies them. (a) $D(X + Y)/dt = DX/dt + DY/dt$ at $t$ when $X, Y$ are differentiable at $t$ ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · covDeriv_add⟧. (b) $D(fX)/dt = f'X + f\,DX/dt$ at $t$ when $f, X$ are differentiable at $t$ and $X(t)$ is tangent ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · covDeriv_smul⟧: the product rule, and $P$ fixes $X(t)$. (c) For $c$ smooth and a map $Y : N \to V$ differentiable at $c(t)$, $D(Y \circ c)/dt\,(t) = c'(t) \vdash Y$ ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · covDeriv_comp⟧, by the chain rule along $c$.

**Lemma 8.3 for the induced metric (V6.10).** For any curve $c$ in $N$, smooth or not, and fields $X, Y$ along $c$ differentiable at $t$,
$$\frac{d}{dt}\langle X, Y\rangle = \Big\langle\frac{DX}{dt}, Y\Big\rangle + \Big\langle X, \frac{DY}{dt}\Big\rangle$$
at $t$ ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · hasDerivAt_inner_covDeriv⟧. *Proof.* The product rule in $V$ gives $\langle X', Y\rangle + \langle X, Y'\rangle$. Since $Y(t)$ is tangent and $P$ is self-adjoint, $\langle X', Y\rangle = \langle PX', Y\rangle$; likewise for the other term. Milnor's proof (p. 47) expands the two fields in a parallel orthonormal frame, which needs Lemma 8.2 for $n$ initial vectors and the smoothness of the coefficients. Here Lemma 8.3 is a direct computation and comes *before* parallel transport, which uses it.

**Lemma 8.1 (V6.6).** Let $c$ be a smooth curve. Call an operation $D$ on maps $\mathbb R \to V$ *admissible* if it satisfies (a) for smooth fields along $c$, (b) for smooth functions $f$ and smooth fields along $c$, and (c) for smooth vector fields $Y$ on $N$ ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · IsCovDerivAlong⟧. Then $X \mapsto P(X')$ is admissible ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · isCovDerivAlong_covDeriv⟧, and every admissible $D$ agrees with it on every smooth field along $c$ ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · IsCovDerivAlong.eq_covDeriv⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Milnor's uniqueness argument works in one coordinate neighbourhood. It uses that $D/dt$ is local in $t$, which follows from (b), and that the coordinate fields $\partial_j$ extend to global vector fields; neither is mentioned when he deduces uniqueness from the axioms. The formal proof uses global fields instead of coordinate fields. Let $(e_i)$ be an orthonormal basis of $V$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/InnerProductSpace/PiL2 · stdOrthonormalBasis⟧ and $E_i$ the smooth vector field $q \mapsto P_qe_i$ (§V.11).

1. A smooth field $X$ along $c$ is tangent, so $X = PX = \sum_i\langle e_i, X\rangle\,(E_i \circ c)$: expand $X(s)$ in the basis and apply $P_{c(s)}$. Each summand is a smooth field along $c$.
2. Axiom (a) gives $D(0) = 0$, from $0 = 0 + 0$, and then, by induction over finite sums, $D$ of the sum is the sum of the $D$'s.
3. Axiom (b) gives $D(\langle e_i, X\rangle(E_i \circ c)) = \langle e_i, X\rangle'\,(E_i \circ c) + \langle e_i, X\rangle\,D(E_i \circ c)$, and axiom (c) gives $D(E_i \circ c)(t) = c'(t) \vdash E_i$.

So every admissible $D$ satisfies
$$D X(t) = \sum_i\Big(\frac{d}{dt}\langle e_i, X\rangle\,P_{c(t)}e_i + \langle e_i, X(t)\rangle\;c'(t) \vdash E_i\Big)$$
⟦Chapter6/Parallel · IsCovDerivAlong.eq_sum⟧. The right side does not involve $D$, so any two admissible operations agree.

### V.18 Linear equations with skew-adjoint coefficients

In the proof of Lemma 8.2, Milnor writes the equations of a parallel field in coordinates, observes that they are linear, and concludes, citing Graves, that the solutions "can be defined for all relevant values of $t$" (p. 47). The local existence theorem gives solutions only on intervals of length about $1/\sup\|A\|$, and a global statement needs an a priori bound and a continuation argument. Mathlib has the Picard–Lindelöf theorem on intervals limited by a bound for the field on a ball ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/ODE/PicardLindelof · IsPicardLindelof⟧ and Grönwall-type uniqueness ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/ODE/ExistUnique · ODE_solution_unique_of_mem_Ioo⟧. It has no global existence theorem for linear equations. The project supplies what parallel transport needs, for a continuous family $A : \mathbb R \to \operatorname{End}(E)$.

**Uniqueness (V6.7 (i)).** On a real normed space, two solutions of $x' = A(t)x$ on an open interval that agree at one time agree on the whole interval ⟦Chapter6/LinearODE · linearODE_eqOn⟧; the same holds on $\mathbb R$ ⟦Chapter6/LinearODE · linearODE_eq⟧. A continuous family is bounded on a compact interval ⟦Chapter6/LinearODE · exists_nnnorm_le_Icc⟧, so the field $(t, x) \mapsto A(t)x$ is uniformly Lipschitz in $x$ there, and Mathlib's uniqueness theorem applies. On $\mathbb R$, restrict to an interval containing both times.

*The radial truncation.* For $R > 0$ put $\rho_R(x) = \frac{R}{\max(R, \|x\|)}\,x$ ⟦Chapter6/LinearODE · radTrunc⟧, the identity on the closed ball of radius $R$ ⟦Chapter6/LinearODE · radTrunc_of_norm_le⟧, with values in that ball ⟦Chapter6/LinearODE · norm_radTrunc_le⟧. It is $2$-Lipschitz ⟦Chapter6/LinearODE · lipschitzWith_radTrunc⟧. With $m_x = \max(R, \|x\|)$ and $m_y = \max(R, \|y\|)$,
$$\rho_R(x) - \rho_R(y) = \frac{R}{m_x}(x - y) + \frac{R(m_y - m_x)}{m_xm_y}\,y.$$
The first term has norm at most $\|x - y\|$, since $R \le m_x$. The second has norm at most $\frac{R}{m_x}|m_y - m_x|$, since $\|y\| \le m_y$, and so at most $|m_y - m_x| \le \big|\|y\| - \|x\|\big| \le \|x - y\|$.

**Global existence for skew-adjoint coefficients (V6.7 (ii)).** Let $E$ be a complete real inner product space and $A$ continuous with $\langle A(t)x, x\rangle = 0$ for all $t, x$. For every $t_0$ and $x_0$ there is a solution of $x' = A(t)x$ on all of $\mathbb R$ with $x(t_0) = x_0$ ⟦Chapter6/LinearODE · exists_skewLinearODE⟧.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. *On a bounded interval* ⟦Chapter6/LinearODE · exists_skewLinearODE_Ioo⟧. Fix $a > 0$, a bound $C$ for $\|A\|$ on $[t_0 - a, t_0 + a]$, and $R = \|x_0\| + 1$. The truncated field $f(t, x) = A(t)\rho_R(x)$ is $2C$-Lipschitz in $x$, continuous in $t$, and bounded by $L = CR$ everywhere. So the Picard–Lindelöf conditions hold on the whole interval in one step, with a ball of radius $La$ around $x_0$. Mathlib's theorem gives $\alpha$ on $[t_0 - a, t_0 + a]$ with $\alpha(t_0) = x_0$ and $\alpha' = f(t, \alpha)$ within the interval ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/ODE/ExistUnique · IsPicardLindelof.exists_eq_forall_mem_Icc_hasDerivWithinAt₀⟧.
2. *The norm is constant.* $\frac{d}{dt}\|\alpha\|^2 = 2\langle\alpha, f(t, \alpha)\rangle = 2\frac{R}{\max(R, \|\alpha\|)}\langle\alpha, A(t)\alpha\rangle = 0$ by skewness. A function continuous on the interval with zero right derivative is constant ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/MeanValue · constant_of_has_deriv_right_zero⟧. So $\|\alpha(t)\| = \|x_0\| < R$ throughout.
3. *The truncation is never active.* Hence $f(t, \alpha(t)) = A(t)\alpha(t)$, and at interior points the derivative within the interval is a genuine derivative.
4. *Gluing.* Choose such a solution $\beta_a$ for every $a > 0$. Two of them agree wherever both are defined, by uniqueness on the smaller interval. Put $\alpha(t) = \beta_{|t - t_0| + 1}(t)$. Near each $t$, $\alpha$ coincides with the single solution $\beta_{|t - t_0| + 1}$, so it solves the equation at $t$, and $\alpha(t_0) = x_0$.

Later, for the Jacobi equation, the project proves global existence for every continuous linear equation, with the a priori bound supplied by Grönwall's inequality ⟦Chapter9/JacobiODE · exists_linearODE⟧ (Part VII). Parallel transport does not use it: the skew-adjoint case gets its bound for free.

### V.19 Parallel transport: Lemma 8.2

**Definition.** A *parallel field* along $c$ (p. 46) is a differentiable field along $c$ whose covariant derivative vanishes at every $t$ ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · IsParallel⟧.

Fix a smooth curve $c$ and write $P(t) = P_{c(t)}$. Its derivative is $P'(t) = D_{c'(t)}$ ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · dProjAlong⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · hasDerivAt_tanProj_along⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · deriv_tanProj_along⟧, a smooth ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · contDiff_dProjAlong⟧, hence continuous ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · continuous_dProjAlong⟧ family of operators.

*A parallel field satisfies $X' = P'X$.* If $X$ is parallel, then $X = PX$, so $X' = P'X + PX' = P'X$, because $PX' = DX/dt = 0$ ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · IsParallel.hasDerivAt⟧. **Uniqueness in Lemma 8.2:** two parallel fields along $c$ with the same value at one time are equal ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · IsParallel.eq⟧, by uniqueness for the linear equation $X' = P'(t)X$ ⟦Chapter6/LinearODE · linearODE_eq⟧.

*Why existence needs another equation.* The natural equation $X' = P'X$ is linear with continuous coefficients, but §V.18 gives global solutions only for skew-adjoint coefficients, and $P'$ is self-adjoint (§V.12). Its solutions have no a priori bound: $\frac{d}{dt}\|X\|^2 = 2\langle P'X, X\rangle$, which vanishes for tangent $X$ but not in general, and one does not know in advance that a solution stays tangent. The formal proof therefore solves instead
$$X' = \Omega(t)X, \qquad \Omega = P'P - PP'$$
⟦Chapter6/Parallel · skewAlong⟧. Its coefficient is skew-adjoint ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · inner_skewAlong_self⟧: $\langle P'Px, x\rangle = \langle Px, P'x\rangle = \langle PP'x, x\rangle$, by self-adjointness of $P'$ and of $P$. On tangent vectors the two equations agree, since $PP'X = PP'PX = 0$ there, so $\Omega X = P'X$.

**Lemma 8.2 (V6.8).** For a smooth curve $c$, a time $t_0$ and $v \in T_{c(t_0)}N$, there is exactly one parallel field $X$ along $c$ with $X(t_0) = v$ ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · exists_unique_isParallel⟧. It is defined for all $t$, and it is smooth ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · IsParallel.contDiff⟧.

*The proof of existence as formalized* ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · exists_isParallel⟧.

1. $\Omega$ is continuous and skew-adjoint, so §V.18 gives $\alpha : \mathbb R \to V$ with $\alpha' = \Omega\alpha$ everywhere and $\alpha(t_0) = v$ ⟦Chapter6/LinearODE · exists_skewLinearODE⟧.
2. *The normal part $U = \alpha - P\alpha$ satisfies $U' = -P'U$.* By the product rule $U' = \Omega\alpha - P'\alpha - P\Omega\alpha$. Since $c'(t)$ is tangent, $PP'P = 0$ (§V.12), so $P\Omega\alpha = PP'P\alpha - P^2P'\alpha = -PP'\alpha$. Hence $U' = P'P\alpha - PP'\alpha - P'\alpha + PP'\alpha = -P'(\alpha - P\alpha)$.
3. $U(t_0) = v - Pv = 0$, since $v$ is tangent. The zero function solves the same linear equation, so $U \equiv 0$ by uniqueness ⟦Chapter6/LinearODE · linearODE_eq⟧. Thus $\alpha = P\alpha$: the solution stays tangent.
4. $\alpha$ is parallel: $D\alpha/dt = P\alpha' = PP'P\alpha - PP'\alpha$, and both terms vanish because $\alpha = P\alpha$ and $PP'P = 0$.

Uniqueness was proved above, among differentiable fields, a stronger statement than Milnor's among smooth ones. Smoothness follows by bootstrapping $X' = P'(t)X$ with $P'$ smooth: if $X$ is $C^n$, then $X'$ is $C^n$, so $X$ is $C^{n+1}$. Milnor states the lemma with initial time $0$; any initial time is allowed here. On a manifold his coordinate equations would also have to be patched from chart to chart along the curve. In the embedded setting there is one equation in $V$.

**Compatibility in Milnor's sense (V6.9).** For parallel fields $X, Y$ along any curve, $\langle X, Y\rangle$ is constant ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · IsParallel.inner_eq⟧. Its derivative is $0$ by Lemma 8.3, and a function with zero derivative is constant. This is Milnor's definition of a connection compatible with the metric (p. 47), proved here for the induced connection. Parallel fields form a vector space: sums ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · IsParallel.add⟧ and scalar multiples ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · IsParallel.const_smul⟧ of parallel fields are parallel.

**Parallel translation (V6.9).** Let $X_v$ be the parallel field along $c$ through $v$ at time $t_0$, chosen once and for all ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · parallelField⟧; formally it is $0$ when there is none, which happens only if $v$ is not tangent or $c$ is not smooth. For tangent $v$ it is parallel with $X_v(t_0) = v$ ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · parallelField_spec⟧, and every parallel field is the chosen one through any of its values ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · IsParallel.eq_parallelField⟧. Translation from $c(t_0)$ to $c(t_1)$ sends $v$ to $X_v(t_1)$ ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · parallelTransportL⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · parallelTransportL_apply⟧.

- It is *linear*: $X_v + X_w$ is parallel with value $v + w$ at $t_0$, so it is $X_{v+w}$ by uniqueness; likewise for scalars.
- It *preserves norms*: $\|X_v(t_1)\|^2 = \|X_v(t_0)\|^2 = \|v\|^2$ by compatibility.
- It is *onto* ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · surjective_parallelTransportL⟧: given $w$ at $t_1$, take $v = X_w(t_0)$, where $X_w$ is the parallel field through $w$ at time $t_1$. By uniqueness $X_v = X_w$, whose value at $t_1$ is $w$.

So parallel translation is a linear isometry $T_{c(t_0)}N \to T_{c(t_1)}N$ ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · parallelTransport⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · parallelTransport_apply⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Normed/Operator/LinearIsometry · LinearIsometryEquiv.ofSurjective⟧. It preserves inner products ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · inner_parallelTransport⟧, is the identity when $t_1 = t_0$ ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · parallelTransport_self⟧, and is transitive ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · parallelTransport_trans⟧: the parallel field through $X_v(t_1)$ at time $t_1$ is $X_v$ itself, so translating to $t_1$ and then to $t_2$ gives $X_v(t_2)$. Milnor uses linearity, surjectivity and transitivity without comment.

### V.20 Parametrized surfaces: Lemmas 8.7 and 9.2

For a map $F$ on $\mathbb R^2$, $\partial F/\partial x = dF(1, 0)$ and $\partial F/\partial y = dF(0, 1)$ ⟦Chapter6/Surface · partialX⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Surface · partialY⟧. They are the derivatives along the horizontal and vertical lines ⟦Chapter6/Surface · hasDerivAt_partialX⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Surface · hasDerivAt_partialY⟧, smooth for smooth $F$ ⟦Chapter6/Surface · contDiff_partialX⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Surface · contDiff_partialY⟧. The mixed partials are second derivatives ⟦Chapter6/Surface · partialY_partialX_eq⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Surface · partialX_partialY_eq⟧, equal by Schwarz ⟦Chapter6/Surface · partialY_partialX⟧. Each partial derivative obeys the product rule for an operator-valued map applied to a vector-valued one ⟦Chapter6/Surface · partialX_clm_apply⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Surface · partialY_clm_apply⟧.

A *parametrized surface* is a map $s : \mathbb R^2 \to N$ with $p \mapsto s(p) \in V$ smooth. It is smooth into $N$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · contMDiff_map⟧, its partial derivatives are tangent ⟦Chapter6/Projection · fderiv_mem_tangentSubspace⟧, the chain rule $d(F \circ s)_p = dF_{s(p)} \circ ds_p$ holds ⟦Chapter6/Projection · hasFDerivAt_comp_map⟧, and smooth maps compose to smooth maps ⟦Chapter6/Projection · contDiff_comp_map⟧. The velocity fields are $\partial s/\partial x$ and $\partial s/\partial y$ (p. 49) ⟦Chapter6/Surface · velX⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Surface · velY⟧, tangent ⟦Chapter6/Surface · velX_mem⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Surface · velY_mem⟧. Milnor's $DW/\partial x$ at $(x, y_0)$ is the covariant derivative of $x \mapsto W(x, y_0)$ along the curve $x \mapsto s(x, y_0)$, and the formal definition is literally that ⟦Chapter6/Surface · covDerivX⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Surface · covDerivY⟧. At a point where $W$ is differentiable it equals $P_{s(p)}(\partial W/\partial x)$ ⟦Chapter6/Surface · covDerivX_eq⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Surface · covDerivY_eq⟧. Milnor defines $DW/\partial x$ one curve at a time, but Lemma 9.2 applies $D/\partial y$ to it, which needs joint smoothness in $(x, y)$. Here it is visible from the formula.

**Lemma 8.7 (V6.15).** $\frac{D}{\partial x}\frac{\partial s}{\partial y} = \frac{D}{\partial y}\frac{\partial s}{\partial x}$ at every point ⟦Chapter6/Surface · covDerivX_velY⟧. Both sides are $P\big(\partial^2s/\partial x\partial y\big)$, by the formula above and Schwarz. Milnor states the lemma for a symmetric connection and proves it by a computation in coordinates. The induced connection is symmetric (§V.15), but the formal proof does not need it, only the symmetry of second derivatives in $V$.

**Lemma 9.2 (V6.19).** For a smooth map $W : \mathbb R^2 \to V$ with $W(p) \in T_{s(p)}N$ for all $p$,
$$\frac{D}{\partial y}\frac{D}{\partial x}W - \frac{D}{\partial x}\frac{D}{\partial y}W = R\Big(\frac{\partial s}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial s}{\partial y}\Big)W$$
at every point, with $R_q(x, y) = D_yD_x - D_xD_y$ the curvature of §V.16 ⟦Chapter6/Surface · covDerivY_covDerivX_sub⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Write $\tilde P(p) = P_{s(p)}$, and $D_x = D_{\partial s/\partial x}$, $D_y = D_{\partial s/\partial y}$ at $s(p)$.

1. $\tilde P$ is smooth, and its partial derivatives at $p$ are $D_x$ and $D_y$, by the chain rule for $s$.
2. $DW/\partial x = \tilde P\,\partial W/\partial x$ and $DW/\partial y = \tilde P\,\partial W/\partial y$ everywhere. These are smooth, so the outer covariant derivatives are again given by the formula.
3. Since $W = \tilde PW$, the product rule gives $\partial W/\partial x = D_xW + \tilde P\,\partial W/\partial x$ at $p$, and likewise for $y$.
4. *Key identity:* for tangent $a, b$ at $s(p)$ and any $w$ with $w = D_aW(p) + P_{s(p)}w$, $P_{s(p)}(D_bw) = D_bD_aW(p)$. Indeed $P D_bP = 0$ kills the second part, and $D_aW(p)$ is normal, $W(p)$ being tangent, so $D_bD_aW(p)$ is tangent and fixed by $P_{s(p)}$.
5. By the product rule, $\frac{D}{\partial y}\big(\tilde P\,\partial W/\partial x\big) = P\big(D_y\,\partial W/\partial x\big) + P\,\partial^2W/\partial y\partial x$. By step 4 with $w = \partial W/\partial x$, the first term is $D_yD_xW$. Symmetrically, $\frac{D}{\partial x}\frac{D}{\partial y}W = D_xD_yW + P\,\partial^2W/\partial x\partial y$. The second-derivative terms cancel by Schwarz, leaving $D_yD_xW - D_xD_yW = R(\partial s/\partial x, \partial s/\partial y)W$.

Lemma 9.2 needs no symmetry of the connection, and the formal proof makes no use of the symmetry of $\mathrm{II}$. Milnor again leaves a computation in local coordinates to the reader; in the embedded setting the computation is steps 3–5.

### V.21 The round sphere

The unit sphere $S^n = \{x \in \mathbb R^{n+1} : \langle x, x\rangle - 1 = 0\}$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter5/HairyBall · Sphere.Sph⟧ is a slice submanifold of $\mathbb R^{n+1}$ with complementary space $\mathbb R^1$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/EulerSphere · Sphere.sphSub⟧. Its points satisfy $\langle q, q\rangle = 1$ ⟦Chapter6/SphereCurvature · inner_self_sph⟧ ⟦Milnor/Chapter5/HairyBall · Sphere.inner_self_eq_one_of_mem⟧, and its tangent space at $q$ is $q^\perp$ ⟦Chapter6/SphereCurvature · mem_tangentSubspace_sph_iff⟧ ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/EulerSphere · Sphere.mem_range_mfd_val_iff⟧, a result of the companion project. Milnor uses the constant curvature of the sphere in §§19–20 without computing it. The formal computation is coordinate-free.

**The projection.** $P_qv = v - \langle q, v\rangle q$ ⟦Chapter6/SphereCurvature · tanProj_sph⟧. The right side is tangent, as $\langle q, v - \langle q, v\rangle q\rangle = 0$, and $v$ minus it, $\langle q, v\rangle q$, is orthogonal to every tangent vector; this characterizes the orthogonal projection ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Projection/Basic · Submodule.eq_starProjection_of_mem_of_inner_eq_zero⟧. In particular $P_qq = 0$ ⟦Chapter6/SphereCurvature · tanProj_sph_self⟧.

**The second fundamental form (V6.25).** For tangent $v, w$ at $q$, $\mathrm{II}_q(v, w) = -\langle v, w\rangle\,q$ ⟦Chapter6/SphereCurvature · secondFundForm_sph⟧.

*Proof.* Use the smooth field $x \mapsto P_xw$, equal to $w$ at $q$ (§V.11). By §V.14, $\mathrm{II}_q(v, w)$ is the normal part of its derivative in the direction $v$. On the sphere this field is $G(x) = w - \langle w, x\rangle x$, an ambient map whose derivative at $q$ is $v \mapsto -\langle w, q\rangle v - \langle w, v\rangle q = -\langle w, v\rangle q$, since $w \perp q$. The normal part of $-\langle w, v\rangle q$ is itself, because $P_qq = 0$.

**The curvature.** For tangent $x, y, z$, $R(x, y)z = \langle x, z\rangle y - \langle y, z\rangle x$, with Milnor's sign ⟦Chapter6/SphereCurvature · curvOp_sph⟧. Both sides are tangent ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvOp_mem⟧. For tangent $w$, the Gauss equation (§V.16) and $\langle q, q\rangle = 1$ give
$$\langle R(x, y)z, w\rangle = \langle x, z\rangle\langle y, w\rangle - \langle x, w\rangle\langle y, z\rangle = \big\langle\langle x, z\rangle y - \langle y, z\rangle x,\ w\big\rangle.$$
So the difference of the two sides is tangent and orthogonal to every tangent vector, hence $0$. On each tangent space the curvature tensor of $S^n$ is therefore the tensor $R_1$ of §V.8 ⟦Chapter6/SphereCurvature · curvTensor_sph⟧. **$S^n$ has constant sectional curvature $1$**, both as $K(x, y) = 1$ for independent tangent vectors ⟦Chapter6/SphereCurvature · sectionalCurvature_sph⟧ and in Milnor's form, $\langle R(A, B)A, B\rangle = 1$ for orthonormal $A, B$ (p. 100) ⟦Chapter6/SphereCurvature · inner_curvTensor_sph_orthonormal⟧. With Milnor's sign the sphere comes out positively curved, as it must.

### V.22 What the formalization taught us

- **The index bound needs no focal points.** Andreotti and Frankel's identity $H(iv, iv) = 2G(v, v) - H(v, v)$ bounds the index by $k$ at every point of a holomorphic chart, critical or not, degenerate or not ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · index_hessian_normSq_sub_holomorphic_le⟧. Milnor's count also needs $p$ and $q$ non-focal and the case $p = q$, which he does not mention.
- **Assertion 1 must be relative.** Focal points are eigenvalues of the second fundamental form relative to the first, not relative to the coordinate inner product. What Assertion 2 needs is the version for a $J$-invariant form ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · nullity_sub_smul_eq_nullity_add_smul⟧, and the vanishing of Milnor's linear terms needs real orthogonality to imply Hermitian orthogonality ⟦Chapter5/IndexBound · inner_eq_zero_of_re_inner_eq_zero⟧.
- **The octahedron needs no linearity**, and its signs, left to the reader, are fourteen instances of three identities ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · octahedron⟧. That the sectional curvature depends only on the plane and determines $R$ is not stated in the book; both are proved ⟦Chapter6/CurvatureAlgebra · IsCurvatureTensor.ext_of_orthonormal⟧.
- **One smooth operator field replaces the Christoffel symbols.** All smoothness in §8 reduces to that of $q \mapsto P_q$, which holds because the projection onto the range of an injective operator is $A(A^{\mathsf T}A)^{-1}A^{\mathsf T}$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · contMDiff_tanProj⟧.
- **Symmetry of the connection is symmetry of $\mathrm{II}$**, a second-order property of the embedding that Part II never mentions ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · secondFundForm_comm⟧. That the ambient bracket is tangent and acts on functions as Milnor's bracket also needs proof ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · bracket_mem⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · dN_bracket⟧.
- **Global existence of parallel fields needs another equation.** Milnor's appeal to linearity hides a global existence theorem, and $X' = P'X$ has no a priori bound. The skew equation $X' = (P'P - PP')X$ preserves norms, and a second application of uniqueness keeps its solutions tangent ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · exists_isParallel⟧.
- **The order of the lemmas changes.** Lemma 8.3 is a direct computation and comes before parallel transport ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · hasDerivAt_inner_covDeriv⟧. Lemma 8.1 needs global fields instead of coordinate fields ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · IsCovDerivAlong.eq_sum⟧.
- **Milnor's "similar computations" hide different ones.** Tensoriality of $R$ in $Z$ uses the symmetry of second derivatives ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvature_eq⟧. Lemma 9.3 (2) and (3) need no extensions with vanishing brackets. Lemmas 8.7 and 9.2 need $DW/\partial x$ jointly smooth, which the formula $P(\partial W/\partial x)$ provides ⟦Chapter6/Surface · covDerivX_eq⟧.
- **Signs.** Milnor's $R$ is the negative of Nomizu's ⟦Chapter6/Connection · milnorCurvature_eq_neg⟧. The sectional curvature is the same number in both conventions, and with Milnor's sign the Gauss equation makes the unit sphere curvature $+1$ ⟦Chapter6/SphereCurvature · inner_curvTensor_sph_orthonormal⟧.
