## Part I. Critical points and the lemma of Morse (Milnor §§1–2)

Milnor's second section fixes the vocabulary of the whole book: critical points, the Hessian $f_{**}$, its index and nullity, Hadamard's lemma (Lemma 2.1), the lemma of Morse (Lemma 2.2), the isolation of non-degenerate critical points (Corollary 2.3), five examples of degenerate critical points, and the one-parameter group generated by a compactly supported vector field (Lemma 2.4). The formal proof establishes all of this in three settings: in a real normed space $E$, which is Milnor's situation once coordinates have been chosen; on an abstract smooth manifold without boundary modelled on $E$, with no Hausdorff or countability hypothesis; and for functions $F|N$ on a slice submanifold $N$ of a Euclidean space, the setting of most later parts. The torus of §1 is not told here: its Lean file uses Theorem 3.1, and it appears in Part III.

Two proofs take a different road from Milnor's. The lemma of Morse is not proved by his induction, which completes one square at a time. A single application of the inverse function theorem, in the space of linear maps of $E$, produces a smooth family of linear maps that turns the quadratic form with variable coefficients into a constant one, and Sylvester's law of inertia, from Mathlib, finishes. The smooth dependence of solutions of an ordinary differential equation on the initial point, which Milnor quotes as well known and which Mathlib does not have, is proved by the inverse function theorem in the Banach space $C([0,1], E)$. The global flow itself comes from the Picard–Lindelöf theorem on time intervals of any length, which a bounded field allows, and this replaces Milnor's iteration of $\varphi_{\varepsilon_0/2}$. Smaller departures are flagged where they occur. Hadamard's lemma to second order comes from Taylor's formula, Corollary 2.3 is proved without the lemma of Morse, and the Hessian is defined in a chart, its independence of the chart being a theorem.

**Map.**

- `Chapter1/Nondegenerate`: critical points, the Hessian, null space, nullity and index in a normed space; linear changes of variables; the second-order chain rule at a critical point; the matrix criterion; Corollary 2.3.
- `Chapter1/Hadamard`: parametric integrals on an open set; Lemma 2.1, coordinate free, in coordinates, and to second order.
- `Chapter1/SquareRoot`: the inverse function theorem with an inverse smooth on its whole domain; the smooth square root of a family of bilinear forms.
- `Chapter1/MorseLemma`: the normal form and its index (Sylvester); diagonalization with the negative squares first; Lemma 2.2, coordinate free and in Milnor's form; the index is forced by the normal form.
- `Chapter1/Examples`: Milnor's examples (a), (c), (d), (e).
- `Chapter1/Manifold`: the same notions on a manifold, through the local representative; smooth coordinate systems and the independence of the chart.
- `Chapter1/ManifoldMorse`: Corollary 2.3 on a manifold, the critical set, finiteness in compact sets; Lemma 2.2 on a manifold and its converse.
- `Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold`: functions $F|N$ on a slice submanifold $N \subseteq V$; critical points through the gradient; Milnor's formulas of pp. 35–36 for $L_p$.

### I.1 Conventions

The three settings share one set of conventions; Part 0 recalls them (§0.1, §0.2, §0.10), and they are repeated here only as far as the proofs below need them.

*Normed spaces.* $E$ is a real normed space, finite-dimensional wherever a dimension is counted; the formal statements say so each time. In statements written in coordinates, Milnor's $\mathbb R^n$ is the space of functions $\{0, \dots, n-1\} \to \mathbb R$ with the maximum norm; only its linear and smooth structure matters. The formal coordinates are numbered from $0$, so Milnor's negative squares $(y^1)^2, \dots, (y^\lambda)^2$ are those with index $i < \lambda$. The derivative $df_x$ is Mathlib's, the continuous linear map $L$ with $f(x + h) = f(x) + Lh + o(\|h\|)$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/FDeriv/Defs · HasFDerivAt⟧, and by convention it is $0$ where $f$ is not differentiable ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/FDeriv/Defs · fderiv⟧. The second derivative $d^2f_x$ is the derivative of $x \mapsto df_x$, a continuous bilinear form, written $d^2f_x(v, w)$. Being $C^n$ at $x$ is Mathlib's notion ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/ContDiff/Defs · ContDiff⟧. For a finite order it propagates to a neighbourhood: a map that is $C^2$ at $x$ is $C^2$ at every point near $x$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/ContDiff/Defs · ContDiffAt.eventually⟧. For the order $\infty$ it does not, which is why the inverse function theorem of §I.7 asks for smoothness on an open set.

*Manifolds.* A manifold $M$ is a charted space modelled on $E$ itself, through the identity model, which has no boundary ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/ChartedSpace · ChartedSpace⟧. Where the atlas is used it is a smooth manifold ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/IsManifold/Basic · IsManifold⟧. No Hausdorff or second countability hypothesis is ever assumed in this Part. Each point $p$ has a preferred chart ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/ChartedSpace · chartAt⟧ and the extended chart $\kappa_p$ built from it ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/IsManifold/ExtChartAt · extChartAt⟧. The tangent space $T_pM$ is, by definition, $E$ ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/IsManifold/Basic · TangentSpace⟧. The derivative $df_p$ of $f : M \to \mathbb R$ is the derivative at $\kappa_p(p)$ of $f$ read in the charts, and $0$ where $f$ is not differentiable ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/MFDeriv/Defs · mfderiv⟧. Because the formal statements must name the model space, the project's definitions on $M$ take $E$ as an explicit argument.

*Submanifolds.* The submanifolds of a Euclidean space are the slice submanifolds of the companion project (§0.1). A set $N \subseteq V$ is one, modelled on $Z$ with complement $W$, if every point of $N$ lies in the domain of a *slice chart*: a diffeomorphism $\sigma$ of an open subset of $V$ onto an open subset of $Z \times W$ which carries the points of $N$ in its domain exactly onto $Z \times \{0\}$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · SliceChart⟧ ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · IsSliceSubmanifold⟧. Such an $N$ is a smooth manifold modelled on $Z$, whose charts are the maps $q \mapsto \sigma(q)_Z$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · SliceChart.toChart⟧ ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · IsSliceSubmanifold.chartedSpace⟧ ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · IsSliceSubmanifold.isManifold⟧, and the inclusion $N \to V$ is smooth ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · IsSliceSubmanifold.contMDiff_val⟧.

### I.2 Critical points, the Hessian, the index and the nullity (Milnor pp. 4–5)

Milnor defines these notions on a manifold and computes them in coordinates. The formal text starts in coordinates, on a normed space $E$, and transports the definitions to manifolds in §I.13. The definitions are collected in §0.2.

**Definitions (V1.3).** Let $f : E \to \mathbb R$ and $p \in E$.

- $p$ is a *critical point* of $f$ if $f$ is differentiable at $p$ with derivative $0$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · IsCriticalPoint⟧. Then $df_p = 0$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · IsCriticalPoint.fderiv_eq⟧, and for $f$ differentiable at $p$ the converse holds ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · isCriticalPoint_iff⟧. A critical point stays critical under a differentiable change of variables, by the chain rule ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · IsCriticalPoint.comp⟧.
- The *Hessian* of $f$ at $p$ is the second derivative $d^2 f_p$, a continuous bilinear form on $E$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · hessian⟧. Its value $d^2f_p(v, w)$ is the derivative at $p$, in the direction $v$, of $x \mapsto df_x(w)$. That is Milnor's $f_{**}(v, w) = \tilde v_p(\tilde w(f))$ for the constant extensions $\tilde v = v$, $\tilde w = w$. The formal Hessian is defined at every point and is used only at critical points.
- For a continuous bilinear form $H$ on $E$: its *quadratic form* is $Q_H(v) = H(v, v)$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · quadForm⟧; its *null space* is $\{v : H(v, w) = 0 \text{ for all } w\}$, the kernel of $v \mapsto H(v, \cdot)$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · nullSpace⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · mem_nullSpace⟧; its *nullity* is the dimension of the null space ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · nullity⟧; $H$ is *non-degenerate* if the null space is $0$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · IsNondegenerate⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · isNondegenerate_iff⟧.
- The *index* of $H$ is Mathlib's negative index of inertia of $Q_H$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · index⟧ ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/QuadraticForm/Signature · sigNeg⟧. Mathlib defines it as the positive index of $-Q_H$. The positive index of a quadratic form $Q$ is the largest $r \le \dim E$ for which some $r$-dimensional subspace carries $Q$ positive definite ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/QuadraticForm/Signature · sigPos⟧. On an infinite-dimensional space the formal dimension is $0$, so the index is $0$ there; this is a junk value, and every statement about indices assumes finite dimension.
- A *non-degenerate critical point* is a critical point at which $d^2f_p$ is non-degenerate ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · IsNondegenerateCriticalPoint⟧.

Nothing in these definitions asks $H$ to be symmetric. A Hessian is symmetric wherever $f$ is $C^2$, by the symmetry of second derivatives ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · hessian_symm⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/FDeriv/Symmetric · ContDiffAt.isSymmSndFDerivAt⟧. Milnor proves symmetry differently, through $\tilde v_p(\tilde w(f)) - \tilde w_p(\tilde v(f)) = [\tilde v, \tilde w]_p(f) = 0$, which uses that $p$ is critical. In the formal text criticality is not needed for symmetry. It is needed for the independence of the coordinates, and §I.4 shows exactly where it enters. Two more facts are used throughout. The Hessian depends only on the germ of $f$ at $p$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · hessian_congr⟧. And for a continuous bilinear form $B$, the function $x \mapsto B(x, x)$ has Hessian $(v, w) \mapsto B(v, w) + B(w, v)$ at every point ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · hessian_bilinear⟧. The proof: by the product rule for bilinear maps ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/FDeriv/Bilinear · ContinuousLinearMap.hasFDerivAt_of_bilinear⟧, the derivative of $x \mapsto B(x, x)$ at $x$ is $v \mapsto B(x, v) + B(v, x)$. As a function of $x$ this is a continuous linear map, so its derivative is the map itself.

**Theorem (the index in Milnor's words).** Let $E$ be finite-dimensional and $H$ a continuous bilinear form on $E$. The index of $H$ is the largest $k$ such that some $k$-dimensional subspace $W$ has $H(v, v) < 0$ for every $v \in W \setminus \{0\}$. ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · index_isGreatest⟧

*The proof as formalized.* Mathlib's defining property of the negative index ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/QuadraticForm/Signature · sigNeg_isGreatest⟧ makes it the largest dimension of a subspace $W$ on which the restriction of $-Q_H$ is positive definite. For a subspace $W$ this restriction is positive definite exactly when $H(v, v) < 0$ for all non-zero $v \in W$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · negDef_iff_posDef_restrict⟧. So the two sets of dimensions coincide. Two consequences are used later: some negative definite subspace has dimension equal to the index ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · exists_negDef_finrank_eq_index⟧, and every negative definite subspace has dimension at most the index ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · finrank_le_index_of_negDef⟧. The index never exceeds $\dim E$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · index_le_finrank⟧ ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/QuadraticForm/Signature · sigPos_le_finrank⟧.

Milnor's remark that a critical point is non-degenerate exactly when $f_{**}$ has nullity $0$ is, for finite-dimensional $E$, the fact that a subspace is $0$ exactly when its dimension is $0$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · isNondegenerate_iff_nullity_eq_zero⟧.

### I.3 Linear changes of variables, and the matrix criterion

Three invariance facts are used over and over, two for a linear change of variables and one for multiplication by a scalar. Let $L : E' \to E$ be a linear isomorphism and $H$ a continuous bilinear form on $E$. Its pull-back is $H(L\cdot, L\cdot)$.

- *Index.* $L$ is an isometry from the quadratic form of the pull-back to $Q_H$, because $Q_H(Lv) = H(Lv, Lv)$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · quadForm_bilinearComp_equivalent⟧. Mathlib's negative index is invariant under such equivalences ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/QuadraticForm/Signature · QuadraticMap.Equivalent.sigNeg_eq⟧, since the isometry carries negative definite subspaces to negative definite subspaces of the same dimension. So the pull-back has the same index as $H$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · index_bilinearComp⟧.
- *Null space and nullity.* The null space of the pull-back is the preimage under $L$ of the null space of $H$: if $H(Lv, Lw) = 0$ for all $w$, then $H(Lv, u) = 0$ for all $u$, taking $w = L^{-1}u$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · nullSpace_bilinearComp⟧. A linear isomorphism preserves the dimension of subspaces, so the two forms have the same nullity ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · nullity_bilinearComp⟧ and are non-degenerate together ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · isNondegenerate_bilinearComp_iff⟧.
- *Scalars.* For $c > 0$, multiplication by $\sqrt c$ is an isometry from $Q_{cH}$ to $Q_H$, since $H(\sqrt c\, v, \sqrt c\, v) = c\,H(v, v)$. Hence $cH$ and $H$ have the same index ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · index_smul⟧. For $c \ne 0$, $cH(v, w) = 0$ exactly when $H(v, w) = 0$, so $cH$ and $H$ are non-degenerate together ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · isNondegenerate_smul_iff⟧. These two facts absorb the factors $2$ and $\tfrac12$ that separate the Hessian from the forms of the lemma of Morse.

**Theorem (Milnor's matrix criterion, p. 4).** A symmetric continuous bilinear form $H$ on $\mathbb R^n$ is non-degenerate if and only if the matrix $\big(H(e_i, e_j)\big)$ has non-zero determinant. Consequently, if $f$ is $C^2$ at $p$, then $d^2f_p$ is non-degenerate if and only if $\det\big(\partial^2 f/\partial x^i \partial x^j(p)\big) \ne 0$. ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · isNondegenerate_iff_det_ne_zero⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · isNondegenerate_hessian_iff_det_ne_zero⟧

*The proof as formalized.* View $H$ as a bilinear form in Mathlib's sense. Its matrix in the standard basis is $\big(H(e_i, e_j)\big)$, by the definition of that matrix. Mathlib proves that a bilinear form on a finite-dimensional space is non-degenerate on both sides exactly when the determinant of its matrix is non-zero ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/Matrix/BilinearForm · LinearMap.BilinForm.nondegenerate_iff_det_ne_zero⟧. The project's non-degeneracy is literally non-degeneracy on the left: $H(v, \cdot) = 0$ forces $v = 0$. Symmetry turns it into non-degeneracy on the right. For a Hessian the symmetry is that of §I.2. Milnor *defines* a non-degenerate critical point through this matrix; here it is a theorem.

### I.4 The second-order chain rule, and the independence of the coordinates

Milnor says that one can check directly that non-degeneracy does not depend on the coordinates (p. 4), and later he computes $f_{**}$ in whatever coordinates are at hand. The check is the chain rule for second derivatives, and its first-order term is where criticality enters.

**Theorem (the second-order chain rule).** Let $\psi : E_1 \to E_2$ be $C^2$ at $q$ and $f : E_2 \to \mathbb R$ be $C^2$ at $\psi(q)$. Then for all $v, w \in E_1$
$$
d^2(f \circ \psi)_q(v, w) = d^2f_{\psi(q)}\big(d\psi_q v,\ d\psi_q w\big) + df_{\psi(q)}\big(d^2\psi_q(v, w)\big).
$$
If $df_{\psi(q)} = 0$, the last term vanishes:
$$
d^2(f \circ \psi)_q(v, w) = d^2f_{\psi(q)}\big(d\psi_q v,\ d\psi_q w\big).
$$
⟦Chapter1/Manifold · hessian_comp_apply⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · hessian_comp⟧

*The proof as formalized.* The project proves the two statements separately, the second in `Chapter1/Nondegenerate` and the first, which only §I.17 needs, in `Chapter1/Manifold`. The proofs are the same.

1. A finite order of smoothness propagates to a neighbourhood, so $\psi$ is $C^2$ at every point near $q$, and, $\psi$ being continuous at $q$, $f$ is $C^2$ at $\psi(x)$ for every $x$ near $q$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/ContDiff/Defs · ContDiffAt.eventually⟧.
2. At each such $x$ the first-order chain rule gives $d(f \circ \psi)_x = df_{\psi(x)} \circ d\psi_x$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/FDeriv/Comp · fderiv_comp⟧. The Hessian of $f \circ \psi$ at $q$ depends only on the germ of $x \mapsto d(f \circ \psi)_x$, so it is the derivative at $q$ of $x \mapsto df_{\psi(x)} \circ d\psi_x$.
3. This map composes two operator-valued maps. The first, $x \mapsto df_{\psi(x)}$, has derivative $v \mapsto d^2f_{\psi(q)}(d\psi_q v)$ at $q$, by the chain rule, since $f$ is $C^2$ at $\psi(q)$. The second, $x \mapsto d\psi_x$, has derivative $d^2\psi_q$. The product rule for the composition of operators ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/FDeriv/CompCLM · HasFDerivAt.clm_comp⟧ gives the derivative
$$
v \mapsto d^2f_{\psi(q)}(d\psi_q v) \circ d\psi_q + df_{\psi(q)} \circ d^2\psi_q(v),
$$
and evaluating at $w$ gives the formula.
4. When $df_{\psi(q)} = 0$ the second term is zero.

**Corollary (non-degeneracy and index do not depend on the coordinates).** If moreover $d\psi_q$ is a linear isomorphism $L$ and $df_{\psi(q)} = 0$, then $d^2(f \circ \psi)_q$ is the pull-back of $d^2f_{\psi(q)}$ by $L$. Hence the two are non-degenerate together and have the same index. ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · isNondegenerate_hessian_comp_iff⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · index_hessian_comp⟧

This is §I.3 applied to the displayed identity.

*Where the formal proof departs from Milnor.* Milnor's Hessian is intrinsic: $f_{**}(v, w) = \tilde v_p(\tilde w(f))$, for vector fields $\tilde v$, $\tilde w$ extending $v$ and $w$. He shows that it is symmetric and independent of the extensions by the bracket identity. The formal Hessian is defined in coordinates, and the two facts become theorems of a different kind. Symmetry is the symmetry of second derivatives (§I.2). Independence of the extensions is replaced by independence of the coordinate system, which is the identity above. The definition through extensions is not formalized. The only use of criticality is to kill the term $df_{\psi(q)}(d^2\psi_q(v, w))$, and only $C^2$ at the point is needed. The first formula, with that term kept, is not idle. In §6 Milnor computes the Hessian of the squared distance from a point, restricted to a submanifold, and there the extra term is the second fundamental form (§I.17).

### I.5 Lemma 2.1: Hadamard's lemma

**Lemma 2.1 (Milnor, p. 5).** Let $f$ be $C^\infty$ on a convex neighbourhood $V$ of $0$ in $\mathbb R^n$, with $f(0) = 0$. Then $f(x) = \sum_i x_i\, g_i(x)$ on $V$ for some $C^\infty$ functions $g_i$ on $V$ with $g_i(0) = \partial f/\partial x_i(0)$.

Milnor writes $f(x) = \int_0^1 \frac{d}{dt} f(tx)\, dt$ and takes $g_i(x) = \int_0^1 \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(tx)\, dt$. That the $g_i$ are smooth is not argued. The companion project proved Hadamard's lemma for a smooth map defined on the whole space, $f(x) = L(x)\,x$ with $L(x) = \int_0^1 df_{tx}\, dt$ smooth and $L(0) = df_0$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/Hadamard · exists_hadamard⟧, on top of a theorem on parametric integrals with a globally $C^n$ integrand ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/Hadamard · contDiff_intervalIntegral⟧ ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/Hadamard · contDiff_intervalIntegral_infty⟧. Milnor's lemma is local. The integrand $(x, t) \mapsto \partial f/\partial x_i(tx)$ exists only on the open set $\{(x, t) : tx \in V\}$, so the project proves local versions of both theorems by the same method.

**A tube around a segment.** Let $h : E \times \mathbb R \to G$, with $G$ a normed group, be continuous on an open set $U$ containing $\{x_0\} \times [0, 1]$. Then there are an $M$, an open $s \ni x_0$ and an open $w \supseteq [0, 1]$ with $s \times w \subseteq U$ and $\|h\| \le M$ on $s \times w$. ⟦Chapter1/Hadamard · exists_tube_bound⟧

*Proof.* The segment $\{x_0\} \times [0, 1]$ is compact and lies in $U$, so $\|h\|$ has a bound $M_0$ on it. The set of points of $U$ where $\|h\| < M_0 + 1$ is open, since $h$ is continuous on the open set $U$, and it contains the segment. The generalized tube lemma ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Compactness/Compact · generalized_tube_lemma⟧ fits a product $s \times w$ of open sets between the product of compact sets $\{x_0\} \times [0, 1]$ and that open set. Take $M = M_0 + 1$.

**Theorem (parametric integrals on an open set).** Let $F$ be a complete normed space, $U \subseteq E \times \mathbb R$ open, and $h : E \times \mathbb R \to F$ of class $C^n$ on $U$, with $n$ finite. Let $V \subseteq E$ be open with $V \times [0, 1] \subseteq U$. Then $x \mapsto \int_0^1 h(x, t)\, dt$ is $C^n$ on $V$, and the same holds with $C^\infty$ in place of $C^n$. ⟦Chapter1/Hadamard · contDiffOn_intervalIntegral⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Hadamard · contDiffOn_intervalIntegral_infty⟧

*The proof as formalized* is an induction on $n$, stated for all complete targets at once ⟦Chapter1/Hadamard · contDiffOn_intervalIntegral_aux⟧.

1. *$n = 0$.* Fix $x_0 \in V$ and a tube $s \times w$ around $\{x_0\} \times [0, 1]$ on which $\|h\| \le M$. For $x \in s$ the integrand $t \mapsto h(x, t)$ is continuous on $[0, 1]$ and bounded by $M$. For each $t$, $x \mapsto h(x, t)$ is continuous at $x_0$, because $h$ is continuous at the point $(x_0, t)$ of the open set $U$. Dominated convergence with the constant bound $M$ ⟦Mathlib/MeasureTheory/Integral/DominatedConvergence · intervalIntegral.continuousAt_of_dominated_interval⟧ makes the integral continuous at $x_0$.
2. *From $n$ to $n + 1$.* The partial derivative in $x$, $D(x, t) = dh_{(x,t)} \circ \iota$ with $\iota(v) = (v, 0)$, is $C^n$ on $U$, being built from the derivative of a $C^{n+1}$ map on an open set. At $x_0 \in V$ a tube bounds $\|D\|$ by some $M$, and Mathlib's differentiation under the integral sign ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/ParametricIntervalIntegral · intervalIntegral.hasFDerivAt_integral_of_dominated_of_fderiv_le⟧ shows that $x \mapsto \int_0^1 h(x, t)\, dt$ is differentiable at $x_0$ with derivative $\int_0^1 D(x_0, t)\, dt$. So the integral is differentiable on $V$, and its derivative is $x \mapsto \int_0^1 D(x, t)\, dt$. That map is $C^n$ on $V$ by the induction hypothesis applied to $D$, whose target is now the space of linear maps $E \to F$. A map on an open set is $C^{n+1}$ exactly when it is differentiable with a $C^n$ derivative ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/ContDiff/Defs · contDiffOn_succ_iff_fderiv_of_isOpen⟧.
3. *Universes.* The induction replaces the target $G$ by the space of linear maps $E \to G$ at each step, so it must range over all targets of one size. It is stated for targets in the universe $\max(u, v)$, where $u$ and $v$ are the universes of $E$ and $F$; that universe contains the space of linear maps $E \to G$ whenever it contains $G$. The theorem for an arbitrary $F$ lifts $F$ into that universe by a continuous linear equivalence, applies the induction, and comes back, commuting the equivalence with the integral ⟦Mathlib/MeasureTheory/Integral/IntervalIntegral/Basic · ContinuousLinearMap.intervalIntegral_comp_comm⟧. This is the companion's device, and the size condition has no mathematical content.
4. *$C^\infty$* is $C^n$ for every finite $n$.

The domination is on a tube $s \times w$, not on a compact neighbourhood of $x_0$, so $E$ need not be locally compact; it is any real normed space.

**Corollary (the integrals of Hadamard's lemma).** Let $V \subseteq E$ be open and star-shaped about $0$, $g$ smooth on $V$ with values in a complete space, and $a : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ smooth. Then $x \mapsto \int_0^1 a(t)\, g(tx)\, dt$ is smooth on $V$. ⟦Chapter1/Hadamard · contDiffOn_integral_smul_comp⟧

*Proof.* Apply the theorem to $h(x, t) = a(t)\, g(tx)$ on $U = \{(x, t) : tx \in V\}$, open as the preimage of $V$ under the continuous map $(x, t) \mapsto tx$. Star-shapedness gives $V \times [0, 1] \subseteq U$, and $h$ is smooth on $U$ as a product and composite of smooth maps.

**Theorem (Lemma 2.1, coordinate free; V1.7).** Let $V \subseteq E$ be open and star-shaped about $0$, and $f : E \to F$ smooth on $V$ with $f(0) = 0$, $F$ complete. Then $f(x) = L(x)\,x$ for $x \in V$, where $L(x) = \int_0^1 df_{tx}\, dt$ is smooth on $V$ and $L(0) = df_0$. ⟦Chapter1/Hadamard · exists_hadamard_on⟧

*The proof as formalized.*

1. $df$ is smooth on the open set $V$, so $L$ is smooth on $V$ by the corollary with $a \equiv 1$.
2. Let $x \in V$. The segment $\{tx : 0 \le t \le 1\}$ lies in $V$, so $f$ is $C^1$ at each of its points. Taylor's formula with integral remainder at order $0$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/TaylorIntegral · map_add_eq_sum_add_integral_iteratedFDeriv⟧ is then the fundamental theorem of calculus along the segment: $f(x) = f(0) + \int_0^1 df_{tx}(x)\, dt$.
3. The integrand $t \mapsto df_{tx}$ is continuous on $[0, 1]$, hence integrable, and evaluation at $x$ is a continuous linear map, which commutes with the integral ⟦Mathlib/MeasureTheory/Integral/IntervalIntegral/Basic · ContinuousLinearMap.intervalIntegral_comp_comm⟧. With $f(0) = 0$ this gives $f(x) = L(x)\,x$.
4. $L(0) = \int_0^1 df_0\, dt = df_0$.

**Theorem (Lemma 2.1 as Milnor states it).** Let $V \subseteq \mathbb R^n$ be open and convex with $0 \in V$, and $f$ smooth on $V$ with $f(0) = 0$. Then $f(x) = \sum_i x_i\, g_i(x)$ on $V$ for functions $g_i$ smooth on $V$ with $g_i(0) = \partial f/\partial x_i(0)$, the derivative $df_0$ applied to the $i$-th basis vector. ⟦Chapter1/Hadamard · exists_hadamard_coord⟧

*Proof.* A convex set containing $0$ is star-shaped about $0$. Put $g_i(x) = L(x)\,e_i$, smooth since evaluation at $e_i$ is linear. Writing $x = \sum_i x_i e_i$, $f(x) = L(x)\,x = \sum_i x_i\, L(x)\,e_i = \sum_i x_i\, g_i(x)$, and $g_i(0) = df_0(e_i)$.

*Hidden hypothesis, and a generalization.* The neighbourhood $V$ must be open, or at least the statement must be read on an open set; a convex neighbourhood of $0$ contains an open convex one, its interior, so nothing is lost. Only star-shapedness about $0$ is used, the source may be any normed space, and the target any complete normed space.

**Theorem (Hadamard's lemma to second order).** Let $V$, $f$ be as in the coordinate-free form, and assume also $df_0 = 0$. Then $f(x) = B(x)(x, x)$ for $x \in V$, where
$$
B(x) = \int_0^1 (1 - t)\, d^2f_{tx}\, dt
$$
is smooth on $V$, each $B(x)$ with $x \in V$ is a symmetric bilinear map, and $B(0) = \tfrac12\, d^2f_0$. ⟦Chapter1/Hadamard · exists_hadamard_two_on⟧

*The proof as formalized.*

1. $d^2f$ is smooth on $V$, so $B$ is smooth on $V$ by the corollary with $a(t) = 1 - t$.
2. Evaluating $B(x)$ at $u$ and then at $w$ commutes with the integral twice: $B(x)(u, w) = \int_0^1 (1 - t)\, d^2f_{tx}(u, w)\, dt$.
3. $f$ is $C^2$ along the segment from $0$ to $x$, and Taylor's formula at order $1$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/TaylorIntegral · map_add_eq_sum_add_integral_iteratedFDeriv⟧ gives $f(x) = f(0) + df_0(x) + \int_0^1 (1 - t)\, d^2f_{tx}(x, x)\, dt$. The first two terms vanish, so $f(x) = B(x)(x, x)$.
4. For $t \in [0, 1]$, $f$ is smooth at $tx \in V$, so $d^2f_{tx}$ is symmetric ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/FDeriv/Symmetric · ContDiffAt.isSymmSndFDerivAt⟧. The integrands of $B(x)(u, w)$ and $B(x)(w, u)$ therefore agree.
5. $B(0) = \big(\int_0^1 (1 - t)\, dt\big)\, d^2f_0 = \tfrac12\, d^2f_0$.

*Where the formal proof departs from Milnor.* On p. 6 Milnor applies Lemma 2.1 twice, obtaining $f = \sum_{i,j} x_i x_j h_{ij}$. He symmetrizes, $H_{ij} = \tfrac12(h_{ij} + h_{ji})$, and asserts without proof that $\big(H_{ij}(0)\big) = \big(\tfrac12\, \partial^2 f/\partial x^i \partial x^j(0)\big)$. That follows by differentiating $f = \sum x_i x_j H_{ij}$ twice at $0$, but he does not say so. Taylor's formula produces a coefficient that is symmetric from the start and gives its value at $0$ in one line. The two coefficients agree: writing out Milnor's two integrals and changing variables gives $h_{ij}(x) = \int_0^1 (1 - t)\, \partial_i\partial_j f(tx)\, dt$.

### I.6 The index through Sylvester's law of inertia

In the first paragraph of his proof of Lemma 2.2 (p. 6) Milnor shows that a normal form $f(p) - \sum_{i \le \lambda}(z^i)^2 + \sum_{i > \lambda}(z^i)^2$ forces $\lambda$ to be the index. In the coordinates $z$ the matrix of $f_{**}$ is $\operatorname{diag}(-2, \dots, -2, 2, \dots, 2)$. A negative definite subspace of dimension greater than $\lambda$ would meet the positive definite span of the last $n - \lambda$ coordinate vectors. This is the uniqueness half of Sylvester's law of inertia, and Mathlib has it.

**Definitions.** For $k \le n$, the *Morse signs* are $s_i = -1$ for $i < k$ and $s_i = +1$ otherwise ⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · morseSign⟧. The *diagonal form* with weights $s$ is $D_s(y, z) = \sum_i s_i\, y_i z_i$ on $\mathbb R^n$ ⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · diagForm⟧ ⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · diagForm_apply⟧. It is symmetric ⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · diagForm_symm⟧, and with the Morse signs it is Milnor's normal form on the diagonal: $D(y, y) = -\sum_{i < k} y_i^2 + \sum_{i \ge k} y_i^2$ ⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · diagForm_morseSign_apply_self⟧. Its quadratic form is Mathlib's weighted sum of squares $\sum_i s_i y_i^2$ ⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · quadForm_diagForm⟧ ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/QuadraticForm/Basic · QuadraticMap.weightedSumSquares⟧.

**Theorem (the index of the normal form).** For $k \le n$, the diagonal form with $k$ minus signs on $\mathbb R^n$ has index $k$. ⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · index_diagForm_morseSign⟧

*The proof as formalized.* The index is the negative index of the weighted sum of squares, which Mathlib computes as the number of negative weights ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/QuadraticForm/Signature · QuadraticForm.sigNeg_weightedSumSquares⟧. Here that is the number of $i < k$, namely $k$. Mathlib's proof is Milnor's argument, made for the positive index and transferred to the negative one by changing the signs of the weights. The coordinate span of the positive weights is positive definite, which bounds the positive index from below. For the bound from above, a positive definite subspace and a negative semidefinite subspace meet only in $0$, so their dimensions add up to at most $n$ ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/QuadraticForm/Signature · QuadraticForm.sigPos_add_finrank_le_of_nonpos⟧.

**Theorem (diagonalization, negative squares first).** Let $E$ be finite-dimensional, $n = \dim E$, and $H$ a symmetric non-degenerate continuous bilinear form on $E$ of index $\lambda$. There is a linear isomorphism $e : E \to \mathbb R^n$ with
$$
H(v, v) = -\sum_{i < \lambda} (ev)_i^2 + \sum_{i \ge \lambda} (ev)_i^2 \qquad \text{for all } v.
$$
⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · exists_linearEquiv_diagForm⟧

*The proof as formalized.*

1. The symmetric bilinear form associated with $Q_H$ is $H$ itself, because $H$ is symmetric ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/QuadraticForm/Basic · QuadraticMap.associated_left_inverse⟧. So the non-degeneracy of $H$ is that of the associated form.
2. The existence half of Sylvester's law, in Mathlib ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/QuadraticForm/Real · QuadraticForm.equivalent_one_neg_one_weighted_sum_squared⟧, gives weights $w_i \in \{-1, +1\}$ and an isometric linear isomorphism $e_0$ from $(E, Q_H)$ to $(\mathbb R^n, \sum_i w_i y_i^2)$. Non-degeneracy is what excludes the weight $0$.
3. The uniqueness half ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/QuadraticForm/Signature · QuadraticForm.sigNeg_of_equiv_weightedSumSquares⟧ identifies the number of negative weights: it is $\lambda$.
4. Let $k = \#\{j : w_j < 0\}$. A permutation $\sigma$ of the indices brings the negative weights to the front: $w_{\sigma(i)} < 0$ exactly when $i < k$ ⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · exists_perm_neg_first⟧. It is built by matching the indices $i < k$ with the indices of negative weight, and the remaining indices with the remaining ones, both pairs of sets having equal cardinality.
5. Then $w_{\sigma(i)}$ is the $i$-th Morse sign for every $i$. Put $(ev)_i = (e_0 v)_{\sigma(i)}$. Reindexing the sum by $\sigma$ gives $H(v, v) = \sum_i w_i (e_0 v)_i^2 = \sum_i w_{\sigma(i)} (ev)_i^2$, the normal form.

Milnor cites Birkhoff–MacLane for the diagonalization of a quadratic form, and his induction imitates it with smooth coefficients. In the formal proof the linear algebra is used once, at the end, on the constant form $d^2f_p$.

### I.7 The inverse function theorem with a smooth inverse

Mathlib's inverse function theorem ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/InverseFunctionTheorem/ContDiff · ContDiffAt.toOpenPartialHomeomorph⟧ produces a local inverse that is known to be $C^n$ only at the image of the base point. A coordinate system must be smooth on its whole domain, so the project proves a strengthened form, which this Part uses twice.

**Theorem.** Let $E$ be complete, $f : E \to F$ of class $C^n$ ($n \ne 0$) on an open set $U \ni a$, and suppose $f$ has an invertible derivative at $a$. Then $f$ restricts to an open partial homeomorphism $\Phi$ with $a$ in its domain, its domain inside $U$, $\Phi$ of class $C^n$ on its domain and $\Phi^{-1}$ of class $C^n$ on its image. ⟦Chapter1/SquareRoot · exists_openPartialHomeomorph_of_contDiffOn⟧

*The proof as formalized.*

1. Let $\Phi_0$ be the open partial homeomorphism that Mathlib's theorem provides ⟦Mathlib/Topology/OpenPartialHomeomorph/Defs · OpenPartialHomeomorph⟧: its forward map is $f$, its domain is a neighbourhood of $a$, and its inverse is a local inverse of $f$.
2. The derivative of $f$ is invertible at every point near $a$. This is a lemma of the companion project ⟦Milnor/Chapter1/InverseFunction · eventually_isInvertible_fderiv⟧: the invertible operators form an open set, and $df$ is continuous at $a$.
3. Let $V$ be the intersection of $U$, the domain of $\Phi_0$, and the interior of the set where $df$ is invertible. It is open and contains $a$. Restrict $\Phi_0$ to $V$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/OpenPartialHomeomorph/IsImage · OpenPartialHomeomorph.restrOpen⟧, and call the result $\Phi$.
4. $\Phi$ is $C^n$ on its domain, which lies in $U$. For $b$ in the image of $\Phi$, the point $x = \Phi^{-1}(b)$ lies in $V$, so $f$ is $C^n$ at $x$ with an invertible derivative there. The inverse of a partial homeomorphism is $C^n$ at the image of any such point ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/ContDiff/Operations · OpenPartialHomeomorph.contDiffAt_symm⟧.

This is the Euclidean core of the companion's inverse function theorem on manifolds ⟦Milnor/Chapter1/InverseFunction · isLocalDiffeomorphAt_of_isInvertible_mfderiv⟧: step 2 makes the inverse smooth at every point of its domain, and not only at $f(a)$.

### I.8 A smooth square root of a family of bilinear forms

The heart of Milnor's proof of Lemma 2.2 is a diagonalization with smooth coefficients, done one square at a time. The formal proof does it at once.

**Theorem (the square root).** Let $E$ be finite-dimensional, and $B_0$ a symmetric non-degenerate continuous bilinear form on $E$. Let $x \mapsto B(x)$ be a family of continuous bilinear forms, smooth on an open set $U \ni 0$, with $B(0) = B_0$. Then there are an open $W$ with $0 \in W \subseteq U$ and a family $x \mapsto C(x)$ of linear maps $E \to E$, smooth on $W$, with $C(0) = \mathrm{id}$ and
$$
B(x)(v, v) = B_0\big(C(x)v,\ C(x)v\big) \qquad \text{for } x \in W \text{ and all } v.
$$
⟦Chapter1/SquareRoot · exists_sqrt_family⟧

*The idea.* Consider the map from linear maps of $E$ to bilinear forms on $E$
$$
\Psi(T) = B_0(T\cdot, T\cdot) + B_0(T\cdot, \cdot) - B_0(\cdot, T\cdot)
$$
⟦Chapter1/SquareRoot · sqrtAux⟧ ⟦Chapter1/SquareRoot · sqrtAux_apply⟧. Then $\Psi(\mathrm{id}) = B_0$ ⟦Chapter1/SquareRoot · sqrtAux_id⟧. Because $B_0$ is symmetric, the last two terms cancel on the diagonal, so $\Psi(T)(v, v) = B_0(Tv, Tv)$ ⟦Chapter1/SquareRoot · sqrtAux_apply_self⟧. If $\Psi$ has a smooth local inverse near $B_0$, then $C(x) = \Psi^{-1}(B(x))$ works.

*The derivative.* Regard $B_0$ as a linear map $E \to E^*$ and write $T^{\mathsf T}$ for precomposition with $T$ on $E^*$ ⟦Chapter1/SquareRoot · precomp⟧. Then $\Psi(T) = T^{\mathsf T} B_0 T + B_0 T - T^{\mathsf T} B_0$ is a polynomial in $T$, hence smooth ⟦Chapter1/SquareRoot · contDiff_sqrtAux⟧. Differentiating at $T = \mathrm{id}$ in the direction $K$, the three terms contribute $B_0(K\cdot, \cdot) + B_0(\cdot, K\cdot)$, then $B_0(K\cdot, \cdot)$, then $-B_0(\cdot, K\cdot)$. The derivative is therefore
$$
K \mapsto 2\,B_0(K\cdot, \cdot)
$$
⟦Chapter1/SquareRoot · sqrtAuxDeriv⟧ ⟦Chapter1/SquareRoot · hasFDerivAt_sqrtAux⟧, a computation that does not use symmetry. It is invertible. Since $E^*$ has the dimension of $E$ ⟦Chapter1/SquareRoot · finrank_dual_eq⟧, the injective map $v \mapsto B_0(v, \cdot)$ is a linear isomorphism $E \to E^*$ ⟦Chapter1/SquareRoot · dualEquiv⟧, and the inverse of the derivative is $S \mapsto \tfrac12\, B_0^{-1} \circ S$, checked by composing both ways ⟦Chapter1/SquareRoot · sqrtAuxDerivEquiv⟧.

*Why the skew term.* Without $B_0(T\cdot, \cdot) - B_0(\cdot, T\cdot)$ the derivative at the identity would be $K \mapsto B_0(K\cdot, \cdot) + B_0(\cdot, K\cdot)$, which kills every $K$ that is skew for $B_0$. The inverse function theorem would then not apply. The added term is skew-symmetric, so it is invisible on the diagonal and costs nothing.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. The space of linear maps of $E$ is finite-dimensional, hence complete. Apply §I.7 to $\Psi$ on the whole space, at $\mathrm{id}$, with the derivative just computed. This gives $\Phi$, equal to $\Psi$ on its domain, with $\mathrm{id}$ in its domain and $\Phi^{-1}$ smooth on its image. The image contains $\Phi(\mathrm{id}) = B_0$.
2. Let $W$ be the set of $x \in U$ with $B(x)$ in the image of $\Phi$. It is open because $B$ is continuous on the open set $U$, and it contains $0$ because $B(0) = B_0$.
3. Put $C(x) = \Phi^{-1}(B(x))$. It is smooth on $W$ as a composite, and $C(0) = \Phi^{-1}(\Phi(\mathrm{id})) = \mathrm{id}$.
4. For $x \in W$, $\Psi(C(x)) = \Phi(\Phi^{-1}(B(x))) = B(x)$, so $B(x)(v, v) = \Psi(C(x))(v, v) = B_0(C(x)v, C(x)v)$.

The forms $B(x)$ need not be symmetric; the statement concerns only the diagonal. When $B(x)$ is symmetric, $C(x)$ is a square root of $B(x)$ relative to $B_0$, whence the name.

### I.9 Lemma 2.2: the lemma of Morse

**Lemma 2.2 (the lemma of Morse, Milnor p. 6; V1.8).** Let $E$ be finite-dimensional, $n = \dim E$, and $f : E \to \mathbb R$ smooth on an open set $U \ni p$. Let $p$ be a critical point of $f$ at which $d^2f_p$ is non-degenerate, and let $\lambda$ be its index. Then there is an open partial homeomorphism $y : E \to \mathbb R^n$, smooth on its domain with an inverse smooth on its image, whose domain contains $p$ and lies in $U$, such that $y(p) = 0$ and
$$
f(x) = f(p) - \sum_{i < \lambda} y_i(x)^2 + \sum_{i \ge \lambda} y_i(x)^2
$$
throughout its domain. ⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · morse_lemma⟧

The formal proof first proves a coordinate-free form and then diagonalizes.

**Theorem (the Morse chart, coordinate free).** Under the same hypotheses there is an open partial homeomorphism $\varphi : E \to E$, smooth with smooth inverse, whose domain contains $p$ and lies in $U$, with $\varphi(p) = 0$, $d\varphi_p = \mathrm{id}$, and
$$
f(x) = f(p) + \tfrac12\, d^2f_p\big(\varphi(x), \varphi(x)\big)
$$
throughout its domain. ⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · exists_morse_chart⟧

*The idea.* Write $f(p + x) - f(p) = B(x)(x, x)$ by Hadamard's lemma to second order, with $B(0) = \tfrac12 d^2f_p =: B_0$. Take the square root $C$ of the family $B$ relative to $B_0$ (§I.8). Then $f(p + x) - f(p) = B_0(C(x)x, C(x)x)$, and $x \mapsto C(x)x$ has derivative $C(0) = \mathrm{id}$ at $0$, so it is a coordinate system.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. *A ball.* Choose $r > 0$ with the ball of radius $r$ about $p$ inside $U$, and let $V$ be the ball of radius $r$ about $0$. It is open and convex, hence star-shaped about $0$.
2. *Translate.* Put $g(x) = f(p + x) - f(p)$. It is smooth on $V$, $g(0) = 0$, and $dg_0 = df_p = 0$ ⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · fderiv_translate⟧. Its Hessian at $0$ is $d^2f_p$ ⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · hessian_translate⟧.
3. *Hadamard.* By §I.5, $g(x) = B(x)(x, x)$ on $V$ with $B$ smooth on $V$ and $B(0) = \tfrac12\, d^2f_p = B_0$ ⟦Chapter1/Hadamard · exists_hadamard_two_on⟧.
4. *$B_0$ is admissible.* $f$ is $C^2$ at $p$, so $d^2f_p$ is symmetric ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · hessian_symm⟧, and so is $B_0$. And $B_0$ is non-degenerate because $d^2f_p$ is and $\tfrac12 \ne 0$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · isNondegenerate_smul_iff⟧.
5. *Square root.* §I.8 gives an open $W$ with $0 \in W \subseteq V$ and $C$ smooth on $W$, with $C(0) = \mathrm{id}$ and $B(x)(v, v) = B_0(C(x)v, C(x)v)$ ⟦Chapter1/SquareRoot · exists_sqrt_family⟧.
6. *The chart before inversion.* Put $\psi(x) = C(x - p)(x - p)$, smooth on the open set $p + W$. Its derivative at $p$ comes from the product rule. The term in which $C$ is differentiated is applied to $x - p$, which is $0$ at $x = p$. What remains is $C(0) = \mathrm{id}$.
7. *Inversion.* §I.7, applied to $\psi$ on $(p + W) \cap U$ at $p$ with derivative $\mathrm{id}$, gives $\Phi$, smooth with smooth inverse, equal to $\psi$ on its domain, which contains $p$ and lies in $U$ ⟦Chapter1/SquareRoot · exists_openPartialHomeomorph_of_contDiffOn⟧.
8. *The identity.* $\Phi(p) = C(0)\,0 = 0$ and $d\Phi_p = \mathrm{id}$. For $x$ in the domain, $x - p \in W \subseteq V$, so
$$
f(x) = f(p) + g(x - p) = f(p) + B(x - p)(x - p, x - p) = f(p) + B_0\big(\Phi(x), \Phi(x)\big),
$$
and $B_0 = \tfrac12\, d^2f_p$.

*The proof of Lemma 2.2 as formalized.*

1. Take $\varphi$ from the coordinate-free form.
2. Diagonalize $d^2f_p$, which is symmetric and non-degenerate of index $\lambda$ (§I.6): there is $e : E \to \mathbb R^n$ with $d^2f_p(v, v) = D(ev, ev)$, where $D$ is the diagonal form with $\lambda$ minus signs ⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · exists_linearEquiv_diagForm⟧.
3. Absorb the factor $\tfrac12$: with $L(z) = e(z/\sqrt2)$, $\tfrac12\, d^2f_p(z, z) = D(Lz, Lz)$.
4. Put $y = L \circ \varphi$, that is, $\varphi$ followed by the linear homeomorphism $L$. It has the domain of $\varphi$, it is smooth with smooth inverse $\varphi^{-1} \circ L^{-1}$, and $y(p) = L(0) = 0$. On its domain $f = f(p) + D(y, y)$, which is the normal form ⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · diagForm_morseSign_apply_self⟧.

*Where the formal proof departs from Milnor.* Milnor proves the existence of the coordinates by induction on $r$. At stage $r$ the function is $\pm (u_1)^2 \pm \dots \pm (u_{r-1})^2 + \sum_{i, j \ge r} u_i u_j H_{ij}(u)$ with $(H_{ij})$ symmetric. A linear change of the last coordinates makes $H_{rr}(0) \ne 0$. One then completes the square with $g = \sqrt{|H_{rr}|}$, and the inverse function theorem shows that the new functions are coordinates. The linear change is asserted without proof. It needs the block $\big(H_{ij}(0)\big)_{i,j \ge r}$ to be non-singular, hence non-zero, which the induction must carry along, and when all its diagonal entries vanish a substitution such as $u_r = w_r + w_l$ is needed first. Here no such step occurs. The square root of §I.8 performs the whole diagonalization with smooth coefficients in one application of the inverse function theorem, and the only linear algebra, Sylvester's law, is applied once to the constant form $d^2f_p$. The statement is also slightly more general than Milnor's: $f$ need only be smooth on a neighbourhood of $p$, and the coordinate-free form holds in any finite-dimensional normed space.

### I.10 The index is forced by the normal form

This is the first paragraph of Milnor's proof of Lemma 2.2, which the formal text proves after the existence.

**Theorem (V1.9).** Let $f : E \to \mathbb R$ be $C^2$ at a critical point $p$. Let $z : E \to \mathbb R^n$ be an open partial homeomorphism, smooth with smooth inverse, $n = \dim E$, with $p$ in its domain and $z(p) = 0$. Suppose that for some $k \le n$
$$
f(x) = f(p) - \sum_{i < k} z_i(x)^2 + \sum_{i \ge k} z_i(x)^2
$$
throughout the domain of $z$. Then $k$ is the index of $d^2f_p$. ⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · index_eq_of_morse_chart⟧

*The proof as formalized.* Let $D$ be the diagonal form with $k$ minus signs.

1. On the image of $z$, an open set containing $0$, $f \circ z^{-1}(w) = f(p) + D(w, w)$.
2. So the Hessian of $f \circ z^{-1}$ at $0$ is $2D$. It depends only on the germ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · hessian_congr⟧, the constant does not contribute, and the Hessian of $w \mapsto D(w, w)$ is $D(v, w) + D(w, v) = 2D(v, w)$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · hessian_bilinear⟧ ⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · diagForm_symm⟧.
3. The derivative of $z^{-1}$ at $0$ is a linear isomorphism, with inverse the derivative of $z$ at $p$. This follows from the chain rule applied to $z^{-1} \circ z = \mathrm{id}$ near $p$ and to $z \circ z^{-1} = \mathrm{id}$ near $0$.
4. By the independence of the coordinates (§I.4), applied to $\psi = z^{-1}$ at $0$, with $z^{-1}(0) = p$ and $df_p = 0$, the Hessian of $f \circ z^{-1}$ at $0$ has the index of $d^2f_p$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · index_hessian_comp⟧.
5. The index of $2D$ is that of $D$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · index_smul⟧, which is $k$ ⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · index_diagForm_morseSign⟧.

Milnor computes the matrix of $f_{**}$ in the coordinates $z$ (p. 6). That presupposes the independence of the coordinates, which he does not prove; the formal proof invokes it in step 4. The formal statement does not assume $E$ finite-dimensional: the derivative of the chart provides the linear isomorphism with $\mathbb R^n$ that step 4 needs.

### I.11 Corollary 2.3: non-degenerate critical points are isolated

Milnor states Corollary 2.3 right after the lemma of Morse, from which it follows (in Morse coordinates $df = \sum \pm 2 y^i\, dy^i$ vanishes only at $y = 0$), but he gives no argument. The formal proof does not use the lemma of Morse.

**Theorem.** Let $E$ be finite-dimensional, $f$ of class $C^2$ at $p$, and $d^2f_p$ non-degenerate. Then no point $x \ne p$ sufficiently close to $p$ is a critical point of $f$. ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · eventually_not_isCriticalPoint⟧

That $p$ itself is critical is not needed.

**Corollary 2.3 (Milnor, p. 8; V1.10).** If $E$ is finite-dimensional, $f$ is $C^2$ at $p$, and $p$ is a non-degenerate critical point, then $p$ has a neighbourhood in which a point is critical exactly when it is $p$. ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · IsNondegenerateCriticalPoint.exists_nhds_isCriticalPoint_eq⟧

*The idea.* The map $x \mapsto df_x$, from $E$ to its dual, has the injective derivative $d^2f_p$ at $p$. A map with an injective derivative at a point does not take its value at that point again nearby. This is the injectivity half of the inverse function theorem.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. Since $f$ is $C^2$ at $p$, the map $x \mapsto df_x$ is differentiable at $p$, with derivative $d^2f_p$, viewed as the linear map $v \mapsto d^2f_p(v, \cdot)$ from $E$ to $E^*$.
2. Non-degeneracy says that this linear map has kernel $0$. In finite dimension an injective linear map is bounded below: $\|v\| \le K\, \|d^2f_p(v, \cdot)\|$ for some $K$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Normed/Module/FiniteDimension · LinearMap.exists_antilipschitzWith⟧.
3. For each value $c$, a map whose derivative at $p$ is bounded below avoids $c$ at all points $x \ne p$ close enough to $p$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/FDeriv/Equiv · HasFDerivAt.eventually_ne⟧. For $c$ different from the value at $p$ this is continuity. For $c = df_p$ it is the estimate $df_x - df_p = d^2f_p(x - p, \cdot) + o(\|x - p\|)$, whose first term has norm at least $\|x - p\|/K$. Applied with $c = 0$: $df_x \ne 0$ for $x \ne p$ near $p$.
4. A critical point $x$ has $df_x = 0$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · IsCriticalPoint.fderiv_eq⟧.

For the corollary, step 3 provides a punctured neighbourhood of $p$ free of critical points, and $p$ is critical by hypothesis.

The companion project proves the analogous statement for vector fields, that a zero $z$ of $v$ with $dv_z$ bijective is isolated ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/Nondegenerate · Sphere.exists_radius_of_nondegenerate⟧. In coordinates on $\mathbb R^n$, Corollary 2.3 is its case $v = \operatorname{grad} f$. Working with $x \mapsto df_x$ in $E^*$ avoids choosing an inner product. *Deviation (stronger):* only $C^2$ at $p$ is assumed.

### I.12 The examples of pp. 8–9

After Corollary 2.3 Milnor lists five examples of degenerate critical points, with pictures (V1.12). In (a) and (c) the formal proofs compute the Hessian. In (d) and (e) they go through one general principle, the contrapositive of the theorem of §I.11, and a device for producing accumulating critical points.

**Contrapositive of Corollary 2.3.** If $E$ is finite-dimensional, $f$ is $C^2$ at $p$, and critical points $x \ne p$ accumulate at $p$, then $d^2f_p$ is degenerate. ⟦Chapter1/Examples · not_isNondegenerate_of_frequently⟧

**Lines.** If a property holds at every point $p + tv$ of a line through $p$, with $v \ne 0$, then it holds at points $x \ne p$ arbitrarily close to $p$, because $t \mapsto p + tv$ maps punctured neighbourhoods of $0$ into punctured neighbourhoods of $p$ ⟦Chapter1/Examples · frequently_of_line⟧.

- **(a)** $f(x) = x^3$ on $\mathbb R$. Its derivative is $3x^2$ ⟦Chapter1/Examples · Examples.hasFDerivAt_cube⟧. A point is critical exactly when it is $0$ ⟦Chapter1/Examples · Examples.isCriticalPoint_cube_iff⟧: by uniqueness of the derivative, a critical $x$ has $3x^2 = 0$. The Hessian at $0$ is $0$, the derivative of $x \mapsto 3x^2$ at $0$ ⟦Chapter1/Examples · Examples.hessian_cube⟧. So $0$ is a degenerate critical point, the vector $1$ lying in the null space ⟦Chapter1/Examples · Examples.cube_degenerate⟧.
- **(b)** $f(x) = e^{-1/x^2}\sin^2(1/x)$ is not formalized. Its smoothness at $0$ needs the flatness of $e^{-1/x^2}$ against the growth of the derivatives of $\sin^2(1/x)$. Degeneracy and non-isolation would then follow as in (d), since the points $1/(k\pi)$ are minima, hence critical.
- **(c)** The monkey saddle $f(x, y) = x^3 - 3xy^2$ ⟦Chapter1/Examples · Examples.monkey⟧. Its derivative is $(3x^2 - 3y^2)\,dx - 6xy\,dy$ ⟦Chapter1/Examples · Examples.hasFDerivAt_monkey⟧. The origin is its only critical point ⟦Chapter1/Examples · Examples.isCriticalPoint_monkey_iff⟧. At a critical point, evaluating the derivative on $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ gives $x^2 = y^2$ and $xy = 0$. Hence $x^4 = x^2y^2 = 0$, so $x = 0$, and then $y^2 = 0$. Both coefficients of the derivative have derivative $0$ at the origin, so the Hessian there is $0$ ⟦Chapter1/Examples · Examples.hessian_monkey⟧, and the origin is degenerate ⟦Chapter1/Examples · Examples.monkey_degenerate⟧.
- **(d)** $f(x, y) = x^2$ ⟦Chapter1/Examples · Examples.sqFst⟧. Its derivative is $2x\,dx$ ⟦Chapter1/Examples · Examples.hasFDerivAt_sqFst⟧. Its critical points are exactly the points with $x = 0$ ⟦Chapter1/Examples · Examples.isCriticalPoint_sqFst_iff⟧. That set is the $y$ axis. Milnor writes that the critical points form the $x$ axis, and the formalization found the slip. Every critical point is degenerate ⟦Chapter1/Examples · Examples.not_isNondegenerate_sqFst⟧: the critical points fill the vertical line through it, so they accumulate there.
- **(e)** $f(x, y) = x^2y^2$ ⟦Chapter1/Examples · Examples.sqMul⟧. Its derivative is $2xy^2\,dx + 2x^2y\,dy$ ⟦Chapter1/Examples · Examples.hasFDerivAt_sqMul⟧. Its critical points are the points of the two axes ⟦Chapter1/Examples · Examples.isCriticalPoint_sqMul_iff⟧: if $x \ne 0$ and $y \ne 0$, the coefficient $2xy^2$ is non-zero. Every critical point is degenerate ⟦Chapter1/Examples · Examples.not_isNondegenerate_sqMul⟧, by the vertical line on the axis $x = 0$ and the horizontal line on the axis $y = 0$.

In (a) and (c) the formal statements are stronger than the pictures: the origin is the only critical point. Milnor's remarks that the critical set of (d) is a submanifold and that of (e) is not are not formalized.

### I.13 Critical points, the Hessian and the index on a manifold

Let $M$ be a manifold modelled on $E$ as in §I.1, with no Hausdorff or countability hypothesis, and $f : M \to \mathbb R$. The definitions transport those of §I.2 through the preferred chart.

**Definitions (V1.4).**

- The *local representative* of $f$ at $p$ is $f \circ \kappa_p^{-1}$, a function on $E$ that is meaningful on the image of the chart, an open neighbourhood of $\kappa_p(p)$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · localRep⟧. For the identity model it is $f$ composed with the inverse of the preferred chart ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · localRep_eq⟧.
- $p$ is a *critical point* of $f$ if $df_p = 0$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsCriticalPointM⟧. Since Mathlib's $df_p$ is $0$ where $f$ is not differentiable, such points count as critical. This junk case never matters, because Milnor's $f$ is smooth.
- The *Hessian* $f_{**}$ at $p$ is the Hessian of the local representative at $\kappa_p(p)$, a continuous bilinear form on $T_pM = E$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · hessianM⟧.
- *Non-degenerate critical point*, *index* and *nullity* are those of $f_{**}$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsNondegenerateCriticalPointM⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · indexM⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · nullityM⟧.
- A *Morse function* is a smooth $f$ all of whose critical points are non-degenerate ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsMorseFunction⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsMorseFunction.isNondegenerateCriticalPointM⟧. These are the differentiable functions with no degenerate critical points of Milnor's Theorem 3.5 (p. 20) and of §6.

As in §I.2, this is Milnor's $\tilde v_p(\tilde w(f))$ for extensions with constant coefficients in the preferred chart. It is defined at every point, and at a critical point it does not depend on the chart (§I.14).

**The local representative computes everything.** On the chart's domain $f$ equals its local representative read through the chart: $f(x) = (f \circ \kappa_p^{-1})(\kappa_p(x))$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · localRep_chartAt⟧, so $f$ agrees with $(f \circ \kappa_p^{-1}) \circ \kappa_p$ near $p$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · eventuallyEq_localRep_comp⟧. Mathlib's representation of $f$ in charts, with the chart of $\mathbb R$ at $f(p)$, which is the identity, is the local representative ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · writtenInExtChartAt_eq_localRep⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/IsManifold/ExtChartAt · writtenInExtChartAt⟧. Hence:

- $f$ is differentiable at $p$ exactly when its local representative is differentiable at $\kappa_p(p)$, and $C^n$ at $p$ exactly when the representative is $C^n$ there ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · mdifferentiableAt_iff_differentiableAt_localRep⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · contMDiffAt_iff_contDiffAt_localRep⟧. Mathlib's characterizations ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/MFDeriv/Defs · mdifferentiableAt_iff⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/ContMDiff/Defs · contMDiffAt_iff⟧ also ask for continuity of $f$ at $p$. That follows from the representative and the chart. The identity model has all of $E$ as its image, so differentiability within the image is plain differentiability.
- $df_p$ is the derivative of the local representative at $\kappa_p(p)$, with no hypothesis at all ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · mfderiv_eq_fderiv_localRep⟧. Where $f$ is not differentiable both sides are $0$ ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/MFDeriv/Basic · mfderiv_zero_of_not_mdifferentiableAt⟧.
- So $p$ is critical exactly when the local representative has derivative $0$ at $\kappa_p(p)$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · isCriticalPointM_iff_fderiv⟧. For $f$ differentiable at $p$, this says that $\kappa_p(p)$ is a critical point of the local representative ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · isCriticalPointM_iff⟧, or that $f$ has derivative $0$ at $p$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · isCriticalPointM_iff_hasMFDerivAt⟧.

**Agreement with the companion volume.** A linear functional is onto exactly when it is non-zero: if $\ell(v) \ne 0$, then $t = \ell\big((t/\ell(v))\,v\big)$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · surjective_iff_ne_zero⟧. So $p$ is critical in Milnor's sense here ($f_* = 0$) exactly when it is critical in the sense of *Topology from the Differentiable Viewpoint* ($df_p$ not onto) ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · isCriticalPointM_iff_milnor⟧ ⟦Milnor/Chapter1/RegularValue · Milnor.IsCriticalPoint⟧.

**Elementary facts.** $f_{**}$ is symmetric where $f$ is $C^2$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · hessianM_symm⟧. The index is at most $\dim E$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · indexM_le_finrank⟧. Non-degeneracy is nullity $0$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · isNondegenerate_hessianM_iff_nullityM_eq_zero⟧. On the model space itself the preferred chart is the identity, so the local representative is $f$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · localRep_model⟧ and the manifold notions are those of §I.2 ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · hessianM_model⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · isCriticalPointM_model_iff⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · indexM_model⟧.

*Not restated.* Milnor remarks on p. 4 that for a regular value $a$, $M^a = f^{-1}(-\infty, a]$ is a smooth manifold with boundary $f^{-1}(a)$. This is the companion's Lemma 3 ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/WithBoundary · isHalfSliceSubmanifold_of_regularValue⟧ applied to $a - f$, and is not restated in this project.

### I.14 Smooth coordinate systems: the independence of the chart

Since $f_{**}$ is defined in one chart, its well-definedness *is* its independence of the chart. Milnor gives no argument. The formal proof is the second-order chain rule of §I.4, applied to the transition map between two coordinate systems.

**Definition.** A *smooth coordinate system* on $M$ with values in a normed space $E'$ is an open partial homeomorphism $y$ from $M$ to $E'$ that is smooth on its domain and has an inverse smooth on its image ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord⟧. It is the same thing as a partial diffeomorphism onto an open subset of $E'$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.toPartialDiffeomorph⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · isSmoothCoord_toOpenPartialHomeomorph⟧. With $E' = \mathbb R^n$ it is Milnor's local coordinate system $(y^1, \dots, y^n)$. With $E' = E$ the smooth coordinate systems are exactly the charts of the maximal smooth atlas ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · isSmoothCoord_iff_mem_maximalAtlas⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/ContMDiff/Atlas · IsManifold.mem_maximalAtlas_iff_contMDiffOn⟧. In particular the preferred chart at $p$ is one ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · isSmoothCoord_chartAt⟧. A smooth coordinate system followed by a diffeomorphism between open subsets of normed spaces is again one ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.trans⟧.

**Transition maps.** Let $y$, $z$ be smooth coordinate systems with values in $E'$, $E''$, both defined at $p$.

- The domain $y(\operatorname{dom} y \cap \operatorname{dom} z)$ of the transition map $z \circ y^{-1}$ is open ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.isOpen_trans_source⟧. The transition map is smooth there ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.contDiffOn_trans⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.contDiffAt_trans⟧.
- Its derivative at $y(p)$ is a linear isomorphism, whose inverse is the derivative of $y \circ z^{-1}$ at $z(p)$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.exists_fderiv_trans_eq⟧. This is a lemma of calculus ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · exists_fderiv_eq_of_localInverse⟧: if $\psi$ and $\chi$ are inverse to each other near $a$ and $\psi(a)$ and differentiable there, the chain rule applied to $\chi \circ \psi = \mathrm{id}$ and $\psi \circ \chi = \mathrm{id}$ shows that $d\psi_a$ and $d\chi_{\psi(a)}$ are inverse isomorphisms.
- Near $y(p)$, $f \circ y^{-1} = (f \circ z^{-1}) \circ (z \circ y^{-1})$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.comp_symm_eventuallyEq⟧.

**Theorem (two coordinate systems).** In this situation, with $f \circ z^{-1}$ differentiable at $z(p)$: $y(p)$ is a critical point of $f \circ y^{-1}$ exactly when $z(p)$ is a critical point of $f \circ z^{-1}$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.isCriticalPoint_trans_iff⟧. If moreover $f \circ z^{-1}$ is $C^2$ at $z(p)$ with derivative $0$ there, then the Hessian of $f \circ y^{-1}$ at $y(p)$ is the Hessian of $f \circ z^{-1}$ at $z(p)$ pulled back by the derivative of the transition map ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.hessian_trans⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.exists_hessian_trans_eq_bilinearComp⟧. Hence they are non-degenerate together and have the same index and the same nullity ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.isNondegenerate_trans_iff⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.index_trans_eq⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.nullity_trans_eq⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* For criticality, the derivative of $f \circ y^{-1}$ at $y(p)$ is that of $f \circ z^{-1}$ composed with an isomorphism, and it vanishes exactly when that of $f \circ z^{-1}$ does. For the Hessian, the germ identity reduces it to the Hessian of a composite with the smooth transition map, and §I.4 applies at the critical point $z(p)$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · hessian_comp⟧. Non-degeneracy, index and nullity then follow from §I.3.

**Theorem (any coordinate system computes $f_{**}$; V1.5).** Let $y$ be a smooth coordinate system with values in $E'$, defined at $p$.

- The derivative of $y$ at $p$ is a linear isomorphism $T_pM \to E'$, and that of $y^{-1}$ at $y(p)$ is a linear isomorphism $E' \to T_pM$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.exists_mfderiv_eq⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.exists_mfderiv_symm_eq⟧. Read in the preferred chart, they are the derivatives of the transition maps ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.mfderiv_eq⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.mfderiv_symm_eq⟧.
- If $f$ is differentiable at $p$, then $p$ is critical exactly when $y(p)$ is a critical point of $f \circ y^{-1}$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.isCriticalPointM_iff⟧. For $E' = \mathbb R^n$ this is Milnor's criterion $\partial f/\partial y^1(p) = \dots = \partial f/\partial y^n(p) = 0$ (p. 4), because a linear functional on $\mathbb R^n$ that vanishes on the basis vectors is $0$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.isCriticalPointM_iff_forall_partial⟧.
- If $f$ is $C^2$ at the critical point $p$, the Hessian of $f \circ y^{-1}$ at $y(p)$ is $f_{**}$ pulled back by the derivative of $y^{-1}$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.hessian_comp_symm⟧. In Milnor's words (p. 5), the matrix $\big(\partial^2 f/\partial y^i \partial y^j(p)\big)$ represents $f_{**}$ in the basis $\partial/\partial y^i|_p$. So non-degeneracy, index and nullity can be computed in $y$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.isNondegenerate_iff⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.index_eq⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.nullity_eq⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.isNondegenerateCriticalPointM_iff⟧. For $E' = \mathbb R^n$, $p$ is non-degenerate exactly when $\det\big(\partial^2 f/\partial y^i \partial y^j(p)\big) \ne 0$, which is Milnor's definition ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.isNondegenerate_iff_det_ne_zero⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Take $z$ to be the preferred chart at $p$ and apply the previous theorem. The Hessian $f_{**}$ is by definition the Hessian of $f$ read in that chart ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · hessianM_eq_chartAt⟧. If $f$ is $C^2$ at $p$, so is that representative ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · contDiffAt_comp_chartAt_symm⟧, and at a critical point its derivative is $0$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · fderiv_comp_chartAt_symm_eq_zero⟧. Representatives in any coordinate system inherit the smoothness of $f$ at $p$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.contDiffAt_comp_symm⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.differentiableAt_comp_symm⟧. The matrix criterion is §I.3 applied in the coordinates $y$.

The same statements are recorded for the charts of the maximal atlas ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · isCriticalPointM_iff_of_mem_maximalAtlas⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · hessian_comp_symm_of_mem_maximalAtlas⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · isNondegenerate_iff_of_mem_maximalAtlas⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · indexM_eq_of_mem_maximalAtlas⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · nullityM_eq_of_mem_maximalAtlas⟧.

*Deviation (stronger).* $f$ need only be $C^2$ at $p$, and the coordinates may take values in any normed space. The only use of criticality is the one of §I.4.

### I.15 Corollary 2.3 on a manifold, and the finiteness of the critical set

**Theorem (criticality near $p$ can be read in the chart at $p$).** If $f$ is $C^1$ at $p$, then for every $x$ near $p$: $x$ is critical exactly when $\kappa_p(x)$ is a critical point of the local representative at $p$. ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · eventually_isCriticalPointM_iff⟧

*Proof.* A finite order of smoothness propagates, so $f$ is $C^1$ at every $x$ near $p$ ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/ContMDiff/Defs · contMDiffAt_iff_contMDiffAt_nhds⟧. For such $x$ in the domain of the preferred chart at $p$, that chart is a smooth coordinate system defined at $x$ (§I.14), and criticality at $x$ can be computed in it ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.isCriticalPointM_iff⟧.

**Theorem.** If $E$ is finite-dimensional, $f$ is $C^2$ at $p$ and $f_{**}$ is non-degenerate at $p$, then no $x \ne p$ near $p$ is critical. ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · eventually_not_isCriticalPointM⟧

*Proof.* Put $a = \kappa_p(p)$. The local representative is $C^2$ at $a$ with non-degenerate Hessian $f_{**}$, so the Euclidean theorem of §I.11 gives a punctured neighbourhood of $a$ free of its critical points ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · eventually_not_isCriticalPoint⟧. The chart maps a punctured neighbourhood of $p$ into a punctured neighbourhood of $a$: it is continuous at $p$, and injective on its domain, so $x \ne p$ gives $\kappa_p(x) \ne a$. The previous theorem translates back.

**Corollary 2.3 on a manifold.** If $E$ is finite-dimensional, $f$ is $C^2$ at $p$, and $p$ is a non-degenerate critical point, then $p$ has a neighbourhood in which it is the only critical point. ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · IsNondegenerateCriticalPointM.exists_nhds_isCriticalPointM_eq⟧

Milnor never states the global consequence, but he uses it repeatedly. On p. 23 the critical values have no cluster point because each $M^a$ is compact. In §5 a compact manifold carries finitely many critical points, arranged in levels. In §6 (p. 36) one sums over the critical points.

**Theorem (V1.11).** Let $f : M \to \mathbb R$.

- If $f$ is $C^1$, its critical set is closed. ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · isClosed_setOf_isCriticalPointM⟧
- If $E$ is finite-dimensional, $f$ is $C^2$ at each point of a set $S \subseteq M$, and every critical point in $S$ is non-degenerate, then the critical points in $S$ form a discrete set. ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · isDiscrete_inter_setOf_isCriticalPointM⟧
- If $E$ is finite-dimensional, $f$ is $C^2$ and all its critical points are non-degenerate, then every compact set contains only finitely many critical points. ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · finite_inter_setOf_isCriticalPointM⟧ In particular a Morse function has finitely many critical points in each compact set, and finitely many in all if $M$ is compact. ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · IsMorseFunction.finite_inter_setOf_isCriticalPointM⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · IsMorseFunction.finite_setOf_isCriticalPointM⟧

*The proof as formalized.*

1. *Closed.* Let $x_0$ be non-critical, $g$ the local representative at $x_0$ and $a = \kappa_{x_0}(x_0)$. Since $f$ is $C^1$, $dg$ is continuous at $a$, and $dg_a \ne 0$. So $dg \ne 0$ near $a$, and, pulled back by the chart, at $\kappa_{x_0}(x)$ for $x$ near $x_0$. By the first theorem of this section such $x$ are not critical. So the complement of the critical set is open.
2. *Discrete.* By Mathlib's criterion ⟦Mathlib/Topology/DiscreteSubset · isDiscrete_iff_forall_mem_exists_isOpen⟧ it suffices that each critical $x \in S$ have an open set meeting the critical points of $S$ only in $x$. The interior of the neighbourhood given by Corollary 2.3 is such a set.
3. *Finite.* The critical points in a compact $K$ form a closed subset of $K$, hence a compact set, and a discrete one by step 2. A compact discrete set is finite ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Compactness/Compact · IsCompact.finite⟧.

*Hidden hypotheses: none.* Hausdorffness is not needed. A closed subset of a compact set is compact, and a compact discrete set is finite, in any topological space. In §5 (p. 29) Milnor asks the critical points to be isolated and non-degenerate; the first condition follows from the second.

### I.16 The lemma of Morse on a manifold, and its converse

**Theorem (the Morse chart on a manifold).** Let $E$ be finite-dimensional, $f$ smooth on an open set $U \ni p$ of $M$, and $p$ a non-degenerate critical point. There is a chart $\varphi$ of the maximal atlas, with $p$ in its domain and its domain inside $U$, such that $\varphi(p) = 0$ and $f = f(p) + \tfrac12\, f_{**}(\varphi, \varphi)$ throughout its domain. ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · exists_morse_chartM⟧

**Lemma 2.2 on a manifold (V1.8).** Under the same hypotheses, with $\lambda$ the index of $f$ at $p$ and $n = \dim E$, there is a smooth coordinate system $y : M \to \mathbb R^n$, with $p$ in its domain and its domain inside $U$, such that $y(p) = 0$ and $f = f(p) - \sum_{i < \lambda} y_i^2 + \sum_{i \ge \lambda} y_i^2$ throughout its domain. ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · morse_lemmaM⟧

*The proof as formalized.* Both are obtained by applying the Euclidean theorems of §I.9 to the local representative in the preferred chart at $p$, and composing with that chart.

1. On the open set of points of the chart's image whose preimage lies in $U$, which contains $\kappa_p(p)$, the representative $f \circ \kappa_p^{-1}$ is smooth. The point $\kappa_p(p)$ is critical for it, and its Hessian there is $f_{**}$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · morse_lemmaM_aux⟧.
2. The Euclidean Morse chart, or Morse coordinates, for $f \circ \kappa_p^{-1}$ at $\kappa_p(p)$ give $\varphi_0$, respectively $y_0$ ⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · exists_morse_chart⟧ ⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · morse_lemma⟧.
3. The composite of the preferred chart with $\varphi_0$, respectively $y_0$, is a smooth coordinate system (§I.14). With values in $E$ it lies in the maximal atlas ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · isSmoothCoord_iff_mem_maximalAtlas⟧. Its domain lies in $U$ because that of $\varphi_0$ lies in the preimage of $U$. The value at $p$ and the formula for $f$ follow by cancelling the chart with its inverse on the chart's domain.

**The converse (Milnor p. 6, first paragraph of the proof; V1.9).** Let $y : M \to \mathbb R^n$ be a smooth coordinate system with $p$ in its domain and $y(p) = 0$, and suppose that for some $k \le n$ the function $f$ has the normal form $f(p) - \sum_{i < k} y_i^2 + \sum_{i \ge k} y_i^2$ throughout the domain of $y$. Then $p$ is a non-degenerate critical point of $f$ of index $k$. No hypothesis is made on $f$. ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · isNondegenerateCriticalPointM_of_morse_chart⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · indexM_eq_of_morse_chart⟧

*The proof as formalized.* Let $D$ be the diagonal form with $k$ minus signs and $Q(w) = f(p) + D(w, w)$ the normal form as a function on $\mathbb R^n$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · morseNormalForm⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · morseNormalForm_eq⟧.

1. $Q$ is smooth, as a constant plus a continuous bilinear form on the diagonal ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · contDiff_morseNormalForm⟧. On the domain of $y$, $f = Q \circ y$, so $f$ agrees near $p$ with a smooth function and is smooth at $p$. Smoothness is a conclusion, not a hypothesis.
2. Near $0 = y(p)$, $f \circ y^{-1} = Q$.
3. $0$ is a critical point of $Q$: the derivative of $w \mapsto D(w, w)$ at $0$ is $D(0, \cdot) + D(\cdot, 0) = 0$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · isCriticalPoint_morseNormalForm⟧. By §I.14, $p$ is critical.
4. The Hessian of $Q$ at $0$ is $2D$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · hessian_morseNormalForm⟧.
5. $D$ is non-degenerate: $D(v, e_i) = \pm v_i$, so a vector in the null space has every coordinate $0$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · isNondegenerate_diagForm_morseSign⟧. By §I.14 and §I.3, $f_{**}$ is non-degenerate.
6. The index, computed in $y$ (§I.14), is that of $2D$, which is $k$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · index_smul⟧ ⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · index_diagForm_morseSign⟧.

This is Milnor's argument with the independence of the coordinates made explicit. *Deviation (stronger):* smoothness, criticality and non-degeneracy are conclusions, not hypotheses.

### I.17 Functions on a submanifold of Euclidean space

Milnor's §6 studies functions $f = F|N$ on a manifold $N$ in Euclidean space, chiefly $L_p(x) = \|x - p\|^2$, by computing in coordinates $(u^1, \dots, u^k)$ on a region of $N$. The inclusion becomes a parametrization $u \mapsto x(u)$, and he states two formulas. On p. 35, $\partial f/\partial u^i = 2\,(\partial x/\partial u^i) \cdot (x - p)$, so $L_p$ has a critical point at $q$ exactly when $q - p$ is normal to $N$ at $q$. On p. 36, at a critical point,
$$
\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial u^i\, \partial u^j} = 2\Big(\frac{\partial x}{\partial u^i} \cdot \frac{\partial x}{\partial u^j} + \frac{\partial^2 x}{\partial u^i\, \partial u^j} \cdot (x - p)\Big),
$$
and the index and nullity of $L_p$ at $q$ are those of this matrix. The file `Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold` connects such computations with the manifold notions of §§I.13–1.14, for the slice submanifolds of the companion project. Most later parts work in this setting.

*Setting (V1.6).* $V$ is a real normed space, an inner product space from the paragraph on gradients onwards (Milnor's $\mathbb R^n$ of §6). $N \subseteq V$ is a slice submanifold modelled on $Z$ with complement $W$ (§I.1), a manifold modelled on $Z$ through the charts $q \mapsto \sigma(q)_Z$ induced by its slice charts $\sigma$. For $F : V \to \mathbb R$ the function on $N$ is $F|N = F \circ \iota$, with $\iota : N \to V$ the inclusion. $N$ need not be closed.

**The parametrization of a slice chart.** For a slice chart $\sigma$ put $x(z) = \sigma^{-1}(z, 0)$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · sliceParam⟧. This is Milnor's $u \mapsto x(u)$, and it is meaningful on the open set of $z$ with $(z, 0)$ in the image of $\sigma$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · isOpen_toChart_target⟧. There it takes values in $N$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · sliceParam_mem⟧ ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · SliceChart.symm_mem⟧ and is the inverse of the induced chart of $N$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · val_toChart_symm⟧ ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · SliceChart.toChart_symm_apply⟧, so $x(\sigma(q)_Z) = q$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · sliceParam_toChart⟧. It is smooth, as $\sigma^{-1}$ composed with $z \mapsto (z, 0)$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · contDiffOn_sliceParam⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · contDiffAt_sliceParam⟧. Near any point of its domain, $F|N$ read in the induced chart is $F \circ x$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · comp_val_comp_toChart_symm_eventuallyEq⟧.

**Slice charts are coordinate systems of $N$.** The induced chart of every slice chart belongs to the atlas of $N$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · IsSliceSubmanifold.mem_atlas_iff⟧, hence to the maximal atlas ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · toChart_mem_maximalAtlas⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/IsManifold/Basic · IsManifold.subset_maximalAtlas⟧. It is therefore a smooth coordinate system in the sense of §I.14 ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · isSmoothCoord_toChart⟧. An ambient function that is $C^n$ at $q$, or differentiable at $q$, or smooth, restricts to one on $N$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · contMDiffAt_comp_val⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · mdifferentiableAt_comp_val⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · contMDiff_comp_val⟧.

**Theorem (computing in a parametrization).** Let $q \in N$ lie in the domain of the slice chart $\sigma$, with $x$ its parametrization and $u = \sigma(q)_Z$.

- If $F$ is differentiable at $q$, then $q$ is a critical point of $F|N$ exactly when $u$ is a critical point of $F \circ x$. ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · isCriticalPointM_comp_val_iff_sliceParam⟧
- If $F$ is $C^2$ at $q$ and $q$ is a critical point of $F|N$, the Hessian of $F \circ x$ at $u$ is $f_{**}$ pulled back by the derivative of the inverse chart ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · hessian_comp_sliceParam⟧. Hence non-degeneracy, index and nullity of $F|N$ at $q$ are those of $F \circ x$ at $u$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · isNondegenerate_hessian_comp_sliceParam_iff⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · index_hessian_comp_sliceParam⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · nullity_hessian_comp_sliceParam⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · isNondegenerateCriticalPointM_comp_val_iff_sliceParam⟧.

*Proof.* Apply §I.14 to the coordinate system induced by $\sigma$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.isCriticalPointM_iff⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.hessian_comp_symm⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.index_eq⟧. The representative $(F|N) \circ (\text{chart})^{-1}$ agrees with $F \circ x$ near $u$, and Hessians and criticality depend only on germs.

Milnor works in one fixed coordinate system; that any parametrization gives the same answer is the chart independence of §I.14.

**The tangent space.** $T_qN \subseteq V$ is the range of the derivative of the inclusion at $q$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · tangentSubspace⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · mem_tangentSubspace_iff⟧ (§0.3), the derivative being read as a linear map $Z \to V$ through the companion's restatement of the manifold derivative ⟦Milnor/Chapter5/Orient · mfd⟧. It is spanned by the partial derivatives of any parametrization: $T_qN$ is the range of $dx_u$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · tangentSubspace_eq_range_fderiv_sliceParam⟧. *Proof:* near $q$ the inclusion is $x$ composed with the induced chart. The chain rule ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/MFDeriv/Basic · mfderiv_comp⟧ gives $d\iota_q = dx_u \circ d(\text{chart})_q$, and the second factor is a linear isomorphism (§I.14). Composing with a surjection does not change the range.

**Theorem (critical points through the ambient derivative).** Let $F$ be differentiable at $q \in N$.

- $q$ is a critical point of $F|N$ exactly when $dF_q$ vanishes on $T_qN$. ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · isCriticalPointM_comp_val_iff_forall_tangent⟧
- If $V$ is a complete inner product space, this holds exactly when the gradient $\nabla F(q)$ is orthogonal to $T_qN$, the condition Milnor uses on p. 35. ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · isCriticalPointM_comp_val_iff_gradient_mem_orthogonal⟧
- If $V$ is finite-dimensional, this holds exactly when the orthogonal projection of $\nabla F(q)$ on $T_qN$ is $0$. This is the fact Milnor uses on p. 36: the zeros of the gradient field of $f$ on $N$ are the critical points of $f$. ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · isCriticalPointM_comp_val_iff_starProjection_gradient_eq_zero⟧

*Proof.* The chain rule gives $d(F|N)_q = dF_q \circ d\iota_q$, which is $0$ exactly when $dF_q$ vanishes on the range of $d\iota_q$. The gradient is the vector with $\langle \nabla F(q), v\rangle = dF_q(v)$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/Gradient/Basic · gradient⟧, so $dF_q$ vanishes on $T_qN$ exactly when $\nabla F(q)$ lies in the orthogonal complement ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Orthogonal · Submodule.orthogonal⟧. A vector lies in $T_qN^\perp$ exactly when its orthogonal projection on $T_qN$ is $0$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Projection/Basic · Submodule.starProjection_apply_eq_zero_iff⟧.

**The function $L_p$.** On an inner product space, $L_p(y) = \|y - p\|^2$ has derivative $v \mapsto 2\langle y - p, v\rangle$ at $y$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · hasFDerivAt_normSq_sub⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · fderiv_normSq_sub_apply⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Calculus · HasFDerivAt.norm_sq⟧. Its Hessian is $2\langle\cdot, \cdot\rangle$ at every point ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · hessian_normSq_sub⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · hessian_normSq_sub_apply⟧, because the derivative is an affine function of $y$. And $L_p$ is smooth ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · contDiff_normSq_sub⟧.

**Theorem (Milnor, p. 35).** A point $q \in N$ is a critical point of $L_p|N$ exactly when $q - p$ is orthogonal to $T_qN$. ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · isCriticalPointM_normSq_sub_iff⟧

*Proof.* By the theorem above, $q$ is critical exactly when $2\langle q - p, v\rangle = 0$ for every $v \in T_qN$, that is, when $q - p \in T_qN^\perp$.

**Theorem (Milnor, p. 36).** Let $x : Z \to V$ be $C^2$ at $u$. Then for all $v, w \in Z$
$$
d^2(L_p \circ x)_u(v, w) = 2\Big(\big\langle dx_u v,\ dx_u w\big\rangle + \big\langle d^2x_u(v, w),\ x(u) - p\big\rangle\Big).
$$
⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · hessian_normSq_sub_comp⟧

*Proof.* This is the second-order chain rule of §I.4 *with* its first-order term ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · hessian_comp_apply⟧. The term $d^2L_p(dx\,v, dx\,w)$ is $2\langle dx\,v, dx\,w\rangle$, and the term $dL_p(d^2x(v, w))$ is $2\langle x(u) - p, d^2x(v, w)\rangle$. Milnor states the formula at a critical point; it holds at every $u$.

**Corollary.** At a critical point $q$ of $L_p|N$ in the domain of a slice chart with parametrization $x$ and $u = \sigma(q)_Z$, the index, the nullity and the non-degeneracy of $L_p|N$ at $q$ are those of the bilinear form $(v, w) \mapsto 2\big(\langle dx_u v, dx_u w\rangle + \langle d^2x_u(v, w), x(u) - p\rangle\big)$. ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · indexM_normSq_sub_eq⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · nullityM_normSq_sub_eq⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · isNondegenerate_hessianM_normSq_sub_iff⟧

The nullity statement is the computation behind Milnor's Lemma 6.5 (p. 36). Part III uses these statements for focal points.

*What Milnor leaves implicit.* The Hessian of $F \circ x$ is not the restriction of the ambient Hessian of $F$. The chain rule adds $dF_q(d^2x(v, w))$, which does not vanish at a critical point of $F|N$, since only the tangential part of $dF_q$ does. For $L_p$ this added term is the second-fundamental-form term of Milnor's formula, and it is why §I.4 keeps the first-order term.

**In the notation of the companion's tubular neighbourhoods.** The companion's Poincaré–Hopf theorem ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/PoincareHopf · Tub.poincare_hopf⟧ works with $N \subseteq \mathbb R^{K+1}$, its chosen slice chart at $q$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/Tubular1 · Tub.σ⟧, the parametrization ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/Tubular1 · Tub.φ⟧, the point over $q$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/Tubular3 · Tub.z₀⟧ and the tangent space ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/ChartIndex · Tub.tanN⟧. These are, by definition, the objects above for that slice chart ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · sliceParam_tub⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · tangentSubspace_eq_tanN⟧. So the index and the non-degeneracy of $F|N$ at a critical point can be computed in the companion's parametrization ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · indexM_comp_val_eq_tub⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · isNondegenerateCriticalPointM_comp_val_iff_tub⟧ ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/Tubular1 · Tub.mem_source⟧. This is how §6, Application 2 (the alternating sum of the numbers of critical points equals the Euler characteristic) is connected to Poincaré–Hopf in Part III.

### I.18 Global flows of bounded Lipschitz vector fields

Milnor's proof of Lemma 2.4 (pp. 10–11) rests on two facts he quotes, citing Graves: locally the equation $d\varphi_t(q)/dt = X_{\varphi_t(q)}$, $\varphi_0(q) = q$, has a unique solution, and that solution depends smoothly on the initial condition. He covers the compact set $K$ outside which $X$ vanishes by finitely many neighbourhoods on which solutions exist for $|t| < \varepsilon$, and lets $\varepsilon_0$ be the least of these $\varepsilon$. He sets $\varphi_t(q) = q$ for $q \notin K$. Larger times come from iterating $\varphi_{\varepsilon_0/2}$, and he leaves the reader to verify that the result is well defined, smooth, and a group. The formal proof works on a Banach space $E$; for the lemma itself only $E = \mathbb R^n$ matters (§I.22). This section proves everything except smoothness, for every *bounded, globally Lipschitz* field, without any iteration. Flows are recalled in §0.10.

**Definitions.** A vector field $X : E \to E$ is *bounded and Lipschitz* if $\|X\| \le M$ everywhere and $X$ is $K$-Lipschitz for some $M$, $K$ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz⟧. An *integral curve* of $X$ is a curve $\gamma : \mathbb R \to E$ with $\gamma'(t) = X(\gamma(t))$ for every $t$, in Mathlib's sense with a field that does not depend on time ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/ODE/Basic · IsIntegralCurve⟧.

A $C^1$ field that vanishes outside a compact set is bounded and Lipschitz ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.of_contDiff⟧. $X$ and $dX$ are continuous with compact support, hence bounded, and a bound $C$ on $\|dX\|$ is a Lipschitz constant by the mean value inequality ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/MeanValue · lipschitzWith_of_nnnorm_fderiv_le⟧. A scalar multiple of a bounded Lipschitz field is one ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.smul⟧.

**Uniqueness.** Two solutions of $\gamma' = X(\gamma)$ on an open interval that agree at one time agree on the whole interval ⟦Flow/Global · eqOn_Ioo_of_hasDerivAt⟧. This is Mathlib's uniqueness theorem, proved by Grönwall's inequality ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/ODE/ExistUnique · ODE_solution_unique_of_mem_Ioo⟧. The same holds for integral curves defined on all of $\mathbb R$ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.isIntegralCurve_eq⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/ODE/ExistUnique · ODE_solution_unique_univ⟧. On a manifold this step would need the Hausdorff property; a normed space has it.

**Theorem (local existence on any interval).** Let $E$ be complete and $X$ bounded and Lipschitz. For every $x \in E$ and every $a$ there is a solution $\alpha$ of $\alpha' = X(\alpha)$ on $(-a, a)$ with $\alpha(0) = x$. ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.exists_local⟧

*Proof.* For $a \le 0$ the interval is empty. For $a > 0$, apply the Picard–Lindelöf theorem ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/ODE/PicardLindelof · IsPicardLindelof⟧ to the time-independent field $X$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/ODE/PicardLindelof · IsPicardLindelof.of_time_independent⟧ on the time interval $[-a, a]$, with initial point $x$ and the closed ball of radius $Ma$ about $x$. Its condition, that the speed bound times the time from $0$ to either end of the interval does not exceed the radius, reads $M \cdot a \le Ma$: a solution moving at speed at most $M$ cannot leave the ball before time $a$. So no shrinking of the time interval is needed. Mathlib provides a solution with a one-sided derivative within $[-a, a]$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/ODE/ExistUnique · IsPicardLindelof.exists_eq_forall_mem_Icc_hasDerivWithinAt₀⟧, which is a genuine derivative in the interior.

**Theorem (global existence).** Under the same hypotheses, through every point there is an integral curve defined on all of $\mathbb R$. ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.exists_isIntegralCurve⟧

*Proof.* Choose a solution $\alpha_a$ on $(-a, a)$ for each $a$. If $|t| < a$ and $|t| < b$, then $\alpha_a(t) = \alpha_b(t)$, by uniqueness on $(-\min(a,b), \min(a,b))$, where both start at $x$. Put $\gamma(t) = \alpha_{|t| + 1}(t)$. Near any $t$, $\gamma$ agrees with $\alpha_{|t|+1}$, because $|s| < |t| + 1$ for $s$ near $t$. So $\gamma$ has derivative $X(\gamma(t))$ at $t$.

**Definition (the flow).** $\varphi_t(x)$ is the value at time $t$ of an integral curve of $X$ through $x$ at time $0$, chosen when one exists; otherwise $\varphi_t(x) = x$, a junk value ⟦Flow/Global · flow⟧. For a bounded Lipschitz field the curve exists and is unique, so the choice is immaterial.

**Theorem (the flow, without smoothness).** Let $E$ be complete and $X$ bounded and Lipschitz.

- $\varphi_0 = \mathrm{id}$, and $t \mapsto \varphi_t(x)$ is an integral curve ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flow_zero⟧ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.isIntegralCurve_flow⟧ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.hasDerivAt_flow⟧.
- Every integral curve is a flow line: $\gamma(t) = \varphi_{t - t_0}(\gamma(t_0))$ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.eq_flow⟧. Both sides are integral curves that agree at $t_0$.
- The group law: $\varphi_{s+t} = \varphi_s \circ \varphi_t$ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flow_add⟧ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flow_comp⟧. Apply the previous item to the flow line through $x$, read from time $t$. In particular $\varphi_{-t}$ inverts $\varphi_t$ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flow_neg_flow⟧ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flow_flow_neg⟧, and $\varphi_{nt} = (\varphi_t)^n$ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flow_nat_mul⟧.
- Rescaling: the flow of $cX$ at time $t$ is the flow of $X$ at time $ct$ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flow_smul⟧, because $s \mapsto \varphi_{cs}(x)$ is an integral curve of $cX$ through $x$.
- Zeros are fixed: if $X(x) = 0$, the constant curve is an integral curve, so $\varphi_t(x) = x$ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flow_of_eq_zero⟧. This gives Milnor's $\varphi_t(q) = q$ for $q$ outside the support ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flow_of_notMem_tsupport⟧.
- Lipschitz dependence: $d(\varphi_t x, \varphi_t y) \le d(x, y)\, e^{K|t|}$ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.dist_flow_le⟧ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.lipschitzWith_flow⟧. This is Grönwall's inequality ⟦Flow/Global · dist_le_of_hasDerivAt⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/ODE/Gronwall · dist_le_of_trajectories_ODE⟧. For negative $t$ it is applied to the curves $s \mapsto \varphi_{-s}(x)$, integral curves of $-X$, which is also $K$-Lipschitz.
- In time, flow lines are $M$-Lipschitz, by the mean value inequality ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.dist_flow_flow_le⟧.
- Joint continuity: $(t, x) \mapsto \varphi_t(x)$ is continuous ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.continuous_flow⟧. Within distance $1$ of $(t_0, x_0)$, for the maximum distance on $\mathbb R \times E$, the two previous items give $d(\varphi_t x, \varphi_{t_0} x_0) \le \big(e^{K(|t_0|+1)} + M\big)\, d\big((t, x), (t_0, x_0)\big)$. A locally Lipschitz map is continuous ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Algebra/MetricSpace/Lipschitz · continuousAt_of_locally_lipschitz⟧. Each $\varphi_t$ is a homeomorphism with inverse $\varphi_{-t}$ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flowHomeomorph⟧.

**Theorem (invariant closed sets).** Let $S \subseteq E$ be closed, and suppose that through every point $y \in S$ there is a solution of $\gamma' = X(\gamma)$ on some $(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$ that starts at $y$ and stays in $S$. Then $\varphi_t(S) \subseteq S$ for every $t$. ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flow_mem⟧

*Proof.* By uniqueness on $(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$, the flow line through $y$ is the given curve for small times, so it stays in $S$ for small $|s|$. Then, for $x \in S$, the set $T = \{t : \varphi_t(x) \in S\}$ is closed, since $S$ is closed and $t \mapsto \varphi_t(x)$ is continuous. It is open: if $t \in T$, then $\varphi_u(x) = \varphi_{u - t}(\varphi_t(x)) \in S$ for $u$ near $t$, by the group law. It contains $0$. A non-empty clopen subset of $\mathbb R$ is $\mathbb R$ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flow_mem_of_eventually⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Connected/Clopen · IsClopen.eq_univ⟧.

This is not in Milnor. Part II uses it to keep the flow of a field tangent to a submanifold $N \subseteq \mathbb R^K$ inside $N$.

*Where the formal proof departs from Milnor.* There is no iteration of $\varphi_{\varepsilon_0/2}$. Milnor's definition $\varphi_t = (\varphi_{\varepsilon_0/2})^k \circ \varphi_r$ depends on a decomposition $t = k(\varepsilon_0/2) + r$ that is not unique ($\varepsilon_0/4 = 0 \cdot \varepsilon_0/2 + \varepsilon_0/4 = 1 \cdot \varepsilon_0/2 - \varepsilon_0/4$), and his remark that well-definedness is not difficult to verify (p. 11) passes over that. A field bounded by $M$ has solutions on intervals of every length, so the flow is defined directly. Compactness of the support is used only to make $X$ bounded and globally Lipschitz. Milnor's remark on p. 11 that the hypothesis cannot be omitted ($d/dt$ on $(0, 1)$) shows that some such global condition is needed; the remark itself is not formalized.

### I.19 The Nemytskii operator

Smoothness of the flow is where Milnor's proof of Lemma 2.4 hides real work, and Mathlib proves only Lipschitz dependence on the initial point. The formal proof writes the flow line through $x$ on $[0, 1]$ as the solution of the integral equation $\gamma(s) = x + \int_0^s X(\gamma(r))\, dr$ in the Banach space $C([0, 1], E)$ of continuous curves on Mathlib's unit interval ⟦Mathlib/Topology/UnitInterval · unitInterval⟧, with the supremum norm. It then applies the inverse function theorem there. The only non-linear ingredient of the equation is the *Nemytskii* (or superposition) operator $\gamma \mapsto Y \circ \gamma$.

**Definitions.** For a continuous $Y : E \to F$, $N_Y(\gamma) = Y \circ \gamma$ ⟦Flow/Nemytskii · nem⟧ ⟦Flow/Nemytskii · nem_apply⟧. It is continuous ⟦Flow/Nemytskii · continuous_nem⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/CompactOpen · ContinuousMap.continuous_postcomp⟧, $K$-Lipschitz if $Y$ is ⟦Flow/Nemytskii · lipschitzWith_nem⟧, and bounded by any bound of $Y$ ⟦Flow/Nemytskii · norm_nem_le⟧. For a continuous curve $A$ of operators $E \to F$, write $\mathcal M_A$ for pointwise application, $\mathcal M_A\eta = \big(s \mapsto A(s)\,\eta(s)\big)$. The map $(A, \eta) \mapsto \mathcal M_A \eta$ is a continuous bilinear map of norm at most $1$ ⟦Flow/Nemytskii · pmulₗ⟧ ⟦Flow/Nemytskii · norm_pmulₗ_le⟧ ⟦Flow/Nemytskii · pmul⟧ ⟦Flow/Nemytskii · pmul_apply⟧ ⟦Flow/Nemytskii · norm_pmul_le⟧. It is therefore smooth ⟦Flow/Nemytskii · contDiff_pmul⟧. For $Y$ of class $C^1$, $dY$ is a continuous map from $E$ to the operators ⟦Flow/Nemytskii · dmap⟧ ⟦Flow/Nemytskii · dmap_apply⟧.

**Theorem (the derivative of $N_Y$).** If $Y$ is $C^1$, then $N_Y$ is differentiable at every $\gamma$, with derivative $\mathcal M_{N_{dY}(\gamma)} : \eta \mapsto \big(s \mapsto dY_{\gamma(s)}\,\eta(s)\big)$. ⟦Flow/Nemytskii · hasFDerivAt_nem⟧

*The proof as formalized.* By the definition of the derivative ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/FDeriv/Basic · hasFDerivAt_iff_isLittleO_nhds_zero⟧, it must be shown that for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $\delta > 0$ such that $\|\eta\| < \delta$ implies
$$
\sup_s \big\| Y(\gamma(s) + \eta(s)) - Y(\gamma(s)) - dY_{\gamma(s)}\, \eta(s) \big\| \le \varepsilon\, \|\eta\|.
$$

1. $dY$ is continuous and $\gamma([0, 1])$ is compact, so by the Heine–Cantor theorem in its form near a compact set ⟦Mathlib/Topology/UniformSpace/HeineCantor · IsCompact.uniformContinuousAt_of_continuousAt⟧ there is $\delta > 0$ with $\|dY_z - dY_{\gamma(s)}\| \le \varepsilon$ whenever $\|z - \gamma(s)\| < \delta$, for every $s$ at once.
2. Let $\|\eta\| < \delta$ and fix $s$. The point $\gamma(s) + \eta(s)$ lies in the ball of radius $\delta$ about $\gamma(s)$. On that convex ball the derivative of $z \mapsto Y(z) - dY_{\gamma(s)} z$ has norm at most $\varepsilon$. The mean value inequality ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/MeanValue · Convex.norm_image_sub_le_of_norm_hasFDerivWithin_le'⟧ bounds the expression above by $\varepsilon \|\eta(s)\| \le \varepsilon \|\eta\|$.

The estimate is uniform in $s$, which is what the supremum norm demands. The operator norm of the derivative is at most $\sup \|dY\|$ ⟦Flow/Nemytskii · norm_pmul_nem_dmap_le⟧.

**Theorem (smoothness of $N_Y$).** If $Y$ is $C^n$, $n \in \mathbb N \cup \{\infty\}$, then $N_Y$ is $C^n$. ⟦Flow/Nemytskii · contDiff_nem_nat⟧ ⟦Flow/Nemytskii · contDiff_nem⟧

*The proof as formalized.* By induction on finite $n$, for all targets $F$ of the size of $E$ at once, since $dY$ takes values in the operators $E \to F$. For $n = 0$ this is continuity. For $n + 1$, the derivative of $N_Y$ is $\gamma \mapsto \mathcal M_{N_{dY}(\gamma)}$. Here $N_{dY}$ is $C^n$ by the induction hypothesis, $dY$ being $C^n$, and $A \mapsto \mathcal M_A$ is continuous linear. A map is $C^{n+1}$ when it has a derivative that is $C^n$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/ContDiff/Defs · contDiff_succ_iff_hasFDerivAt⟧. The case $n = \infty$ follows from all finite cases.

Neither theorem is in Mathlib. Milnor's reference proves smooth dependence by another route, the variational equation.

### I.20 The Volterra operator, and the inverse function theorem in $C([0,1], E)$

**Definitions.** The primitive $P\gamma(s) = \int_0^s \gamma(r)\, dr$ ⟦Flow/Volterra · primFun⟧ ⟦Flow/Volterra · primFun_apply⟧ uses $\gamma$ extended to $\mathbb R$ by composing with the projection of $\mathbb R$ onto $[0, 1]$, and is continuous in $s$ ⟦Mathlib/MeasureTheory/Integral/DominatedConvergence · intervalIntegral.continuous_primitive⟧. Since $s \le 1$, $\|P\gamma\| \le \|\gamma\|$ ⟦Flow/Volterra · norm_primFun_le⟧. $P$ is linear ⟦Flow/Volterra · primₗ⟧, hence a continuous linear operator of norm at most $1$ ⟦Flow/Volterra · prim⟧ ⟦Flow/Volterra · prim_apply⟧ ⟦Flow/Volterra · norm_prim_le⟧ ⟦Flow/Volterra · norm_prim_apply_le⟧, and smooth ⟦Flow/Volterra · contDiff_prim⟧. The *Volterra operator* of a continuous $Y : E \to E$ is
$$
\Psi_Y(\gamma) = \gamma - P(Y \circ \gamma)
$$
⟦Flow/Volterra · volt⟧ ⟦Flow/Volterra · volt_apply⟧. A curve $\gamma$ solves $\gamma' = Y(\gamma)$ on $[0, 1]$ with $\gamma(0) = x$ exactly when $\Psi_Y(\gamma)$ is the constant curve $x$; one direction is checked in §I.21.

**Theorem ($\Psi$ is a homeomorphism).** Let $E$ be complete and $Y$ Lipschitz with constant $c < 1$. Then $\Psi_Y$ is a homeomorphism of $C([0,1], E)$, and its inverse is $(1 - c)^{-1}$-Lipschitz. ⟦Flow/Volterra · voltHomeomorph⟧ ⟦Flow/Volterra · coe_voltHomeomorph⟧

*The proof as formalized.*

1. $\gamma \mapsto P(Y \circ \gamma)$ is $c$-Lipschitz, since $P$ has norm at most $1$ and $N_Y$ is $c$-Lipschitz ⟦Flow/Volterra · dist_prim_nem_le⟧.
2. *The key estimate.* $\|\gamma - \delta\| \le (1 - c)^{-1}\, \|\Psi\gamma - \Psi\delta\|$ ⟦Flow/Volterra · dist_le_dist_volt⟧. Indeed $\gamma - \delta = (\Psi\gamma - \Psi\delta) + \big(P(Y\gamma) - P(Y\delta)\big)$, so $\|\gamma - \delta\| \le \|\Psi\gamma - \Psi\delta\| + c\,\|\gamma - \delta\|$. Hence $\Psi$ is injective ⟦Flow/Volterra · volt_injective⟧.
3. *Surjectivity.* For fixed $\psi$, the map $\gamma \mapsto \psi + P(Y \circ \gamma)$ is a contraction with constant $c$ ⟦Flow/Volterra · contractingWith_volt⟧. $C([0,1], E)$ is complete, so the contraction principle gives a fixed point ⟦Mathlib/Topology/MetricSpace/Contracting · ContractingWith.fixedPoint⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/MetricSpace/Contracting · ContractingWith.fixedPoint_isFixedPt⟧, and this fixed point defines $\Psi^{-1}(\psi)$ ⟦Flow/Volterra · voltInv⟧. Then $\Psi(\Psi^{-1}\psi) = \psi$ ⟦Flow/Volterra · volt_voltInv⟧, and $\Psi^{-1}(\Psi\gamma) = \gamma$ by injectivity ⟦Flow/Volterra · voltInv_volt⟧.
4. $\Psi$ is continuous. By the key estimate, its inverse is $(1 - c)^{-1}$-Lipschitz.

**Theorem ($\Psi^{-1}$ is as smooth as $X$).** Let $E$ be complete and $X : E \to E$ of class $C^n$, $1 \le n \le \infty$, and Lipschitz with constant $c < 1$. Then $\Psi_X^{-1}$ is $C^n$. ⟦Flow/Volterra · contDiff_voltHomeomorph_symm⟧

*The proof as formalized.*

1. $\Psi_X$ is $C^n$, since $N_X$ is (§I.19) and $P$ is continuous linear ⟦Flow/Volterra · contDiff_volt⟧.
2. Its derivative at $\gamma$ is $1 - P\, \mathcal M_{N_{dX}(\gamma)}$ ⟦Flow/Volterra · hasFDerivAt_volt⟧.
3. A $c$-Lipschitz map has $\|dX\| \le c$ everywhere ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/FDeriv/Basic · norm_fderiv_le_of_lipschitz⟧. So the operator $P\, \mathcal M_{N_{dX}(\gamma)}$ has norm at most $1 \cdot c < 1$ ⟦Flow/Volterra · norm_prim_comp_pmul_lt⟧, and the Neumann series ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/SpecificLimits/Normed · Units.oneSub⟧ makes the derivative an invertible operator ⟦Flow/Volterra · voltDeriv⟧ ⟦Flow/Volterra · coe_voltDeriv⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Algebra/Module/Equiv · ContinuousLinearEquiv.ofUnit⟧.
4. A homeomorphism that is $C^n$ with an invertible derivative at every point has a $C^n$ inverse ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/ContDiff/Operations · Homeomorph.contDiff_symm⟧. This is the easy half of the inverse function theorem, available because $\Psi$ is already known to be a homeomorphism.

*Why $c < 1$.* With $c < 1$ the operator $P\, \mathcal M_{N_{dX}(\gamma)}$ is a contraction, so no estimate of its iterates (of the classical form $c^n/n!$) is needed. It costs nothing, because the flow at time $t$ is the time-one map of $(t/N) X$ iterated $N$ times (§I.21).

### I.21 Smooth dependence on the initial point, and the joint smoothness

Let $E$ be complete; Mathlib infers completeness from finite dimension, and the applications have $E = \mathbb R^K$.

**The flow line solves the integral equation.** For $X$ bounded and Lipschitz, hence continuous, the flow line through $x$, restricted to $[0, 1]$, is a point of $C([0, 1], E)$ ⟦Flow/Smooth · IsBddLipschitz.flowCurve⟧ ⟦Flow/Smooth · IsBddLipschitz.flowCurve_apply⟧, and $\Psi_X$ maps it to the constant curve $x$ ⟦Flow/Smooth · IsBddLipschitz.volt_flowCurve⟧. On $[0, s] \subseteq [0, 1]$ the projection does nothing, and the fundamental theorem of calculus ⟦Mathlib/MeasureTheory/Integral/IntervalIntegral/FundThmCalculus · intervalIntegral.integral_eq_sub_of_hasDerivAt⟧ gives $\int_0^s X(\varphi_r x)\, dr = \varphi_s(x) - \varphi_0(x)$, with $\varphi_0(x) = x$. Constant curves form a continuous linear map $x \mapsto \underline{x}$ from $E$ to $C([0, 1], E)$ ⟦Flow/Smooth · constCLM⟧ ⟦Flow/Smooth · constCLM_apply⟧.

**Step 1 (the time-one map of a slow field).** If $X$ is $C^n$ with $n \ge 1$, bounded and Lipschitz with constant $c < 1$, then $\varphi_1$ is $C^n$. ⟦Flow/Smooth · contDiff_flow_one_of_lipschitz⟧

*Proof.* $\Psi_X$ maps the flow line through $x$ to $\underline{x}$, so the flow line is $\Psi_X^{-1}(\underline{x})$ and $\varphi_1(x) = \Psi_X^{-1}(\underline{x})(1)$. This composes the continuous linear map $x \mapsto \underline{x}$, the $C^n$ map $\Psi_X^{-1}$ (§I.20), and evaluation at $1$, which is continuous linear ⟦Mathlib/Topology/ContinuousMap/Algebra · ContinuousMap.evalCLM⟧.

**Step 2 (all times; V1.14).** If $X$ is $C^n$, $1 \le n \le \infty$, and vanishes outside a compact set, then each $\varphi_t$ is $C^n$. ⟦Flow/Smooth · contDiff_flow⟧

*Proof.*

1. $X$ is bounded and Lipschitz with some constant $K$ (§I.18).
2. Choose an integer $N > |t| K$ and put $\tau = t/N$. The field $\tau X$ is $C^n$, bounded and Lipschitz with constant $|\tau| K < 1$.
3. By Step 1, its time-one map is $C^n$. By rescaling (§I.18) that map is $\varphi_\tau$.
4. $\varphi_t = (\varphi_\tau)^N$ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flow_nat_mul⟧, and an iterate of a $C^n$ map is $C^n$ ⟦Flow/Smooth · contDiff_iterate⟧.

**Step 3 (joint smoothness).** If $X$ is $C^n$, $1 \le n \le \infty$, and vanishes outside a compact set, then $(t, x) \mapsto \varphi_t(x)$ is $C^n$ on $\mathbb R \times E$. ⟦Flow/Smooth · contDiff_flow_uncurry⟧

*The idea (the $Z$-trick).* Trade the time variable for a space variable. On $E \times \mathbb R$ consider the field $Z(x, \tau) = (\rho(\tau) X(x), 0)$, where $\rho(\tau) = \tau$ for $|\tau| \le R$ and $\rho$ has compact support. Along a flow line of $Z$ the coordinate $\tau$ is constant, and the $E$-coordinate follows the flow of $X$ at speed $\rho(\tau)$. So the time-one map of $Z$ is $(x, \tau) \mapsto (\varphi_{\rho(\tau)}(x), \tau)$, which for $|\tau| \le R$ is $(\varphi_\tau(x), \tau)$. Step 2, at the single time $1$, now gives smoothness in $(x, \tau)$ jointly.

*The proof as formalized,* at a point $(t_0, x_0)$.

1. Let $R = |t_0| + 1$. A smooth bump function $\chi$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/BumpFunction/Basic · ContDiffBump⟧ equals $1$ on $[-R, R]$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/BumpFunction/Basic · ContDiffBump.one_of_mem_closedBall⟧ and vanishes outside $[-R-1, R+1]$. Then $\rho(\tau) = \chi(\tau)\,\tau$ is smooth with compact support, and $\rho(\tau) = \tau$ for $|\tau| \le R$.
2. $Z(x, \tau) = (\rho(\tau) X(x), 0)$ is $C^n$, and it vanishes outside the product of the supports of $X$ and $\rho$, a compact set. So it is bounded and Lipschitz.
3. For each $(x, \tau)$, the curve $s \mapsto (\varphi_{\rho(\tau) s}(x), \tau)$ is an integral curve of $Z$ through $(x, \tau)$. Its derivative is $(\rho(\tau) X(\varphi_{\rho(\tau)s}(x)), 0)$. Since integral curves are flow lines (§I.18), the time-one map of $Z$ is $(x, \tau) \mapsto (\varphi_{\rho(\tau)}(x), \tau)$.
4. On the open set $|t| < R$, which contains $(t_0, x_0)$, $\rho(t) = t$, so $\varphi_t(x)$ is the first component of $\varphi^Z_1(x, t)$.
5. $\varphi^Z_1$ is $C^n$ by Step 2 applied to $Z$ on $E \times \mathbb R$. So $(t, x) \mapsto \varphi_t(x)$ agrees near $(t_0, x_0)$ with a $C^n$ map, and is $C^n$ there.

**Diffeomorphisms.** Each $\varphi_t$ is a $C^n$ diffeomorphism of $E$ with inverse $\varphi_{-t}$ ⟦Flow/Smooth · flowDiffeomorph⟧ ⟦Flow/Smooth · coe_flowDiffeomorph⟧ ⟦Flow/Smooth · coe_flowDiffeomorph_symm⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/Diffeomorph · Diffeomorph⟧, by Step 2 and the group law.

*What Milnor leaves implicit.* The smooth dependence on the initial condition is a theorem, not a remark. Smoothness of $(t, q) \mapsto \varphi_t(q)$ for large $t$ needs the joint smoothness of the local flow. Milnor's iteration $\varphi_t = (\varphi_{\varepsilon_0/2})^k \circ \varphi_r$, with $k$ and $r$ depending on $t$, gives it only after one checks that the decomposition of $t$ can be chosen locally constant in $t$. Step 3 avoids the issue, and Step 1 is needed only for a fixed time. The argument gives $C^n$ dependence for a $C^n$ field, $1 \le n \le \infty$.

### I.22 Lemma 2.4

**Definitions (Milnor pp. 9–10, on $M = E$; V1.13).** A *one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms* of $E$ is a map $\varphi : \mathbb R \to (E \to E)$ such that $(t, x) \mapsto \varphi_t(x)$ is $C^\infty$, each $\varphi_t$ is a $C^\infty$ diffeomorphism of $E$, and $\varphi_{s+t} = \varphi_s \circ \varphi_t$ ⟦Flow/Smooth · IsOneParameterGroup⟧. A vector field $X$ *generates* $\varphi$ if for every $x$ the curve $h \mapsto \varphi_h(x)$ has derivative $X(x)$ at $h = 0$ ⟦Flow/Smooth · Generates⟧. On $E$ a tangent vector is a vector, so Milnor's $X_q(f) = \lim_{h \to 0} \big(f(\varphi_h(q)) - f(q)\big)/h$ becomes the derivative of $h \mapsto \varphi_h(q)$.

Two facts Milnor leaves implicit:

- $\varphi_0 = \mathrm{id}$ ⟦Flow/Smooth · IsOneParameterGroup.zero⟧. The group law gives $\varphi_0(\varphi_0(x)) = \varphi_0(x)$, and $\varphi_0$ is injective, being a diffeomorphism. With the group law, injectivity of $\varphi_0$ alone would suffice.
- If $X$ generates $\varphi$, every orbit $t \mapsto \varphi_t(x)$ is an integral curve of $X$ ⟦Flow/Smooth · IsOneParameterGroup.isIntegralCurve⟧. Write $\varphi_s(x) = \varphi_{s-t}(\varphi_t(x))$ and differentiate at $s = t$ with the generating property at the point $\varphi_t(x)$. This is Milnor's computation on p. 10, which uses $\varphi_0 = \mathrm{id}$.

**Lemma 2.4 (Milnor, p. 10; V1.15).** Let $E$ be a complete real normed space and $X : E \to E$ a $C^\infty$ vector field that vanishes outside a compact set. Then $X$ generates exactly one one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms of $E$, namely its flow. ⟦Flow/Smooth · lemma_2_4⟧

*The proof as formalized.*

1. *Existence.* The flow $\varphi$ of $X$ is jointly $C^\infty$ (§I.21, Step 3) ⟦Flow/Smooth · contDiff_flow_uncurry⟧. Each $\varphi_t$ is a $C^\infty$ diffeomorphism ⟦Flow/Smooth · flowDiffeomorph⟧. The group law holds ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flow_comp⟧. $X$ generates $\varphi$, because $h \mapsto \varphi_h(x)$ has derivative $X(\varphi_0(x)) = X(x)$ at $h = 0$ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.hasDerivAt_flow⟧.
2. *Uniqueness.* Let $\psi$ be a one-parameter group generated by $X$. Its orbits are integral curves of $X$, and $\psi_0 = \mathrm{id}$. Since every integral curve is a flow line (§I.18) ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.eq_flow⟧, $\psi_t(x) = \varphi_{t - 0}(\psi_0(x)) = \varphi_t(x)$.

*Deviation (narrower), and what is implicit.* Milnor's lemma is about a manifold $M$; the formal one is about $M = E$. If $E$ is infinite-dimensional the hypothesis forces $X = 0$: the support of a continuous field is open, and a non-empty open set in an infinite-dimensional normed space has non-compact closure. So the content is the case $E = \mathbb R^n$. For submanifolds $N \subseteq \mathbb R^K$, Part II uses instead the ambient flow of a field tangent to $N$, kept inside $N$ by the invariance theorem of §I.18. Milnor's statement asserts uniqueness, and his proof leaves it implicit. It holds among all one-parameter groups generated by $X$, because uniqueness of integral curves is global in time.

### I.23 What the formalization taught us

- **The Hessian is the second derivative.** Its symmetry holds at every point where $f$ is $C^2$, and criticality is used exactly once, to kill the first-order term $df(d^2\psi)$ of the second-order chain rule, which is what makes the Hessian independent of the coordinates ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · hessian_comp⟧. Milnor's definition through extending vector fields is replaced by a definition in one chart plus this theorem ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.hessian_comp_symm⟧.
- **The index is Mathlib's negative index of inertia.** Milnor's definition, the maximal dimension of a subspace on which the form is negative definite, is a theorem ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · index_isGreatest⟧, and his argument for the index of the normal form is the uniqueness half of Sylvester's law ⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · index_diagForm_morseSign⟧.
- **Hadamard's lemma needs differentiation under the integral sign on an open set,** which Milnor does not argue. It is proved by domination on a tube around the segment, with no local compactness ⟦Chapter1/Hadamard · contDiffOn_intervalIntegral⟧. The second-order form comes from Taylor's formula, which yields the symmetric coefficient and its value $\tfrac12 d^2f_0$ that Milnor asserts ⟦Chapter1/Hadamard · exists_hadamard_two_on⟧.
- **The lemma of Morse in one step.** The inverse function theorem in the space of linear maps of $E$, applied to $T \mapsto B_0(T\cdot, T\cdot) + B_0(T\cdot, \cdot) - B_0(\cdot, T\cdot)$, diagonalizes a smooth family of quadratic forms at once ⟦Chapter1/SquareRoot · exists_sqrt_family⟧. The gap in Milnor's induction, the unjustified linear change making $H_{rr}(0) \ne 0$, does not arise ⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · exists_morse_chart⟧.
- **That the normal form forces the index uses the independence of the coordinates,** which Milnor takes for granted ⟦Chapter1/MorseLemma · index_eq_of_morse_chart⟧. On a manifold the converse needs no hypothesis on $f$ at all ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · isNondegenerateCriticalPointM_of_morse_chart⟧.
- **Corollary 2.3 needs only $C^2$ at the point and no lemma of Morse** ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · eventually_not_isCriticalPoint⟧. Its global consequence, finitely many critical points in every compact set, which Milnor uses silently, needs no Hausdorff hypothesis ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · finite_inter_setOf_isCriticalPointM⟧.
- **One possible misprint:** in example (d) the critical set of $x^2$, which the text calls the $x$ axis, is the $y$ axis ⟦Chapter1/Examples · Examples.isCriticalPoint_sqFst_iff⟧.
- **On a submanifold the Hessian of $F \circ x$ is not the ambient Hessian restricted.** The first-order term of the chain rule survives, and for $L_p$ it is the second fundamental form ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · hessian_normSq_sub_comp⟧.
- **Smooth dependence on initial conditions is a theorem that Mathlib lacked.** It is proved by the inverse function theorem in $C([0, 1], E)$ ⟦Flow/Volterra · contDiff_voltHomeomorph_symm⟧, with joint smoothness from the field $(\rho(\tau)X(x), 0)$ ⟦Flow/Smooth · contDiff_flow_uncurry⟧. Milnor's iteration, whose well-definedness he leaves unchecked, is replaced by Picard–Lindelöf on intervals of any length ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.exists_isIntegralCurve⟧. The uniqueness in Lemma 2.4 is global in time ⟦Flow/Smooth · lemma_2_4⟧.
