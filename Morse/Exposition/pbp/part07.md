## Part VII. Energy, variations, Jacobi fields and the index theorem (Milnor §§11–15)

Milnor's §§11–15 set up a calculus of variations on the space of paths and use it to compute the index of a geodesic. §11 introduces the path space $\Omega$, its "tangent spaces" and its variations; §12 defines the energy, proves the first variation formula and identifies the critical paths of the energy with the geodesics; §13 defines the Hessian $E_{**}$ at a geodesic through two-parameter variations and proves the second variation formula; §14 introduces Jacobi fields and shows that they are the null vectors of $E_{**}$ and the variation fields of families of geodesics; §15 proves the Morse index theorem: the index of $E_{**}$ is the number of points conjugate to the initial point in the open segment, counted with multiplicity. The formal proof does all of this for a submanifold $N$ of a Euclidean space with the induced metric, the setting of Parts V and VI. Every Riemannian manifold is of this kind by Nash's embedding theorem, which is quoted and not proved. Every numbered statement of §§11–15 is proved, with three exceptions: Examples 1 and 2 of §14, Lemma 12.1, of which only the inequality $E(\gamma) \le E(\omega)$ is proved, and the part of Remark 14.6 about negative parameters and cluster points. Remark 14.5 is proved in a restated form (§VII.17), and the vanishing of the index in Lemma 13.6 in its equivalent form, positive semi-definiteness (§VII.10).

The formal route departs from Milnor's in more places than in any earlier part, and each departure has a reason. **(1)** As in the book, $\Omega$ carries no topology and no manifold structure. $T\Omega_\omega$ is *defined* as a vector space of fields along $\omega$ and is tied to $\Omega$ by variations in both directions: the variation field of every variation lies in it, and every finite family of its elements is the family of variation fields of a single variation (Lemma 11.A). A derivative "on $\Omega$" is always the derivative of a function of finitely many real variables. **(2)** $E_{**}$ is *defined* as twice the right side of Milnor's second variation formula. Theorem 13.1 and Lemma 11.A then show that Milnor's own definition, through two-parameter variations, is well defined and agrees with it. Symmetry and bilinearity come from an integral form, not from the symmetry of the mixed partials of $E \circ \bar\alpha$, a function the book never shows to be of class $C^2$. **(3)** The Jacobi equation is solved without a parallel frame: the formal proof writes it as a first-order linear system for the pair $(J, DJ/dt)$ in $V \times V$, with a correction term that keeps tangent data tangent, and obtains global solutions from Grönwall's inequality. **(4)** Lemma 14.4 is proved from initial data rather than from end points. This avoids minimal geodesics, the restriction to a short initial segment, and a step that Milnor's proof leaves unargued. **(5)** The two properties of a "sufficiently fine" subdivision, and Assertion (2), both come from a single Poincaré-type estimate instead of from minimal geodesics. **(6)** $\lambda(\tau)$ is defined as the index of $E_{**}$ restricted to the fields that vanish outside $[0, \tau]$, so no Hessian on $[0, \tau]$ and no rescaling is needed. **(7)** Theorem 15.1 needs the conjugate points to be finite in number before the jumps of $\lambda$ can be added up, and the formal proof establishes this first.

### VII.1 The setting: tangent projections, jumps and covariant derivatives

Throughout, $V$ is a finite-dimensional real inner product space (Milnor's $\mathbb R^K$) and $N \subseteq V$ is a slice submanifold modelled on a finite-dimensional space $Z$ with complement $W$ (§§0.1, 0.3) ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · IsSliceSubmanifold⟧. We write $n = \dim Z$. $N$ carries the induced metric. It need not be closed, compact or connected, and no completeness is assumed anywhere in this part. For $x \in N$ the tangent space $T_xN \subseteq V$ is the range of the derivative of the inclusion ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · tangentSubspace⟧. That derivative is injective, so $\dim T_xN = n$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · finrank_tanAt⟧.

**The tangent projection.** The orthogonal projection of $V$ onto $T_xN$ is $P_x$ (Part V, §V.11) ⟦Chapter6/Projection · tanProj⟧. The formal proofs use a version defined at every point of $V$: $P_x$ for $x \in N$, and $P_x = 0$ for $x \notin N$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · prj⟧. Paths lie in $N$ only for $t \in [0, 1]$, and integrands are easier to handle when $x \mapsto P_x$ is defined everywhere. The basic facts are that $P_xv = v$ if and only if $v \in T_xN$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · prj_eq_self_iff⟧, that $P_x^2 = P_x$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · prj_prj⟧, and that $P_x$ is self-adjoint ⟦Chapter8/Energy · inner_prj_left⟧. Hence $\langle v, P_xw\rangle = \langle v, w\rangle$ for tangent $v$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · inner_prj_right_of_eq⟧. Below, this identity is what lets an ordinary derivative replace a covariant one inside an inner product with a tangent field.

Two lemmas about $P$ are used throughout the part.

*(a) Derivatives of maps into $N$ are tangent, including derivatives within a set.* If $g$ maps a set $s$ into $N$ and has derivative $g'$ within $s$ at a point $p \in s$ at which $s$ has unique derivatives, then $g'(w) \in T_{g(p)}N$ for every $w$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · hasFDerivWithinAt_mem_tangentSubspace⟧. The proof takes the slice chart $\sigma$ chosen at $g(p)$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · sliceAt⟧. Near $p$ in $s$, $g(x)$ lies in the domain of $\sigma$ and in $N$, so the $W$-component of $\sigma(g(x))$ vanishes. Its derivative within $s$ at $p$ is therefore both the $W$-component of $d\sigma \circ g'$ and $0$, and the two agree because derivatives within $s$ are unique. Vanishing of the $W$-component of $d\sigma(g'w)$ characterizes $T_{g(p)}N$. The one-variable form ⟦Chapter8/Energy · hasDerivWithinAt_mem_tangentSubspace⟧ makes one-sided velocities at break points tangent, and also the one-sided $t$-derivatives along the edges of the strips used below.

*(b) Along a smooth map into $N$, $P$ is smooth.* If $g$ is $C^\infty$ within $s$ at $p$ and maps $s$ into $N$, then so is $x \mapsto P_{g(x)}$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · contDiffWithinAt_prj_comp⟧ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · ContDiffOn.prj_comp⟧. Near $g(p)$, $P$ coincides on $N$ with a smooth map defined on an open subset of $V$, the local ambient extension built from a slice chart and written $\mathrm{projRep}_\sigma$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · projRep⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · projRep_eq⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · contDiffAt_projRep⟧, and the composite with $g$ is smooth. In particular $t \mapsto P_{\omega(t)}$ is piecewise smooth along a piecewise smooth path in $N$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · IsPiecewiseSmooth.prj_comp⟧.

**The second fundamental form and the curvature.** $\mathrm{II}_x(v, w) = (dP)_x(v)\,w$ is the derivative of $P$ along $N$ in the direction $v$, applied to $w$ (Part V, §V.14) ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · secondFundForm⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN⟧. For tangent $v$ it is the derivative of the extension $\mathrm{projRep}_\sigma$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_tanProj_eq_fderiv⟧. It is symmetric on tangent vectors ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · secondFundForm_comm⟧, takes two tangent vectors to a normal one ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_tanProj_mem_orthogonal_of_mem⟧ and a normal vector to a tangent one ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_tanProj_mem_of_mem_orthogonal⟧, and satisfies $P\,\mathrm{II}(v, Pw) = 0$ for tangent $v$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · tanProj_dN_tanProj⟧. The curvature tensor is
$$
R(a, b)c = \mathrm{II}\big(b, \mathrm{II}(a, c)\big) - \mathrm{II}\big(a, \mathrm{II}(b, c)\big)
$$
at $x \in N$ (Part V, §V.16) ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvOp⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvOp_apply⟧, extended by $0$ off $N$ ⟦Chapter8/SecondVariation · curvR⟧ ⟦Chapter8/SecondVariation · curvR_of_mem⟧. This is Milnor's sign convention, $R(X, Y)Z = -\nabla_X\nabla_YZ + \nabla_Y\nabla_XZ + \nabla_{[X,Y]}Z$ (p. 51; Part V, §V.9), which is opposite to that of many modern texts. With it the unit sphere has curvature $+1$, that is $\langle R(a, b)a, b\rangle = \|a\|^2\|b\|^2 - \langle a, b\rangle^2$, and the Gauss equation reads $\langle R(x, y)z, w\rangle = \langle\mathrm{II}(x, z), \mathrm{II}(y, w)\rangle - \langle\mathrm{II}(x, w), \mathrm{II}(y, z)\rangle$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · inner_curvOp⟧. For tangent arguments $R(a, b)c$ is tangent ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvOp_mem⟧.

**Paths and piecewise smoothness.** A path, and likewise a vector field along a path, is a map $\mathbb R \to V$ of which only the values on $[0, 1]$ matter (§0.8). As everywhere in Mathlib, $c'(t)$ denotes the derivative of $c$ at $t$ where it exists and $0$ where it does not. A map $c$ is *piecewise smooth* on $[a, b]$ (Part VI, §VI.10) if $a \le b$, $c$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, and there is a finite set $B$ of break points such that $c$ is $C^\infty$, with one-sided derivatives at the ends, on every closed interval $[s, t] \subseteq [a, b]$ whose interior contains no point of $B$ ⟦Chapter7/Path · IsPiecewiseSmooth⟧. Such an interval is called a *piece*. The class is closed under the operations this part needs. A smooth function of two piecewise smooth maps is piecewise smooth, with the union of the two break sets ⟦Chapter8/Energy · IsPiecewiseSmooth.map₂⟧; hence so are sums, products with scalar functions, and applications of operator-valued maps ⟦Chapter8/Energy · IsPiecewiseSmooth.add⟧ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · IsPiecewiseSmooth.smul⟧ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · IsPiecewiseSmooth.clm_apply⟧. On a piece, the derivative computed within the piece is smooth and equals the derivative at interior points ⟦Chapter8/Energy · IsPiecewiseSmooth.derivWithin_piece⟧.

**Jumps.** The jump of the derivative of $f$ at $t$ is $\Delta_tf = f'(t^+) - f'(t^-)$, the difference of the right and the left derivative ⟦Chapter8/Energy · jump⟧. It vanishes where $f$ is differentiable ⟦Chapter8/Energy · jump_eq_zero⟧ and depends only on $f$ near $t$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · jump_congr⟧. If $f$ is smooth on a piece $[s, t]$, the right derivative at $s$ and the left derivative at $t$ are the derivatives within the piece ⟦Chapter8/Energy · derivWithin_Ici_eq_of_piece⟧ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · derivWithin_Iic_eq_of_piece⟧, and at a point of the piece where $f$ is differentiable the derivative within the piece is the derivative ⟦Chapter8/Energy · derivWithin_eq_deriv_of_piece⟧.

**Covariant derivative and acceleration.** Along a path $c$, the covariant derivative of a field $X$ is $\frac{DX}{dt}(t) = P_{c(t)}X'(t)$ ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · covD⟧. For a curve with values in $N$ this is the covariant derivative of the Levi-Civita connection of the induced metric constructed in Part V (§§V.13, V.17) ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · covD_eq_covDeriv⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · covDeriv⟧. The acceleration is $A = \frac{D}{dt}c'$ ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · accel⟧. Both are meaningful only where the derivatives exist, which for piecewise smooth data means everywhere except at finitely many points.

### VII.2 Calculus on closed strips

Milnor's proofs of the variation formulas differentiate under the integral sign, integrate by parts "on each $[t_{i-1}, t_i]$" and add up the results (pp. 71–72, 74–76). The file `Chapter8/Piecewise` proves what these steps rest on.

**Induction over the pieces.** Let $B$ be a finite set and let $\mathcal P(a, b)$ be a property of intervals $[a, b] \subseteq [lo, hi]$. Suppose $\mathcal P$ holds for every interval whose interior contains no point of $B$, and that $\mathcal P(a, x)$ and $\mathcal P(x, b)$ imply $\mathcal P(a, b)$ whenever $a < x < b$ and $x \in B$. Then $\mathcal P(a, b)$ holds for all $lo \le a < b \le hi$ ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · piecewise_induction⟧. The proof is an induction on the number of points of $B$ in $(a, b)$. If there is such a point $x$, then $(a, x)$ and $(x, b)$ each contain fewer. Some companion facts locate pieces: a point of $(a, b]$ has a piece ending at it, a point of $[a, b)$ has a piece starting at it, a point of $(a, b)$ outside $B$ lies in the interior of a piece, and every point of $[a, b]$ lies in some piece ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · exists_piece_left⟧ ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · exists_piece_right⟧ ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · exists_piece_around⟧ ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · exists_piece_mem⟧. Each piece is found by taking the largest point of $B \cup \{a\}$ below the given point and the smallest point of $B \cup \{b\}$ above it.

Three gluing results follow from the induction.

1. A function continuous on every piece of $[lo, hi]$ is continuous on $[lo, hi]$, because a function continuous on two closed intervals is continuous on their union ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · continuousOn_of_piecewise⟧.
2. If on every piece $[s, t]$ the function $g$ agrees on $(s, t)$ with a function continuous on $[s, t]$, then $g$ is integrable on every $[a, b] \subseteq [lo, hi]$ ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · intervalIntegrable_of_piecewise⟧.
3. *The telescoping.* Suppose that on every piece $[s, t]$ one has $\int_s^t g = F^-(t) - F^+(s)$, where $F^-$ and $F^+$ are functions of the end point, the left and the right boundary values. Then
$$
\int_a^b g = F^-(b) - F^+(a) - \sum_{x \in B \cap (a, b)} \big(F^+(x) - F^-(x)\big)
$$
⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · integral_eq_of_piecewise⟧. The proof is the induction again, using additivity of the integral over adjacent intervals and the splitting of the sum at the gluing point ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · sum_filter_Ioo_split⟧. In the variation formulas $F^\pm(x) = \langle Y(x), f'(x^\pm)\rangle$ for a continuous field $Y$, and the sum becomes $\sum_x\langle Y(x), \Delta_xf\rangle$. This is how the boundary terms of the integrations by parts "telescope into the jump terms".

**Partial derivatives on a closed strip.** Let $S = U \times [a, b]$ with $U \subseteq \mathbb R$ open and $a < b$. For $F$ defined on $S$, $\partial F/\partial u$ and $\partial F/\partial t$ are the derivative of $F$ within $S$, applied to $(1, 0)$ and to $(0, 1)$ ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · pdU⟧ ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · pdT⟧. Since $S$ has unique derivatives ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · uniqueDiffOn_strip⟧, both are smooth on $S$ when $F$ is ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · contDiffOn_pdU⟧ ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · contDiffOn_pdT⟧. They are honest partial derivatives. $\frac{\partial F}{\partial u}(u, t)$ is the derivative of $u \mapsto F(u, t)$ at $u$, because the $u$-direction is open ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · hasDerivAt_pdU⟧ ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · deriv_eq_pdU⟧. $\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}$ is the derivative within $[a, b]$ of $t \mapsto F(u, t)$ ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · hasDerivWithinAt_pdT⟧: the two-sided derivative inside ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · hasDerivAt_pdT⟧, the right derivative on the edge $t = a$ and the left derivative on the edge $t = b$ ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · derivWithin_Ici_eq_pdT⟧ ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · derivWithin_Iic_eq_pdT⟧.

**Symmetry of the mixed partials on the closed strip, edges included.** For $F$ smooth on $S$, $\partial_u\partial_tF = \partial_t\partial_uF$ at every point of $S$ ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · pdU_pdT⟧. Both sides are values of the second derivative within $S$, namely at the pairs $((1, 0), (0, 1))$ and $((0, 1), (1, 0))$, because the derivative of $q \mapsto D_SF(q)v$ is $D_S(D_SF)(\cdot)\,v$. Mathlib's symmetry theorem for second derivatives within a set applies at every point of the closure of the interior of a set with unique derivatives ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/FDeriv/Symmetric · ContDiffWithinAt.isSymmSndFDerivWithinAt⟧. The interior of $S$ is $U \times (a, b)$, whose closure contains $S$. Milnor never comments on this point. It matters because the $t$-derivatives on the edges are one-sided, and the jump terms are made of exactly these derivatives.

**Differentiation under the integral sign on a closed strip.** Let $F$ and $F'$ be continuous on $S$, with $F'(u, t)$ the derivative in $u$ of $F$ at every $(u, t)$, $t \in (a, b)$. Then $u \mapsto \int_a^bF(u, t)\,dt$ has derivative $\int_a^bF'(u_0, t)\,dt$ at every $u_0 \in U$ ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · hasDerivAt_integral_strip⟧. To prove it, choose $\delta$ with the $\delta$-ball around $u_0$ inside $U$. Then $F'$ is bounded by some $M$ on the compact set $\bar B(u_0, \delta/2) \times [a, b]$. This constant is the domination required by Mathlib's theorem on differentiation of parametric integrals ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/ParametricIntervalIntegral · hasDerivAt_integral_of_dominated_loc_of_deriv_le⟧. That theorem needs the hypothesis on derivatives only for almost every $t$, so the end point $t = b$, a null set, is excluded. The piecewise version ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · hasDerivAt_integral_of_piecewise⟧ assumes that on every piece $[s, t]$ the integrand $g(u, \tau)$ agrees, for $\tau \in (s, t)$, with a function of class $C^1$ on the closed strip $U \times [s, t]$. It concludes that $u \mapsto \int_a^bg(u, \tau)\,d\tau$ has derivative $\int_a^b\partial_ug(u_0, \tau)\,d\tau$. On each piece this is the strip theorem applied to the $C^1$ representative and its partial derivative in $u$, and replacing $g$ by the representative inside the piece does not change the integrals. The pieces are then glued by the induction and additivity, with integrability of $g$ and of $\partial_ug$ coming from the second gluing result. Milnor uses all of this without comment. The compactness behind the domination is the reason the strips must be closed in $t$.

### VII.3 The path space, its "tangent spaces", and variations

**Definition (Milnor p. 67; V8.1; §0.8).** $\Omega(N; p, q)$ is the set of maps $c : \mathbb R \to V$ that are piecewise smooth on $[0, 1]$, with $c(t) \in N$ for $t \in [0, 1]$, $c(0) = p$ and $c(1) = q$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · pathSpace⟧. For a map $\omega$, $T\Omega_\omega$ is the set of fields $X : \mathbb R \to V$ that are piecewise smooth on $[0, 1]$, tangent along $\omega$ ($P_{\omega(t)}X(t) = X(t)$ for $t \in [0, 1]$), and vanish at $0$ and $1$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · pathTangent⟧ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · mem_pathTangent⟧. It is a real vector space, by the closure properties of §VII.1 and the linearity of each $P_x$, and it depends only on $\omega$ on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · mem_pathTangent_congr⟧. The break points of a field need not be those of $\omega$.

*How $T\Omega_\omega$ is formalized.* Milnor thinks of $\Omega$ as a kind of infinite-dimensional manifold (p. 67) but gives it a topology only in §16. The formal proof follows him. No topology or manifold structure is put on $\Omega$, and $T\Omega_\omega$ is not a tangent space of anything: it is the vector space just defined. It is connected to $\Omega$ through variations, in two directions. The variation field of every variation of $\omega$ lies in $T\Omega_\omega$ (below), and every finite family of elements of $T\Omega_\omega$ is the family of variation fields of one variation (Lemma 11.A, §VII.5). So each statement Milnor makes about derivatives on $\Omega$ becomes a statement about the derivatives of functions $u \mapsto E(\bar\alpha(u))$ of one or two real variables. The second half of the connection is what turns these statements into statements about all of $T\Omega_\omega$. Elements of $T\Omega_\omega$ are functions on $\mathbb R$, and only their values on $[0, 1]$ enter the first and second variations ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · firstVariation_congr_field⟧ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · secondVariation_congr⟧. This convention has one visible consequence, met in §VII.14: the literal null space of the formal $E_{**}$ contains every field vanishing on $[0, 1]$, and the nullity has to be read on $[0, 1]$.

**Definition (Milnor p. 68; V8.2).** Let $\mathcal P$ be a real normed space, $U \subseteq \mathcal P$ an open set containing $0$, and $B$ a finite set. A map $\alpha(u, t)$, $u \in \mathcal P$, $t \in \mathbb R$, is a *variation with break points $B$* if $\alpha(u, t) \in N$ for $u \in U$ and $t \in [0, 1]$; the end points $\alpha(u, 0)$ and $\alpha(u, 1)$ do not depend on $u \in U$; and $(u, t) \mapsto \alpha(u, t)$ is $C^\infty$ on every closed strip $U \times [s, t]$ with $0 \le s < t \le 1$ and no point of $B$ in $(s, t)$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · IsVariation⟧. It is a variation *of* the path $\alpha(0, \cdot)$. For $\mathcal P = \mathbb R$ and $U = (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$ this is Milnor's definition; §13 uses $\mathcal P = \mathbb R^2$. The formal definition is more general than the book's, since it allows any open parameter set in any normed space.

Each path $\alpha(u, \cdot)$, $u \in U$, is piecewise smooth, as the restriction of the strip smoothness to the segment $\{u\} \times [s, t]$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · IsVariation.isPiecewiseSmooth⟧, and lies in $\Omega(N; \alpha(0, 0), \alpha(0, 1))$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · IsVariation.mem_pathSpace⟧. A variation can be reparametrized: for a smooth $\varphi$ from another normed space into $\mathcal P$ with $\varphi(0) \in U$, the map $u \mapsto \alpha(\varphi(u), \cdot)$ is a variation on $\varphi^{-1}(U)$ with the same break points ⟦Chapter8/Energy · IsVariation.comp⟧, its strip smoothness coming from composition with $(u, t) \mapsto (\varphi(u), t)$. The slices $u_2 \mapsto \alpha(u_1, u_2, \cdot)$ of a two-parameter variation, the restriction $u_1 \mapsto \alpha(u_1, 0, \cdot)$, and Milnor's $(u_1, u_2) \mapsto \bar\alpha(u_1 + u_2)$ of Remark 13.5 are all instances of this.

**Definition.** The *variation field* of a one-parameter variation is $W_t = \frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial u}(0, t)$, the derivative of $u \mapsto \alpha(u, t)$ at $0$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · varField⟧. For a one-parameter variation, $u \mapsto \alpha(u, t)$ is differentiable at every $u \in U$ when $t \in [0, 1]$, since $t$ lies in some piece and the derivative there is $\partial_u$ on the strip ⟦Chapter8/Energy · IsVariation.hasDerivAt_param⟧. Moreover $\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial u}(u, t) \in T_{\alpha(u, t)}N$, as the velocity of a curve in $N$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · IsVariation.prj_varDeriv⟧.

**Lemma ("clearly $W \in T\Omega_\omega$", Milnor p. 68; V8.2).** The variation field of a variation of $\omega$ lies in $T\Omega_\omega$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · IsVariation.varField_mem⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* On a piece $[s, t]$, $W$ coincides on the whole closed piece with $\tau \mapsto \partial_u\alpha(0, \tau)$ computed within the strip ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · deriv_eq_pdU⟧, and that function is smooth (§VII.2). Hence $W$ is smooth on every piece. Continuity at a break point $t_i$ comes for free, which is the content hidden behind Milnor's "clearly". $W(t_i)$ is the derivative of the single curve $u \mapsto \alpha(u, t_i)$, and the two adjacent strips both compute that same derivative. So $W$ is a single function, smooth on every closed piece and therefore continuous ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · continuousOn_of_piecewise⟧. Tangency is the previous fact at $u = 0$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · IsVariation.prj_varDeriv⟧. At $t = 0$ and $t = 1$ the curve $u \mapsto \alpha(u, t)$ is constant near $0$, so its derivative vanishes.

### VII.4 Energy and Schwarz's inequality

**Definition (Milnor p. 70; V8.5; §0.8).** For a map $c$ into a normed space and $a \le b$, the energy is $E_a^b(c) = \int_a^b\|c'(t)\|^2\,dt$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · energy⟧, and $E = E_0^1$. The length is $L_a^b(c) = \int_a^b\|c'(t)\|\,dt$ ⟦Chapter7/Path · pathLength⟧ (Part VI, §VI.10). For piecewise smooth $c$ the function $\|c'\|^2$ is integrable, because on each piece it agrees inside with the squared norm of the derivative within the piece, which is continuous ⟦Chapter8/Energy · IsPiecewiseSmooth.intervalIntegrable_norm_deriv_sq⟧. Piecewise smooth paths are admissible in the sense of Part VI (§VI.10), so that their speed is integrable ⟦Chapter7/Path · IsPiecewiseSmooth.isAdmissible⟧ ⟦Chapter7/Path · IsAdmissible⟧.

**Lemma 12.A of the exposition (Schwarz's inequality and its equality case; V8.5).** Let $c$ be piecewise smooth on $[a, b]$. Then $(L_a^b)^2 \le (b - a)E_a^b$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · pathLength_sq_le_energy⟧, with equality if and only if there is a number $k$ such that $\|c'(t)\| = k$ at every $t \in (a, b)$ at which $c$ is differentiable ⟦Chapter8/Energy · pathLength_sq_eq_energy_iff⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* It runs through the variance identity
$$
\int_a^b\big(\|c'\| - k\big)^2 = E_a^b - 2kL_a^b + k^2(b - a),
$$
obtained by expanding, all terms being integrable ⟦Chapter8/Energy · integral_norm_deriv_sub_sq⟧. For $a < b$ put $k = L/(b - a)$, the mean speed. Then $(b - a)(E - 2kL + k^2(b - a)) = (b - a)E - L^2$, and the left side is $(b - a)$ times the integral of a square, so it is nonnegative. For the equality case, equality makes that integral zero, so $(\|c'\| - k)^2 = 0$ almost everywhere. Turning this into a statement at a given $t \in (a, b)$ where $c$ is differentiable takes a continuity argument. Take a piece $[s, t]$ ending at $t$ ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · exists_piece_left⟧. On it, the function $(\|c'_{[s,t]}\| - k)^2$ built from the derivative within the piece is continuous on $[s, t]$ and almost everywhere $0$ on $(s, t)$, hence $0$ on all of $[s, t]$, which lies in the closure of its interior. At $t$ the derivative within the piece is $c'(t)$, because $c$ is differentiable at $t$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · derivWithin_eq_deriv_of_piece⟧. Conversely, a constant speed $k$ gives $L = k(b - a)$ ⟦Chapter7/Path · pathLength_eq_of_norm_deriv⟧ and $E = k^2(b - a)$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · energy_eq_of_norm_deriv⟧, the speed being $k$ off a countable set.

*A hidden hypothesis.* Milnor's equality case reads "$\|d\omega/dt\|$ constant", that is, parameter proportional to arc length. For a speed that is only piecewise continuous, "constant" can only mean constant away from the break points, which is the formal statement. *Deviation (stronger).* Any $a \le b$ (Milnor: $0 \le a < b \le 1$) and any normed target space. The inequality of Lemma 12.1 is proved in §VII.10, where it is used.

### VII.5 Lemma 11.A: a variation for every family of fields

Milnor observes on p. 68 that every $W \in T\Omega_\omega$ is the variation field of some variation, namely of $\bar\alpha(u)(t) = \exp_{\omega(t)}(uW_t)$. On p. 74 he takes two-parameter variations with given fields without comment. The exposition states both as Lemma 11.A, for any number of fields. The formal proof proves it with Milnor's formula, after supplying the one missing ingredient: a radius $\varepsilon$ that works uniformly along $\omega$.

Recall from Part VI (§VI.6; see also §0.9) that $\exp_q(v) = \gamma(1)$ for the geodesic $\gamma$ defined on an open interval containing $[0, 1]$ with $\gamma(0) = q$ and $\gamma'(0) = v$, and $\exp_q(v) = q$ if there is no such geodesic ⟦Chapter7/Exp · exp⟧. The pairs $(q, v)$ for which the geodesic exists form the *domain* of $\exp$ ⟦Chapter7/Exp · expDom⟧. Two facts from Part VI (§VI.7) are used. First, $\exp$ is defined and smooth on an open neighbourhood of the zero section: there is an open set $O \subseteq V \times V$ containing every $(q, 0)$, $q \in N$, such that every point of $TN \cap O$ is in the domain of $\exp$, and near every point of $TN \cap O$, $\exp$ agrees on $TN$ with a smooth map defined on an open subset of $V \times V$ ⟦Chapter7/Exp · exists_isOpen_exp⟧. Here $TN$ is the set of pairs $(q, v)$ with $q \in N$ and $v \in T_qN$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter8/TangentBundle · tangentBundleSet⟧. Second, $d(\exp_q)_0$ is the inclusion of $T_qN$ in $V$ ⟦Chapter7/Exp · hasFDerivAt_exp_zero⟧.

**Lemma 11.A of the exposition (V8.3).** Let $\omega \in \Omega(N; p, q)$, let $\mathcal P$ be a real normed space, and let $\Lambda(t) : \mathcal P \to V$ be continuous linear maps, depending piecewise smoothly on $t \in [0, 1]$, with values in $T_{\omega(t)}N$ and with $\Lambda(0) = \Lambda(1) = 0$. There are $\varepsilon > 0$ and a finite set $B$ such that
$$
\alpha(u, t) = \exp_{\omega(t)}\big(\Lambda(t)u\big), \qquad \|u\| < \varepsilon,
$$
is a variation with break points $B$, with $\alpha(0, t) = \omega(t)$ and $\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial u}(0, t) = \Lambda(t)$ for $t \in [0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · exists_variation⟧.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. *A uniform radius.* The set $K = \{(\omega(t), 0) : t \in [0, 1]\}$ is compact, as the continuous image of $[0, 1]$, and it is contained in $O$. Some $\delta$-thickening of $K$ therefore lies in $O$. Since the distance on $V \times V$ is the maximum of the two distances, $(\omega(t), v) \in O$ whenever $t \in [0, 1]$ and $\|v\| < \delta$.
2. *A bound for $\Lambda$.* $\|\Lambda(t)\| \le C$ on $[0, 1]$ for some $C \ge 0$, by continuity on a compact interval. Put $\varepsilon = \delta/(C + 1)$. Then $\|\Lambda(t)u\| \le C\varepsilon < \delta$ for $\|u\| < \varepsilon$.
3. *Values in $N$.* Hence $(\omega(t), \Lambda(t)u) \in TN \cap O$ for $t \in [0, 1]$ and $\|u\| < \varepsilon$. The pair is in the domain of $\exp$, and $\alpha(u, t) \in N$ because it is the value at time $1$ of a geodesic in $N$ ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · exp_mem_of_mem_expDom⟧ ⟦Chapter7/Exp · exp_eq_of_mem_expDom⟧.
4. *Fixed end points.* Since $\Lambda(0) = \Lambda(1) = 0$, $\alpha(u, 0) = \exp_{\omega(0)}(0)$ for every $u$, and likewise at $t = 1$.
5. *Smoothness on the strips.* Take for $B$ the union of the break points of $\omega$ and of $\Lambda$. On a strip $\{\|u\| < \varepsilon\} \times [s, t]$ free of $B$, the map $(u, \tau) \mapsto (\omega(\tau), \Lambda(\tau)u)$ is smooth. Near each point of the strip, $\exp$ agrees on $TN$ with a smooth map $F$ ⟦Chapter7/Exp · exists_isOpen_exp⟧, so $\alpha$ agrees near that point, within the strip, with the composite of $F$ with a smooth map, and is smooth there.
6. *The derivative at $u = 0$.* $\alpha(0, t) = \exp_{\omega(t)}(0) = \omega(t)$ ⟦Chapter7/Exp · exp_zero⟧. Regard $\Lambda(t)$ as a linear map into $T_{\omega(t)}N$ and compose it with $d(\exp_{\omega(t)})_0$, which is the inclusion. The chain rule gives $\Lambda(t)$ as the derivative in $u$ at $0$.

*What Milnor leaves implicit.* $\exp_{\omega(t)}(uW_t)$ makes sense only near the zero section (Lemma 10.2). A uniform $\varepsilon$ therefore has to be found, here by the compactness of $\omega([0, 1])$ together with a bound for $\|\Lambda\|$. Completeness of $N$ is never used.

Two special cases are what the rest of the part uses. For one field $W \in T\Omega_\omega$, take $\Lambda(t)u = uW(t)$, which is piecewise smooth as the image of $W$ under a fixed continuous linear map ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · IsPiecewiseSmooth.smulRight⟧. The variation $\exp_{\omega(t)}(uW(t))$, $|u| < \varepsilon$, then has variation field $W$ on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · exists_variation_one⟧. For two fields $W_1, W_2$, take $\Lambda(t)(u_1, u_2) = u_1W_1(t) + u_2W_2(t)$, which gives a two-parameter variation with $\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial u_i}(0, 0, t) = W_i(t)$, the partial derivatives being derivatives along the coordinate axes by the chain rule ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · exists_variation_two⟧.

### VII.6 The first variation formula

**Definition (Milnor p. 71; V8.7).** The *first variation* of a path $\omega$ in the direction of a field $X$ is
$$
\mathrm{FV}(\omega, X) = -\sum_{0 < t < 1}\langle X(t), \Delta_t\omega'\rangle - \int_0^1\langle X(t), A(t)\rangle\,dt,
$$
with $A$ the acceleration ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · firstVariation⟧. The sum runs over all of $(0, 1)$ and is Mathlib's sum of the finitely many nonzero terms (by convention $0$ if there are infinitely many). If $\omega$ is differentiable off a finite set $B$, it equals the finite sum over $B \cap (0, 1)$ ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · finsum_jump_eq_sum⟧ ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · finsum_mem_Ioo_eq_sum⟧. $\mathrm{FV}(\omega, X)$ depends only on $X$ on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · firstVariation_congr_field⟧. Milnor writes the sum over the break points $t_i$. The formal sum runs over the whole open interval, where its terms vanish away from the break points.

The proof works on one strip at a time, and a group of small lemmas translates between a variation and its strips. Inside a piece, with $c = \alpha(u_0, \cdot)$ and $Y = \frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial u}(u_0, \cdot)$, one has $c' = \partial_t\alpha$, $Y = \partial_u\alpha$ on the closed piece, $Y' = \partial_t\partial_u\alpha$ and $c'' = \partial_t\partial_t\alpha$, all partials computed within the strip ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · piece_deriv⟧ ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · piece_varDeriv⟧ ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · piece_deriv_varDeriv⟧ ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · piece_deriv_deriv⟧. The one-sided velocities of $c$ at the ends of a piece are $\partial_t\alpha$ on the edges ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · piece_derivWithin_Ici⟧ ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · piece_derivWithin_Iic⟧. Away from the break points, both $c$ and $Y$ are differentiable ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · IsVariation.differentiableAt⟧ ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · IsVariation.differentiableAt_varDeriv⟧.

**Formula (12.2′) of the exposition: the first variation at every $u$ (V8.9).** Let $\alpha$ be a one-parameter variation with parameter set $U \subseteq \mathbb R$. For every $u_0 \in U$, the function $u \mapsto E(\alpha(u, \cdot))$ is differentiable at $u_0$, and
$$
\frac12\,\frac{d}{du}E\big(\alpha(u, \cdot)\big)\Big|_{u = u_0} = -\sum_t\Big\langle\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial u}, \Delta_t\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial t}\Big\rangle - \int_0^1\Big\langle\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial u}, \frac{D}{\partial t}\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial t}\Big\rangle dt,
$$
everything evaluated at $(u_0, t)$. In other words, the derivative is $2\,\mathrm{FV}(\alpha(u_0, \cdot), \frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial u}(u_0, \cdot))$ ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · IsVariation.hasDerivAt_energy⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Write $c = \alpha(u_0, \cdot)$ and $Y = \frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial u}(u_0, \cdot)$.

1. *Differentiation under the integral sign.* On each piece, the integrand $\|\partial_t\alpha(u, \tau)\|^2$ agrees inside with the same expression computed within the strip, which is smooth on the closed strip. So by the piecewise theorem of §VII.2 ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · hasDerivAt_integral_of_piecewise⟧, $\frac{d}{du}E(\alpha(u))(u_0) = \int_0^1\partial_u\big(\|\partial_t\alpha\|^2\big)(u_0, \tau)\,d\tau$.
2. *The integrand.* Inside a piece, $\partial_u\|\partial_t\alpha\|^2 = 2\langle\partial_u\partial_t\alpha, \partial_t\alpha\rangle = 2\langle\partial_t\partial_u\alpha, \partial_t\alpha\rangle = 2\langle Y', c'\rangle$ at $u_0$, by the symmetry of the mixed partials on the strip ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · pdU_pdT⟧. Milnor's proof here uses Lemma 8.3 and "Lemma 8.5", that is, $\frac{D}{\partial u}\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial t} = \frac{D}{\partial t}\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial u}$. The reference should be to Lemma 8.7, since Lemma 8.5 is the definition of a symmetric connection. In $V$ no projection is needed at this stage.
3. *Integration by parts on each piece.* On a piece $[s, t]$, the function $\tau \mapsto \langle Y(\tau), c'(\tau)\rangle$, with the strip's partials in place of $Y$ and $c'$, is continuous on the closed piece and has derivative $\langle Y', c'\rangle + \langle Y, c''\rangle$ inside. Hence $\int_s^t(\langle Y', c'\rangle + \langle Y, c''\rangle) = \langle Y(t), c'(t^-)\rangle - \langle Y(s), c'(s^+)\rangle$. The telescoping of §VII.2 ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · integral_eq_of_piecewise⟧ gives
$$
\int_0^1\big(\langle Y', c'\rangle + \langle Y, c''\rangle\big) = \langle Y(1), c'(1^-)\rangle - \langle Y(0), c'(0^+)\rangle - \sum_{x \in B \cap (0, 1)}\langle Y(x), \Delta_xc'\rangle.
$$
4. *The ends.* $Y(0) = Y(1) = 0$, because $u \mapsto \alpha(u, 0)$ and $u \mapsto \alpha(u, 1)$ are constant near $u_0$.
5. *The covariant derivative appears.* $Y$ is tangent ⟦Chapter8/Energy · IsVariation.prj_varDeriv⟧, so $\langle Y, c''\rangle = \langle Y, P c''\rangle = \langle Y, A\rangle$ on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · inner_prj_right_of_eq⟧.
6. *Assembly.* Off the finite set $B \cup \{1\}$ the integrand of step 1 is $2\langle Y', c'\rangle$. Its integral is $2\big(-\sum_x\langle Y(x), \Delta_xc'\rangle - \int_0^1\langle Y, A\rangle\big)$, which is $2\,\mathrm{FV}(c, Y)$.

**Theorem 12.2 (the first variation formula, Milnor p. 71; V8.9).** Let $\alpha$ be a variation with $\alpha(0, t) = \omega(t)$ for $t \in [0, 1]$ and variation field $W$. Then $u \mapsto E(\alpha(u, \cdot))$ has derivative $2\,\mathrm{FV}(\omega, W)$ at $u = 0$, that is,
$$
\frac12\,\frac{dE(\bar\alpha(u))}{du}(0) = -\sum_t\langle W_t, \Delta_tV\rangle - \int_0^1\langle W_t, A_t\rangle\,dt
$$
⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · first_variation⟧.

*Proof.* This is (12.2′) at $u_0 = 0$ ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · IsVariation.hasDerivAt_energy⟧. The first variation of $\alpha(0, \cdot)$ equals that of $\omega$, because jumps and accelerations at points of $(0, 1)$ depend only on the path near those points, and $\alpha(0, \cdot) = \omega$ on $[0, 1]$. *Remarks.* In the scan, the leading minus sign of the displayed statement (p. 71) is lost; the end of the proof on p. 72 has it. Milnor states the formula at $u = 0$ only, but his proof gives it at every $u$, and §13 needs it there: the proof of the second variation formula differentiates (12.2′) in a second parameter (§VII.8).

### VII.7 Critical paths: Corollary 12.3

**Definitions (Milnor pp. 69, 73; V8.4, V8.10).** A map $c$ is a *critical path* of the energy if, for every variation $\alpha$ (any parameter set, any break points) with $\alpha(0, t) = c(t)$ on $[0, 1]$, the function $u \mapsto E(\alpha(u, \cdot))$ has derivative $0$ at $u = 0$ ⟦Chapter8/Critical · IsCriticalPath⟧. Milnor defines critical paths for every function on $\Omega$; the formal definition is for $E$ only, which is what Corollary 12.3 needs. A map $\gamma$ is an *(unbroken) geodesic* if it is $C^\infty$ on $[0, 1]$, with one-sided derivatives at the ends, and is a geodesic on $(0, 1)$ ⟦Chapter8/Critical · IsUnbrokenGeodesic⟧. Here (Part VI, §VI.2) a geodesic on an open set $s$ is a curve that is smooth on $s$, takes values in $N$ there, and has acceleration normal to $N$: $\gamma''(t) \perp T_{\gamma(t)}N$ ⟦Chapter7/Geodesic · IsGeodesicOn⟧. This is Milnor's $\frac{D}{dt}\frac{d\gamma}{dt} = 0$, since $D/dt = P \circ d/dt$.

**Criticality through the first variation (V8.10).** For $c \in \Omega(N; p, q)$, $c$ is critical if and only if $\mathrm{FV}(c, X) = 0$ for every $X \in T\Omega_c$ ⟦Chapter8/Critical · isCriticalPath_iff⟧. Suppose $c$ is critical and $X \in T\Omega_c$. Lemma 11.A gives the variation $\exp_{c(t)}(uX(t))$ ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · exists_variation_one⟧. Criticality says $u \mapsto E$ has derivative $0$ at $0$, Theorem 12.2 says the derivative is $2\,\mathrm{FV}(c, W)$ ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · first_variation⟧ with $W = X$ on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · firstVariation_congr_field⟧, and derivatives are unique. Conversely, if $\mathrm{FV}(c, \cdot)$ vanishes on $T\Omega_c$, Theorem 12.2 gives derivative $0$ for any variation, whose variation field lies in $T\Omega_c$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · IsVariation.varField_mem⟧. The first direction needs Lemma 11.A, which is hidden behind Milnor's "clearly".

**A geodesic is critical** (Milnor p. 73, "clearly"). For an unbroken geodesic, $\mathrm{FV}(\gamma, X) = 0$ for every field $X$ ⟦Chapter8/Critical · IsUnbrokenGeodesic.firstVariation_eq_zero⟧. The jumps vanish because $\gamma$ is differentiable on $(0, 1)$, and the integrand vanishes on $(0, 1)$ because $P\gamma'' = 0$ there ⟦Chapter7/Geodesic · IsGeodesicOn.tanProj_deriv_deriv⟧.

**Corollary 12.3 (Milnor p. 73; V8.11).** A path $c \in \Omega(N; p, q)$ is a critical path of $E$ if and only if it is an unbroken geodesic ⟦Chapter8/Critical · corollary_12_3⟧.

*The idea* is Milnor's. Test fields concentrated on one piece make the acceleration vanish there; test fields concentrated at a break point make the velocity jump vanish; and a path that is a geodesic on both sides of a point and $C^1$ across it is a geodesic across it. The formal proof does the three steps separately. Let $c$ be critical, with break points $B$.

*Step 1: the acceleration vanishes off $B$* ⟦Chapter8/Critical · accel_eq_zero_of_critical⟧. Let $x \in (0, 1) \setminus B$, lying inside a piece $(s, s')$. Put $r = \min(x - s, s' - x)/2$ and let $\varphi$ be a smooth bump function centred at $x$, equal to $1$ on $[x - r/2, x + r/2]$ and supported in $(x - r, x + r)$ ⟦Chapter8/Critical · bumpAt⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/BumpFunction/Basic · ContDiffBump⟧. The acceleration $A = P_{c}c''$ is smooth on $(s, s')$. The test field $X = \varphi A$ is smooth on all of $\mathbb R$, because $\varphi$ vanishes outside a compact subset of $(s, s')$, where $A$ is smooth ⟦Chapter8/Critical · contDiff_smul_of_tsupport_subset⟧. It is tangent, since $PA = A$, and it vanishes at $0$ and $1$. So $X \in T\Omega_c$ and $\mathrm{FV}(c, X) = 0$. The jump terms vanish: inside $(s, s')$ the path is differentiable, and outside it $\varphi = 0$. What remains is $\int_0^1\varphi\|A\|^2 = 0$. The integrand is continuous and nonnegative, hence zero on $(0, 1)$, and at $x$, where $\varphi(x) = 1$, this gives $A(x) = 0$. Milnor uses $W = f(t)A(t)$ with $f$ positive off the break points. That field must be checked to be continuous at the $t_i$, where the one-sided accelerations differ. The formal proof avoids the issue by treating one point at a time.

*Step 2: no velocity jumps* ⟦Chapter8/Critical · jump_eq_zero_of_critical⟧. At $x \notin B$ the path is differentiable. Let $x \in B$, with pieces $[s, x]$ and $[x, s']$ on either side, and $\Delta = \Delta_xc'$. The one-sided velocities of a path in $N$ are tangent by lemma (a) of §VII.1, hence so is $\Delta$ ⟦Chapter8/Critical · prj_jump⟧. Take the test field $X(t) = \varphi(t)P_{c(t)}\Delta$, with $\varphi$ a bump at $x$ of radius $r = \min(x - s, s' - x)/2$. It is piecewise smooth, being a smooth function times the piecewise smooth $t \mapsto P_{c(t)}\Delta$, tangent, and zero at the ends. In $\mathrm{FV}(c, X) = 0$ the integral term vanishes by Step 1, the acceleration being zero off $B \cup \{1\}$ ⟦Chapter8/Critical · accel_eq_zero_of_critical⟧. The sum reduces to the term at $x$: at any other $t$, either $\varphi(t) = 0$, or $t$ lies in $(x - r, x)$ or in $(x, x + r)$, where $c$ is differentiable. The term at $x$ is $\langle P\Delta, \Delta\rangle = \|\Delta\|^2$. Hence $\Delta = 0$. Milnor asks for a variation whose field takes the value $\Delta_{t_i}V$ at $t_i$; this needs an explicit field, and here the projection $P$ plays the role that parallel transport would play on an abstract manifold.

*Step 3: across a former break point, by uniqueness of geodesics* ⟦Chapter8/Critical · isGeodesicOn_of_glue⟧. Let $\gamma$ be smooth on $[x - r, x]$ and on $[x, x + r]$, a geodesic on $(x - r, x)$ and on $(x, x + r)$, with $\gamma(x) \in N$ and $\gamma$ differentiable at $x$. Then $\gamma$ is a geodesic on a neighbourhood of $x$.

1. *The lift.* Let $Q = \mathrm{projRep}_\sigma$ for the slice chart $\sigma$ at $\gamma(x)$, and $G(y, v) = (v, dQ_y(v)v)$ on $V \times V$ ⟦Chapter8/Critical · liftField⟧. By the geodesic equation $\gamma'' = \mathrm{II}(\gamma', \gamma')$ of Part VI (§VI.2) ⟦Chapter7/Geodesic · IsGeodesicOn.deriv_deriv_eq⟧, rewritten with $\mathrm{II}(v, \cdot) = dQ(v)$, the lift $f = (\gamma, \gamma')$ satisfies $f' = G(f)$ on both sides of $x$ ⟦Chapter8/Critical · IsGeodesicOn.hasDerivAt_lift⟧. $G$ is $C^1$ near $f(x)$ ⟦Chapter8/Critical · contDiffAt_liftField⟧.
2. *The lift solves the system at $x$ too.* $\gamma'$ is continuous at $x$: from each side the derivative within the piece is continuous and equals $\gamma'(x)$ at $x$, since $\gamma$ is differentiable there. So $f$ is continuous at $x$ and $f' = G \circ f$ on a punctured neighbourhood of $x$, with $G \circ f$ continuous at $x$. A derivative extension theorem, applied from the right and from the left ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/FDeriv/Extend · hasDerivWithinAt_Ici_of_tendsto_deriv⟧, shows that $f$ is differentiable at $x$ with $f'(x) = G(f(x))$.
3. *Comparison.* Let $\sigma$ be the geodesic through $\gamma(x)$ at time $x$ with velocity $\gamma'(x)$ ⟦Chapter8/Critical · exists_isGeodesicOn_through⟧. It comes from Lemma 10.2 (Part VI, §VI.5) ⟦Chapter7/GeodesicLocal · lemma_10_2⟧, which gives geodesics $\Gamma(q, w, \cdot)$ on $(-2, 2)$ for $\|w\| < \varepsilon$, by rescaling: with $k = 2(\|v\| + 1)/\varepsilon$ and $w = v/k$, the curve $\sigma(t) = \Gamma(q, w, k(t - x))$ has $\sigma(x) = q$ and $\sigma'(x) = v$, and is a geodesic on $(x - 1/k, x + 1/k)$. The lifts of $\gamma$ and $\sigma$ solve $f' = G(f)$ near $x$ with the same value at $x$, so by local uniqueness for an autonomous $C^1$ system (Part VI, §VI.3) ⟦Chapter7/ODE · ode_eventuallyEq⟧ they agree near $x$. Hence $\gamma = \sigma$ near $x$, and $\gamma$ is a geodesic there.

*Assembly.* Near each $x \in (0, 1)$, $c$ is a geodesic. If $x \notin B$, this is Step 1 on the interior of the piece, because a smooth path in $N$ with zero acceleration is a geodesic ⟦Chapter8/Critical · isGeodesicOn_of_accel⟧. If $x \in B$, Step 2 makes the left and right derivatives agree ⟦Chapter8/Critical · jump_eq_zero_of_critical⟧, so they combine into a derivative at $x$, and Step 3 applies ⟦Chapter8/Critical · isGeodesicOn_of_glue⟧. Being a geodesic is a local property ⟦Chapter7/Minimal · isGeodesicOn_of_locally⟧, so $c$ is a geodesic on $(0, 1)$. It is smooth on $[0, 1]$: near $0$ and $1$ by the first and last pieces, and inside because it is a geodesic.

*What Milnor leaves implicit.* The book settles the last step by an appeal to uniqueness for differential equations (p. 73). At a former break point the path is known to be a geodesic only on each side. Before uniqueness can be applied, the lift has to be shown to solve the geodesic system at the break point itself, which is the derivative-extension step 2.

### VII.8 The curvature identity and the second variation formula

Milnor proves the second variation formula by differentiating (12.2′) in a second parameter. He rearranges the jump terms (p. 75) and, through Lemmas 8.7 and 9.2, reaches the identity (13.3), $\frac{D}{\partial u_1}\frac{D}{\partial t}V = \frac{D^2W_1}{dt^2} + R(V, W_1)V$. The formal proof follows the same plan. It needs (13.3) for a surface that is defined only on the strips, and it needs the jump terms to be differentiable in the parameter.

**The curvature identity (13.3) (V8.12 (i)).** Let $F(u, t)$ be smooth on an open set $O \subseteq \mathbb R^2$ with values in $N$. At every point of $O$,
$$
\frac{D}{\partial u}\frac{D}{\partial t}\frac{\partial F}{\partial t} = \frac{D}{\partial t}\frac{D}{\partial t}\frac{\partial F}{\partial u} + R\Big(\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}, \frac{\partial F}{\partial u}\Big)\frac{\partial F}{\partial t},
$$
where $\frac{D}{\partial u}X = P(\partial X/\partial u)$ and $\frac{D}{\partial t}X = P(\partial X/\partial t)$ ⟦Chapter8/SecondVariation · curvature_identity⟧. The partial derivatives are the ordinary ones of Part V (§V.20) ⟦Chapter6/Surface · partialX⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Surface · partialY⟧.

*The proof as formalized* is a direct computation in $V$, with the second fundamental form doing the work of Lemmas 8.7 and 9.2. Fix $p_0$, shrink $O$ so that $F$ lands in the domain of the slice chart $\sigma$ at $F(p_0)$, and let $Q = \mathrm{projRep}_\sigma \circ F$, which equals $P_F$ there. Write $a = \partial F/\partial t$, $b = \partial F/\partial u$, and $D_vw = \mathrm{II}_{F(p_0)}(v, w)$.

1. $a$ and $b$ are tangent everywhere, as derivatives of a map into $N$, so $Qa = a$ and $Qb = b$. By the chain rule, $\partial_uQ(p_0) = D_{b}$ and $\partial_tQ(p_0) = D_{a}$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_tanProj_eq_fderiv⟧.
2. *Normal parts.* Differentiating $a = Qa$ in $t$ by the product rule for partial derivatives ⟦Chapter6/Surface · partialY_clm_apply⟧ gives $\partial_ta = D_aa + P\partial_ta$, and likewise $\partial_tb = D_ab + P\partial_tb$.
3. *Symmetry of mixed partials* at an interior point ⟦Chapter8/SecondVariation · partialX_partialY_of_contDiffOn⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/FDeriv/Symmetric · ContDiffAt.isSymmSndFDerivAt⟧, used once for $a$ and once for $F$ near $p_0$: $\partial_u\partial_ta = \partial_t\partial_tb$. This is Lemma 8.7 in $V$.
4. *The left side.* $P\partial_u(Q\partial_ta) = P D_b(\partial_ta) + P\partial_u\partial_ta$. By step 2, $P D_b(\partial_ta) = P D_b(P\partial_ta) + P D_b(D_aa)$. The first term is $0$ because $P D_bP = 0$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · tanProj_dN_tanProj⟧. In the second, $D_aa$ is normal, so $D_b(D_aa)$ is tangent and $P$ fixes it. The left side is therefore $D_bD_aa + P\partial_u\partial_ta$.
5. *The first term on the right* is, in the same way, $D_aD_ab + P\partial_t\partial_tb$.
6. By steps 3–5, the difference of the two is $D_bD_aa - D_aD_ab$. Since $D_ab = D_ba$ (symmetry of $\mathrm{II}$), this is $\mathrm{II}(b, \mathrm{II}(a, a)) - \mathrm{II}(a, \mathrm{II}(b, a)) = R(a, b)a$ by the definition of $R$ (§VII.1).

Milnor applies Lemma 9.2, which Part V proves for surfaces defined on all of $\mathbb R^2$, to a variation defined only on strips. Proving the identity at an interior point of an arbitrary open set removes the difficulty.

**Definition (V8.13).** For a path $\gamma$ with velocity $V = \gamma'$ and fields $X_1, X_2$,
$$
\Phi(X_1, X_2) = -\sum_{0 < t < 1}\Big\langle X_2(t), \Delta_t\frac{DX_1}{dt}\Big\rangle - \int_0^1\Big\langle X_2, \frac{D^2X_1}{dt^2} + R(V, X_1)V\Big\rangle\,dt,
$$
where $\Delta_t\frac{DX_1}{dt} = P_{\gamma(t)}(\Delta_tX_1')$ ⟦Chapter8/SecondVariation · secondVariation⟧. Since $DX_1/dt = PX_1'$ and $P$ is continuous along $\gamma$, the jump of $DX_1/dt$ is the projection of the jump of $X_1'$.

**Jumps under differentiation (V8.12 (ii)).** For a one-parameter variation $\alpha$, a parameter $u_0 \in U$ and $x \in (0, 1)$, the jump $\Delta_x\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial t}(u, \cdot)$ is differentiable in $u$ at $u_0$, with derivative $\Delta_x\big(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial u}\big)(u_0, \cdot)$ ⟦Chapter8/SecondVariation · IsVariation.hasDerivAt_jump⟧. Take the pieces $[s, x]$ and $[x, s']$ on either side of $x$. The jump is the difference between $\partial_t\alpha(u, x)$ computed within the right strip and within the left strip. Each is smooth in $u$, and its $u$-derivative is $\partial_u\partial_t$ on that strip. By the symmetry of the mixed partials *on the edge of the strip* ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · pdU_pdT⟧, this equals $\partial_t\partial_u$, which is the one-sided derivative at $x$ of $t \mapsto \partial_u\alpha(u_0, t)$. This is what Milnor calls "rearranging the second term" (p. 75).

For a two-parameter variation, $\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial u_2}$ on the slice $u_2 = 0$ agrees, on each strip, with a function of $(u_1, \tau)$ that is smooth on the closed strip, namely the derivative of $\alpha$ within the strip applied to the direction $u_2$ ⟦Chapter8/SecondVariation · IsVariation.exists_param₂⟧.

**Theorem 13.1 (the second variation formula, Milnor p. 74; V8.13).** Let $\gamma$ be an unbroken geodesic and $\alpha(u_1, u_2, t)$ a two-parameter variation, with parameter set $U \subseteq \mathbb R^2$, such that $\alpha(0, 0, t) = \gamma(t)$ on $[0, 1]$. Let $W_1 = \frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial u_1}(0, 0, \cdot)$ and $W_2 = \frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial u_2}(0, 0, \cdot)$. Then $u_1 \mapsto \frac{\partial}{\partial u_2}E(\alpha(u_1, u_2, \cdot))\big|_{u_2 = 0}$ is differentiable at $u_1 = 0$, with derivative $2\Phi(W_1, W_2)$ ⟦Chapter8/SecondVariation · second_variation⟧:
$$
\frac12\,\frac{\partial^2E}{\partial u_1\,\partial u_2}(0, 0) = -\sum_t\Big\langle W_2(t), \Delta_t\frac{DW_1}{dt}\Big\rangle - \int_0^1\Big\langle W_2, \frac{D^2W_1}{dt^2} + R(V, W_1)V\Big\rangle\,dt.
$$

*The factor and the sign.* Milnor's right side is *half* the mixed second derivative, so the Hessian is $E_{**} = 2\Phi$, and this factor reappears in every formula below. The curvature term carries Milnor's sign. With the opposite convention it would read $-R(V, W_1)V$, and the same is true of the Jacobi equation. The mixed second derivative is formalized as the iterated derivative $\partial/\partial u_1$ of $\partial E/\partial u_2$ along $u_2 = 0$, which is exactly what Milnor's proof computes. Its existence is part of the conclusion, and $E \circ \bar\alpha$ is not assumed to be $C^2$. The scan drops the "$-\sum_t$" in front of the first term of the displayed statement on p. 75; (13.2) on the same page has it.

*The proof as formalized.* Let $U_1 = \{u_1 : (u_1, 0) \in U\}$, $\sigma(u_1) = \alpha(u_1, 0, \cdot)$, and $Y(u_1)(t) = \frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial u_2}(u_1, 0, t)$. By reparametrization ⟦Chapter8/Energy · IsVariation.comp⟧, $\sigma$ is a variation of $\gamma$ with variation field $W_1$, and $W_2$, the field of the slice $u_2 \mapsto \alpha(0, u_2)$, is tangent along $\gamma$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · IsVariation.varField_mem⟧.

1. *(12.2′) at every $u_1$.* For $u_1 \in U_1$, the slice $u_2 \mapsto \alpha(u_1, u_2, \cdot)$ is a variation, and (12.2′) at $u_2 = 0$ gives $\frac{\partial E}{\partial u_2}(u_1, 0) = 2\,\mathrm{FV}(\sigma(u_1), Y(u_1))$ ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · IsVariation.hasDerivAt_energy⟧. It therefore suffices to differentiate $u_1 \mapsto \mathrm{FV}(\sigma(u_1), Y(u_1))$ at $0$. This is where Milnor's proof uses (12.2′) away from $u = 0$.
2. *The jump terms.* $\mathrm{FV}(\sigma(u_1), Y(u_1))$ contains $-\sum_{x \in B}\langle Y(u_1)(x), \Delta_x\sigma(u_1)'\rangle$. The map $u_1 \mapsto Y(u_1)(x)$ is differentiable, by the smooth representative on the strip to the right of $x$ ⟦Chapter8/SecondVariation · IsVariation.exists_param₂⟧. By the product rule, the derivative of each term at $0$ is $\langle\partial_{u_1}Y(x), \Delta_x\gamma'\rangle + \langle W_2(x), \partial_{u_1}\Delta_x\sigma(u_1)'\rangle$. The first part vanishes because the geodesic $\gamma$ is differentiable at $x$. The second part is $\langle W_2(x), \Delta_xW_1'\rangle$ by the lemma on jumps under differentiation ⟦Chapter8/SecondVariation · IsVariation.hasDerivAt_jump⟧, and this equals $\langle W_2(x), P\Delta_xW_1'\rangle$ because $W_2$ is tangent.
3. *The integral term.* On each piece, the integrand $\langle Y(u_1)(\tau), P_{\sigma(u_1)(\tau)}\partial_t\partial_t\sigma(u_1, \tau)\rangle$ agrees inside with a function that is smooth on the closed strip: the representative of $\partial\alpha/\partial u_2$ from the lemma above, paired with $P \circ \sigma$ applied to the second $t$-derivative computed within the strip. Differentiation under the integral sign ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · hasDerivAt_integral_of_piecewise⟧ gives the derivative $\int_0^1\partial_{u_1}(\ldots)(0, \tau)\,d\tau$.
4. *The integrand at $u_1 = 0$.* Fix $t \notin B$ in a piece $(s, s')$ and work on the open set $U_1 \times (s, s')$, where all partials are ordinary. By the product rule, the derivative is $\langle\partial_{u_1}Y, \mathrm{Acc}(0, t)\rangle + \langle W_2(t), \partial_{u_1}\mathrm{Acc}(0, t)\rangle$, with $\mathrm{Acc} = P(\partial_t\partial_t\sigma)$. Here $\mathrm{Acc}(0, t)$ is the acceleration of $\gamma$, which is $0$. The second term equals $\langle W_2, P\,\partial_{u_1}\mathrm{Acc}\rangle$ because $W_2$ is tangent. By the curvature identity at $(0, t)$ ⟦Chapter8/SecondVariation · curvature_identity⟧ it equals $\big\langle W_2, P\partial_t\big(P\partial_t\partial_{u_1}\sigma\big) + R(\partial_t\sigma, \partial_{u_1}\sigma)\partial_t\sigma\big\rangle$. On the line $u_1 = 0$ one has $\partial_{u_1}\sigma = W_1$, $\partial_t\sigma = \gamma'$, and $P\partial_t\partial_{u_1}\sigma = DW_1/dt$ near $t$, so $P\partial_t$ of the latter is $D^2W_1/dt^2$. The integrand is therefore $\langle W_2, \frac{D^2W_1}{dt^2} + R(V, W_1)V\rangle$.
5. *Assembly.* The sum in $\Phi$ reduces to the finite sum over $B$, since $W_1$ is differentiable off $B$, and the integrands of steps 3 and 4 agree off the finite set $B \cup \{1\}$.

*What Milnor leaves implicit:* (12.2′) at every $(u_1, 0)$; the differentiation of the jump and integral terms in $u_1$, which needs smoothness on closed strips and the symmetry of mixed partials on their edges; the curvature identity on strips; and the fact that $\frac{D}{\partial t}\frac{D}{\partial u_1}V$ at $u_1 = 0$ is $\frac{D^2W_1}{dt^2}$, because $D/\partial t$ along $u_1 = 0$ involves only values on that line.

**Well-definedness (Milnor p. 74).** Two two-parameter variations of $\gamma$ whose variation fields agree on $[0, 1]$ have the same $\frac{\partial^2E}{\partial u_1\partial u_2}(0, 0)$ ⟦Chapter8/SecondVariation · second_variation_eq⟧. The reason is that $\Phi$ depends only on the fields on $[0, 1]$: jumps and covariant derivatives at interior points are local.

**Remark 13.5 (Milnor p. 76; V8.17).** Let $\alpha$ be a one-parameter variation of the unbroken geodesic $\gamma$ with field $W$. Then $u \mapsto \frac{d}{du}E(\alpha(u))$ is differentiable at $0$, with derivative $2\Phi(W, W)$ ⟦Chapter8/SecondVariation · remark_13_5⟧. Milnor's proof works as stated. $\beta(u_1, u_2) = \alpha(u_1 + u_2)$ is a two-parameter variation ⟦Chapter8/Energy · IsVariation.comp⟧ with both fields equal to $W$, so Theorem 13.1 applies to it ⟦Chapter8/SecondVariation · second_variation⟧, and $\frac{\partial}{\partial u_2}E(\beta(u_1, u_2))\big|_{u_2 = 0} = \frac{dE(\alpha)}{du}(u_1)$, the derivative of a translate.

### VII.9 The Hessian $E_{**}$: integral form, bilinearity, symmetry

**Definition (Milnor p. 74; V8.14; §0.9).** For $\gamma \in \Omega(N; p, q)$, the Hessian is the bilinear form on $T\Omega_\gamma$
$$
E_{**}(W_1, W_2) = 2\Phi(W_1, W_2)
$$
⟦Chapter8/Hessian · energyHessian⟧ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · energyHessian_apply⟧, with bilinearity proved below. It is defined for every $\gamma \in \Omega$. Only at a geodesic is it the second derivative of $E$, and there it agrees with Milnor's definition, in three senses. (i) The variation fields of every two-parameter variation of $\gamma$ lie in $T\Omega_\gamma$ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · IsVariation.partial₁_mem⟧ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · IsVariation.partial₂_mem⟧, and Milnor's $\frac{\partial^2E}{\partial u_1\partial u_2}(0, 0)$ exists and equals $E_{**}(W_1, W_2)$ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · hasDerivAt_energyHessian⟧. (ii) Every pair $W_1, W_2 \in T\Omega_\gamma$ arises in this way, by Lemma 11.A for two fields ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · exists_variation_two⟧. (iii) Remark 13.5, in terms of $E_{**}$: $\frac{d^2}{du^2}E(\bar\alpha(u))(0) = E_{**}(W, W)$ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · hasDerivAt_deriv_energy⟧. $E_{**}$ depends only on the fields on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · secondVariation_congr⟧.

**Proposition 13.A of the exposition (the Hessian as an integral; V8.15).** For $\gamma \in \Omega(N; p, q)$ and $X_1, X_2 \in T\Omega_\gamma$,
$$
\frac12E_{**}(X_1, X_2) = \int_0^1\Big(\Big\langle\frac{DX_1}{dt}, \frac{DX_2}{dt}\Big\rangle - \langle R(V, X_1)V, X_2\rangle\Big)\,dt
$$
⟦Chapter8/Hessian · hessForm⟧ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · secondVariation_eq_hessForm⟧ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · energyHessian_eq_integral⟧. $\gamma$ need not be a geodesic. The formula is not in Milnor's book.

*The proof as formalized.* Take a common subdivision $B$ for $\gamma$, $X_1$ and $X_2$, the union of their break sets ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · exists_pieceData⟧. On each piece $[s, t]$ the three maps are smooth, $\gamma$ lies in $N$, and the fields are tangent ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · PieceData⟧. On a piece, $DX = P X'_{[s,t]}$, built from the derivative within the piece ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · PieceData.D⟧, is smooth on the closed piece ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · PieceData.contDiffOn_D⟧ and equals the covariant derivative inside ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · PieceData.D_eq⟧; the same holds for $D^2X$ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · PieceData.covD_covD_eq⟧.

1. *Integration by parts on a piece* (Lemma 8.3):
$$
\int_s^t\Big(\Big\langle\frac{DX_2}{dt}, \frac{DX_1}{dt}\Big\rangle + \Big\langle X_2, \frac{D^2X_1}{dt^2}\Big\rangle\Big) = \langle X_2(t), PX_1'(t^-)\rangle - \langle X_2(s), PX_1'(s^+)\rangle
$$
⟦Chapter8/Hessian · PieceData.integral_eq⟧. The derivative of $\langle X_2, DX_1\rangle$ is $\langle X_2', DX_1\rangle + \langle X_2, (DX_1)'\rangle$. Since $P$ is self-adjoint and $P^2 = P$, $\langle X_2', P(\ldots)\rangle = \langle DX_2, DX_1\rangle$. Since $X_2$ is tangent, $\langle X_2, (DX_1)'\rangle = \langle X_2, P(DX_1)'\rangle = \langle X_2, D^2X_1\rangle$.
2. *Integrability.* The curvature term is continuous on the closed piece ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · PieceData.continuousOn_curv⟧. Near $\gamma(\tau_0)$, $R(a, b)c$ is an expression in the derivative of $\mathrm{projRep}_\sigma$ at $\gamma(\tau)$, valid for tangent $a$ and $b$ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · continuousWithinAt_curvR⟧. All integrands are thus integrable by the gluing of §VII.2 ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · intervalIntegrable_of_piecewise⟧.
3. *The telescoping* ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · integral_eq_of_piecewise⟧, with $X_2$ vanishing at the ends: $\int_0^1(\langle DX_2, DX_1\rangle + \langle X_2, D^2X_1\rangle) = -\sum_x\langle X_2(x), P\Delta_xX_1'\rangle$.
4. *Substitution.* In $\Phi = -\sum - \int\langle X_2, D^2X_1\rangle - \int\langle X_2, R(V, X_1)V\rangle$ the jump terms cancel, leaving $\int\langle DX_2, DX_1\rangle - \int\langle R(V, X_1)V, X_2\rangle$.

**Corollary 13.4 (Milnor p. 76; V8.16).** For $\gamma \in \Omega(N; p, q)$, $E_{**}$ is a symmetric bilinear form on $T\Omega_\gamma$ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · energyHessian_isSymm⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* *Symmetry.* For tangent $v, w_1, w_2$ at a point of $N$, $\langle R(v, w_1)v, w_2\rangle = \langle R(v, w_2)v, w_1\rangle$ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · inner_curvR_comm⟧. This is Lemma 9.3 (4) with $X = Z = V$, obtained here from the Gauss equation: the left side is $\langle\mathrm{II}(v, v), \mathrm{II}(w_1, w_2)\rangle - \langle\mathrm{II}(v, w_2), \mathrm{II}(w_1, v)\rangle$, which is symmetric in $w_1, w_2$ by the symmetry of $\mathrm{II}$. Hence the integrand of the integral form is symmetric wherever $\gamma$ is differentiable, its velocity then being tangent, and the integral form is symmetric ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · hessForm_comm⟧. *Linearity in the second argument.* Where the fields are differentiable, which is almost everywhere ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · IsPiecewiseSmooth.ae_differentiableAt⟧, the covariant derivative is additive and homogeneous ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · covD_add⟧ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · covD_smul⟧. With integrability ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · intervalIntegrable_hessIntegrand⟧ this gives linearity in $X_2$ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · hessForm_add_right⟧ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · hessForm_smul_right⟧. *Linearity in the first argument* follows by symmetry, and this is how $E_{**}$ is constructed as a bilinear form: Proposition 13.A, then symmetry, then linearity in the second argument.

*What Milnor leaves implicit.* Milnor derives symmetry from $\frac{\partial^2E}{\partial u_1\partial u_2} = \frac{\partial^2E}{\partial u_2\partial u_1}$, which needs $E \circ \bar\alpha$ to be $C^2$ near $(0, 0)$, a point he does not address. Bilinearity in $W_1$ is not visible in his formula without knowing that $W \mapsto \Delta_t\frac{DW}{dt}$ and $W \mapsto \frac{D^2W}{dt^2}$ are linear on piecewise smooth fields, which involves one-sided differentiability at the break points. The integral form gives both properties at once.

### VII.10 The inequality of Lemma 12.1, and Lemma 13.6

**Definition (V8.6).** A path $\gamma$ is *minimal* from $p$ to $q$ if $\gamma \in \Omega(N; p, q)$ and $L(\gamma) \le L(\omega)$ for every $\omega \in \Omega(N; p, q)$ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · IsMinimalPath⟧. Up to affine reparametrization, an equivalence that is not stated formally, this is Milnor's notion.

**The inequality of Lemma 12.1 (Milnor p. 70; V8.6).** If $\gamma$ is minimal and an unbroken geodesic, then $E(\gamma) \le E(\omega)$ for every $\omega \in \Omega(N; p, q)$ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · energy_le_of_isMinimalPath⟧.

*Proof.* This is Milnor's chain $E(\gamma) = L(\gamma)^2 \le L(\omega)^2 \le E(\omega)$. A geodesic has constant speed on $(0, 1)$ ⟦Chapter7/Geodesic · IsGeodesicOn.norm_deriv_eq⟧, so Lemma 12.A holds with equality for $\gamma$ (with $b - a = 1$) ⟦Chapter8/Energy · pathLength_sq_eq_energy_iff⟧ and with inequality for $\omega$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · pathLength_sq_le_energy⟧. Minimality gives $0 \le L(\gamma) \le L(\omega)$. Completeness serves in the book only to guarantee that a minimal geodesic exists (Part VI, §§VI.18–VI.19); it plays no role in the inequality. The value $d^2$ of the minimum and the converse (a path of energy $d^2$ is a minimal geodesic) are not formalized.

**Lemma 13.6 (Milnor p. 76; V8.18).** If $\gamma$ is a minimal unbroken geodesic, then $E_{**}(W, W) \ge 0$ for every $W \in T\Omega_\gamma$ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · lemma_13_6⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Suppose $E_{**}(W, W) < 0$. Lemma 11.A gives the variation $\alpha(u, t) = \exp_{\gamma(t)}(uW(t))$, $|u| < \varepsilon$ ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · exists_variation_one⟧. Put $f(u) = E(\alpha(u))$.

1. $f$ has a local minimum at $0$. Every $\alpha(u)$ lies in $\Omega(N; p, q)$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · IsVariation.mem_pathSpace⟧, so $f(u) \ge E(\gamma) = f(0)$ by the inequality above ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · energy_le_of_isMinimalPath⟧, the energy depending only on the values on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · energy_congr⟧.
2. $f$ is differentiable at $0$ by (12.2′) ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · IsVariation.hasDerivAt_energy⟧, so $f'(0) = 0$. Its second derivative at $0$ exists and equals $E_{**}(W, W)$ by Remark 13.5 ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · hasDerivAt_deriv_energy⟧, since the variation field equals $W$ on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · secondVariation_congr⟧. So it is negative.
3. By the second derivative test ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/DerivativeTest · isLocalMax_of_deriv_deriv_neg⟧, $0$ is also a local maximum of $f$. Then $f$ is constant near $0$ and $f''(0) = 0$, a contradiction.

*What Milnor leaves implicit.* That a minimum forces a nonnegative second derivative needs an argument when only the second derivative *at* $0$ is known to exist: Taylor's formula with the second derivative at intermediate points would need more regularity than has been proved. Milnor concludes that the index of $E_{**}$ is zero. The index is defined formally in §VII.18, but its vanishing at a minimal geodesic is not stated as a separate theorem. What is proved is the positive semi-definiteness, which is equivalent to it.

### VII.11 Theorem 14.1: the null vectors of $E_{**}$ are the Jacobi fields

**Definitions (Milnor p. 77; V9.2; §0.9).** Along a map $\gamma$ with $V = \gamma'$, the *Jacobi operator* of a field $J$ is $\frac{D^2J}{dt^2} + R(V, J)V$ ⟦Chapter8/Jacobi · jacobiExpr⟧. $J$ is a *Jacobi field along $\gamma$* if it is $C^\infty$ on $[0, 1]$, with one-sided derivatives at the ends, tangent along $\gamma$ on $[0, 1]$, and satisfies the Jacobi equation $\frac{D^2J}{dt^2} + R(V, J)V = 0$ on $(0, 1)$ ⟦Chapter8/Jacobi · IsJacobiField⟧. Milnor defines Jacobi fields along geodesics. The formal definition makes sense along any curve, and the theory of §§VII.12–VII.13 is developed in that generality.

**The easy half.** A Jacobi field vanishing at $0$ and $1$ lies in $T\Omega_\gamma$ ⟦Chapter8/Jacobi · IsJacobiField.mem_pathTangent⟧. For a Jacobi field $J$, $\Phi(J, Y) = 0$ for *every* field $Y$ ⟦Chapter8/Jacobi · IsJacobiField.secondVariation_eq_zero⟧: $J$ is smooth on $(0, 1)$, so it has no jumps, and the integrand vanishes by the Jacobi equation.

**Theorem 14.1 (Milnor p. 78; V9.5).** Let $\gamma \in \Omega(N; p, q)$ be an unbroken geodesic and $W_1 \in T\Omega_\gamma$. Then $E_{**}(W_1, W_2) = 0$ for every $W_2 \in T\Omega_\gamma$ if and only if $W_1$ is a Jacobi field along $\gamma$ ⟦Chapter8/Jacobi · theorem_14_1⟧. In particular a null vector is $C^\infty$ on all of $[0, 1]$.

*The idea* is Milnor's. Test fields concentrated inside a piece show that $W_1$ satisfies the Jacobi equation there, and test fields concentrated at a break point show that $DW_1/dt$ does not jump. The pieces then "fit together". The formal proof replaces this last step, which in the book rests on the existence and uniqueness of Jacobi fields along all of $\gamma$, by a local regularity argument. Let $X \in T\Omega_\gamma$ be a null vector, smooth on the pieces of a finite set $B$.

*Step 1: the Jacobi equation off $B$* ⟦Chapter8/Jacobi · jacobiExpr_eq_zero_of_null⟧. As in Step 1 of Corollary 12.3, with the test field $\varphi\,\mathcal J(X)$ ⟦Chapter8/Critical · bumpAt⟧ ⟦Chapter8/Critical · contDiff_smul_of_tsupport_subset⟧, where $\mathcal J(X)$ is the Jacobi operator of $X$ and $\varphi$ a bump function inside the piece around $x$. On the interior of the piece $\mathcal J(X)$ is smooth ⟦Chapter8/Jacobi · contDiffOn_jacobiExpr⟧, the curvature term being smooth along smooth tangent data ⟦Chapter8/Jacobi · contDiffAt_curvR⟧. It is tangent ⟦Chapter8/Jacobi · prj_jacobiExpr⟧, since $D^2X/dt^2$ is a projection and $R(V, X)V$ is tangent for tangent $V$ and $X$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvOp_mem⟧ ⟦Chapter8/Jacobi · IsUnbrokenGeodesic.prj_deriv⟧. The test field lies in $T\Omega_\gamma$, the jump terms vanish, and $0 = \Phi(X, \varphi\mathcal J(X)) = -\int_0^1\varphi\|\mathcal J(X)\|^2$ forces $\mathcal J(X)(x) = 0$.

*Step 2: no jumps* ⟦Chapter8/Jacobi · jump_eq_zero_of_null⟧. At a break point $x$, the test field $\varphi(t)P_{\gamma(t)}\Delta$ with $\Delta = P_{\gamma(x)}\Delta_xX'$ and $\varphi$ a bump at $x$ gives $\|\Delta\|^2 = 0$, exactly as in Step 2 of Corollary 12.3; the integral term vanishes by Step 1 ⟦Chapter8/Jacobi · jacobiExpr_eq_zero_of_null⟧. This kills the tangential part of the jump. The normal part vanishes for another reason: $X = PX$ near $x$, so $X'(x^\pm) = P'(x)X(x) + P X'(x^\pm)$, and the two one-sided derivatives have the same normal part, hence $\Delta_xX' = P\Delta_xX'$ ⟦Chapter8/Jacobi · jump_eq_prj_jump⟧. In Milnor's language both normal parts are $\mathrm{II}(V, W_1)$. So $X$ is $C^1$ at $x$. In $\mathbb R^K$, the absence of jumps of $DW_1/dt$ gives $W_1 \in C^1$ only because of this observation.

*Step 3: smoothness across the former break points* ⟦Chapter8/Jacobi · smooth_near_of_null⟧.

1. *The Jacobi equation as a second-order equation in $V$* ⟦Chapter8/Jacobi · deriv_deriv_eq_of_jacobiExpr⟧. For a smooth tangent field $X$, write $P(t) = P_{\gamma(t)}$. Then $X = PX$ gives $X' = P'X + PX'$ and $X'' = P''X + 2P'X' + PX''$, so $(1 - P)(P''X + 2P'X') = X'' - PX''$. The Jacobi equation $P(P'X' + PX'') + R(V, X)V = 0$ gives $PX''$. Adding,
$$
X'' = -P(P'X') - R(V, X)V + (1 - P)\big(P''X + 2P'X'\big).
$$
2. *A smooth right side.* Near a break point $x$, define $F(t, J, J')$ by this right side, with $R(V, J)V$ written through the derivative of $\mathrm{projRep}_\sigma$ applied to $P(t)J$. Then $F$ is smooth in $(t, J, J') \in O \times V \times V$ for an interval $O$ around $x$, and it agrees with the formula above for tangent $J$.
3. *The equation holds at $x$ too.* By Step 2, $X$ is differentiable at $x$ and $X'$ is continuous there, since the one-sided derivatives agree and are continuous from each side. At $t \ne x$ in $O$, Step 1 and the second-order form give $X'' = F(t, X, X')$. A derivative extension lemma ⟦Chapter8/Jacobi · hasDerivAt_of_hasDerivAt_punctured⟧ says that if $f$ and $g$ are continuous at $x$ and $f' = g$ on a punctured neighbourhood, then $f'(x) = g(x)$. It shows that $X'$ is differentiable at $x$ with derivative $F(x, X(x), X'(x))$.
4. *Bootstrap.* A solution of $J'' = F(t, J, J')$ with $F$ smooth is smooth ⟦Chapter8/Jacobi · contDiffOn_of_ode⟧, by induction: if $J$ and $J'$ are $C^k$, then $J'' = F(t, J, J')$ is $C^k$, so $J$ and $J'$ are $C^{k+1}$. So $X$ is smooth on $O$. Its Jacobi operator is then continuous on $O$ ⟦Chapter8/Jacobi · contDiffOn_jacobiExpr⟧ and vanishes off $x$, hence at $x$.

*Assembly.* $X$ is smooth on $[0, 1]$: at $0$ and $1$ by the first and last pieces, inside by Step 3. The Jacobi equation holds on $(0, 1)$ by Steps 1 and 3 ⟦Chapter8/Jacobi · smooth_near_of_null⟧. The converse is the easy half above ⟦Chapter8/Jacobi · IsJacobiField.secondVariation_eq_zero⟧.

*What Milnor leaves implicit.* The test fields must be continuous at the break points, which the bump functions ensure. The field with prescribed values at the break points has to be constructed. And "fit together", justified in the book because a Jacobi field is determined by its value and derivative at one point, needs the global existence and uniqueness theory of §VII.12. That theory is formalized but not used here. The local argument above needs no existence theorem at all.

### VII.12 Linear equations on the whole line

Milnor writes the Jacobi equation in a parallel orthonormal frame as a linear second-order system and concludes that it has $2n$ independent solutions, all defined along the whole of $\gamma$ (p. 77). The local existence theorem gives solutions only on intervals of length about $1/\sup\|A\|$, so a global theorem for linear equations is needed. Part V (§V.18) proved one only for skew-adjoint coefficients, those of parallel transport. The file `Chapter9/JacobiODE` proves it in general.

**Lemma 14.A of the exposition (V9.1).** Let $E$ be a complete real normed space and $t \mapsto A(t)$ a continuous map from $\mathbb R$ to the continuous linear maps of $E$.

(i) *Global existence.* For every $t_0$ and $x_0$ there is a solution of $x' = A(t)x$ on all of $\mathbb R$ with $x(t_0) = x_0$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiODE · exists_linearODE⟧.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. *On a bounded interval* $(t_0 - a, t_0 + a)$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiODE · exists_linearODE_Ioo⟧. Let $\|A(t)\| \le C$ on $[t_0 - a, t_0 + a]$ ⟦Chapter6/LinearODE · exists_nnnorm_le_Icc⟧, and put $R = \|x_0\|e^{Ca} + 1$. Truncate the field radially: $f(t, x) = A(t)\rho_R(x)$, with $\rho_R(x) = \frac{R}{\max(R, \|x\|)}x$ the radial retraction onto the ball of radius $R$ ⟦Chapter6/LinearODE · radTrunc⟧. $\rho_R$ is $2$-Lipschitz and bounded by $R$ ⟦Chapter6/LinearODE · lipschitzWith_radTrunc⟧ ⟦Chapter6/LinearODE · norm_radTrunc_le⟧, so $f$ is bounded by $CR$ and Lipschitz in $x$ with constant $2C$. The Picard–Lindelöf theorem ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/ODE/PicardLindelof · IsPicardLindelof⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/ODE/ExistUnique · IsPicardLindelof.exists_eq_forall_mem_Icc_hasDerivWithinAt₀⟧ then gives a solution on the whole closed interval in one step.
2. *The truncation is never active.* $\rho_R$ does not increase norms ⟦Chapter9/JacobiODE · norm_radTrunc_le_norm⟧, so $\|f(t, \alpha(t))\| \le C\|\alpha(t)\|$. Grönwall's inequality forward in time ⟦Chapter9/JacobiODE · norm_le_of_norm_deriv_le⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/ODE/Gronwall · norm_le_gronwallBound_of_norm_deriv_right_le⟧ gives $\|\alpha(t)\| \le \|x_0\|e^{C(t - t_0)} < R$ for $t \ge t_0$. Applied to $s \mapsto \alpha(t_0 - s)$, it gives the same backward in time. So $\rho_R(\alpha(t)) = \alpha(t)$ ⟦Chapter6/LinearODE · radTrunc_of_norm_le⟧, and $\alpha$ solves the true equation on the open interval.
3. *On $\mathbb R$.* Choose such a solution $\beta_a$ for every $a > 0$. Two of them agree where both are defined, by uniqueness for linear equations on open intervals (Part V, §V.18) ⟦Chapter6/LinearODE · linearODE_eqOn⟧. So $\alpha(t) = \beta_{|t - t_0| + 1}(t)$ solves the equation at every $t$. (The exposition uses Picard iteration on the whole interval instead.)

(ii) *The propagator.* $\mathrm{sol}_s(\xi)$ is a chosen global solution with value $\xi$ at $s$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiODE · linSol⟧ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiODE · linSol_self⟧ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiODE · hasDerivAt_linSol⟧. By uniqueness on $\mathbb R$ ⟦Chapter6/LinearODE · linearODE_eq⟧, every global solution is the propagated solution through any of its values ⟦Chapter9/JacobiODE · eq_linSol⟧. Hence $\mathrm{sol}_s$ is linear in $\xi$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiODE · linSol_add⟧ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiODE · linSol_smul⟧: a sum of solutions is a solution, with the summed value at $s$. For finite-dimensional $E$, the propagator $\xi \mapsto \mathrm{sol}_s(\xi)(t)$ is a continuous linear map ⟦Chapter9/JacobiODE · linFlow⟧ that depends continuously on $t$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiODE · continuous_linFlow⟧, since in finite dimension an operator-valued map is continuous when its values on each vector are.

(iii) *Uniqueness on closed intervals, end points included.* A function continuous on $[a, b]$ that solves the equation on $(a, b)$ agrees on $[a, b]$ with the propagated solution through its value at any $s \in [a, b]$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiODE · eqOn_Icc_linSol⟧. It agrees on $(a, b)$ with the solution through its value at the midpoint ⟦Chapter6/LinearODE · linearODE_eqOn⟧, hence on $[a, b]$ by continuity ⟦Chapter9/JacobiODE · continuous_linSol⟧, and that solution is the one through $f(s)$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiODE · eq_linSol⟧.

(iv) *Regularity.* If $A$ is smooth on $[a, b]$, with one-sided derivatives at the ends, and $f' = Af$ at every point of $[a, b]$, then $f$ is smooth on $[a, b]$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · contDiffOn_Icc_of_hasDerivAt⟧. This is a bootstrap with derivatives within $[a, b]$.

### VII.13 The Jacobi equation as a linear system

**The setting along a curve (V9.2).** A *smooth curve in $N$ on $[0, 1]$* is a map $\gamma$ that is $C^\infty$ on $[0, 1]$, with one-sided derivatives at the ends, and takes values in $N$ there ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsSmoothCurveIn⟧. Every unbroken geodesic of $\Omega$ is one ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsUnbrokenGeodesic.isSmoothCurveIn⟧, and every such curve lies in $\Omega(N; \gamma(0), \gamma(1))$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · IsSmoothCurveIn.mem_pathSpace⟧. Along it, the velocity $V(t)$ is the derivative within $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · velAlong⟧, $P'(t)$ is the derivative within $[0, 1]$ of $t \mapsto P_{\gamma(t)}$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · dpAlong⟧, and $\frac{DX}{dt}(t) = P(t)X'(t)$ with the derivative taken within $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · covAlong⟧. All three are therefore one-sided at the ends, as Milnor's initial conditions at $t = 0$ require. $T_xN$ is also regarded as a subspace of $V$, the zero subspace off $N$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · tanAt⟧. Differentiating $P^2 = P$ within $[0, 1]$ gives $P' = P'P + PP'$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsSmoothCurveIn.dp_eq⟧, hence $PP'P = 0$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsSmoothCurveIn.prj_dp_prj⟧. The velocity is tangent ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsSmoothCurveIn.velAlong_mem⟧.

**The coefficients.** $K(t)v = R(V(t), P(t)v)V(t)$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · curvAlong⟧ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · curvAlong_apply⟧, built from the linear map $b \mapsto R(a, b)a$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · curvMid⟧. The projection $P(t)$ inside makes $K$ defined on all of $V$. Put $S = P'P - PP'$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · jacS⟧. The *Jacobi system* is the first-order linear system
$$
J' = SJ + Y, \qquad Y' = SY - KJ
$$
for $(J, Y) \in V \times V$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · jacSys⟧ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · jacSys_apply⟧. Its coefficient is extended to $\mathbb R$ as a constant outside $[0, 1]$, by evaluating it at the nearest point of $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · jacA⟧. The algebra used below is that $Sv = P'v$ and $PSv = 0$ for tangent $v$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsSmoothCurveIn.jacS_of_tangent⟧ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsSmoothCurveIn.prj_jacS_of_tangent⟧, that $(1 - P)Sv = P'Pv$ for every $v$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsSmoothCurveIn.jacS_sub_prj⟧, and that $K$ takes tangent values ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsSmoothCurveIn.prj_curvAlong⟧. The coefficients are smooth on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsSmoothCurveIn.contDiffOn_curvAlong⟧ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsSmoothCurveIn.contDiffOn_jacSys⟧: near $\gamma(t_0)$, $K$ is an expression in the derivative of $\mathrm{projRep}_\sigma$ along $\gamma$. The extended coefficient is therefore continuous on $\mathbb R$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsSmoothCurveIn.continuous_jacA⟧, and §VII.12 applies. The solution with value $\zeta$ at $s$ is the propagated one ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · jacSol⟧. It is linear in $\zeta$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · jacSol_add⟧ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · jacSol_smul⟧ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · jacSol_zero⟧ and smooth on $[0, 1]$ by (iv) ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · contDiffOn_jacSol⟧.

*Why the term $S$.* Without a frame, a solution of a linear system in $V \times V$ has no reason to stay tangent to $N$. The term $S$ is chosen to make the tangent bundle along $\gamma$ invariant.

**Tangent data stay tangent** ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · jacSol_mem_tanAt⟧. Let $s \in [0, 1]$ and $\zeta = (x, y)$ with $x, y \in T_{\gamma(s)}N$. Then both components of the solution are tangent on $[0, 1]$.

*Proof.* Let $a = (1 - P)J$ and $b = (1 - P)Y$ be the normal parts. On $(0, 1)$,
$$
a' = (1 - P)J' - P'J = (1 - P)(SJ + Y) - P'J = P'PJ + (1 - P)Y - P'J = -P'a + b,
$$
and $b' = (1 - P)(SY - KJ) - P'Y = P'PY - P'Y = -P'b$, because $(1 - P)KJ = 0$. So $(a, b)$ solves a homogeneous linear system with continuous coefficients, and it vanishes at $s$. Uniqueness on $[0, 1]$, in the form (iii) of §VII.12 ⟦Chapter9/JacobiODE · eqOn_Icc_linSol⟧, makes it vanish on $[0, 1]$.

**Solutions with tangent data are Jacobi fields** ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · isJacobiField_jacSol⟧. For such $\zeta$, the first component $J$ is a Jacobi field and the second is $DJ/dt$ on $[0, 1]$. Indeed $DJ/dt = PJ' = P(SJ + Y) = Y$, because $PSJ = 0$ and $Y$ is tangent, both components being tangent by the previous result ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · jacSol_mem_tanAt⟧. Then $D^2J/dt^2 = PY' = P(SY - KJ) = -KJ = -R(V, J)V$.

**Every Jacobi field is a solution** ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsJacobiField.eqOn_jacSol⟧. Let $J$ be a Jacobi field along $\gamma$ and $s \in [0, 1]$. On $[0, 1]$, $(J, DJ/dt)$ agrees with the solution whose value at $s$ is $(J(s), \frac{DJ}{dt}(s))$.

*Proof.* The pair is continuous on $[0, 1]$, and on $(0, 1)$ it solves the system. First, $J = PJ$ gives $J' = P'J + PJ'$, and $SJ = P'J - PP'PJ = P'J$ for tangent $J$, so $J' = SJ + DJ/dt$. Second, $(PJ')' = P'J' + PJ''$, and the Jacobi equation says $PP'J' + PJ'' = -KJ$. Together with $P' = P'P + PP'$ this gives $(DJ/dt)' = S(DJ/dt) - KJ$. Uniqueness on closed intervals, end points included ⟦Chapter9/JacobiODE · eqOn_Icc_linSol⟧, concludes the proof. This is why $s$ may be $0$ or $1$.

**Uniqueness (Milnor p. 77).** A Jacobi field with $J(s) = 0$ and $\frac{DJ}{dt}(s) = 0$ at some $s \in [0, 1]$ vanishes on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsJacobiField.eqOn_zero⟧: it is the solution with data $0$, which is $0$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · jacSol_zero⟧.

*Deviation (restated).* Milnor's coordinates need orthonormal parallel fields along $\gamma$ (Lemma 8.2), and Part V (§V.19) provides parallel transport only for curves defined on all of $\mathbb R$. Here there is no frame: the unknown is $(J, DJ/dt)$ in $V \times V$, and the invariance of the tangent bundle under the system replaces the frame. *Deviation (stronger).* Everything holds along any smooth curve in $N$, not only along geodesics, with initial values at any $s \in [0, 1]$.

### VII.14 The $2n$-dimensional space of Jacobi fields; conjugate points; nullity equals multiplicity

**Definition (V9.3).** For a smooth curve $\gamma$ in $N$ on $[0, 1]$, $\mathcal J(\gamma)$ is the set of Jacobi fields along $\gamma$ that are constant off $[0, 1]$, that is, $J(t) = J(\pi(t))$ with $\pi$ the nearest-point map of $\mathbb R$ onto $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · jacobiSpace⟧ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · mem_jacobiSpace⟧. The normalization makes a field along $\gamma : [0, 1] \to N$ a single element. Without it, functions that differ only off $[0, 1]$ would be distinct elements, and the space would be infinite-dimensional. Being a Jacobi field depends only on the values on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · IsJacobiField.congr⟧, and the Jacobi fields are exactly the first components of the solutions of the Jacobi system with tangent data at $0$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · isJacobiField_iff⟧. Since those solutions depend linearly on the data, $\mathcal J(\gamma)$ is a vector space. Every Jacobi field agrees on $[0, 1]$ with its normalization, which lies in $\mathcal J(\gamma)$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · IsJacobiField.comp_projIcc_mem⟧, and an element of $\mathcal J(\gamma)$ that vanishes on $[0, 1]$ is $0$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · jacobiSpace_eq_zero⟧.

**Corollary 14.B of the exposition (Milnor p. 77; V9.3).** For every $s \in [0, 1]$, the map $J \mapsto (J(s), \frac{DJ}{dt}(s))$ is a linear isomorphism $\mathcal J(\gamma) \to T_{\gamma(s)}N \times T_{\gamma(s)}N$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · jacEval⟧ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · jacEval_bijective⟧ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · jacEquiv⟧. Hence $\dim\mathcal J(\gamma) = 2n$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · finrank_jacobiSpace⟧, and Jacobi fields with any prescribed tangent data at $s$ exist ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · exists_jacobi⟧.

*Proof.* The map is linear because $D/dt$ is additive and homogeneous on smooth fields ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · covAlong_add⟧ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · covAlong_smul⟧. It is injective by uniqueness ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsJacobiField.eqOn_zero⟧ and the normalization. It is surjective because the solution with data $(x, y)$ at $s$ is a Jacobi field ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · isJacobiField_jacSol⟧; its normalization has the same value and the same covariant derivative at $s$, since the derivative is computed within $[0, 1]$. The dimension count uses $\dim T_xN = n$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · finrank_tanAt⟧. *Hidden hypothesis.* That every pair of initial values occurs needs the global existence theorem of §VII.12. *Deviation (stronger).* The isomorphism holds at every $s \in [0, 1]$, along any smooth curve.

**Definition (Milnor p. 77; V9.4; §0.9).** Parameters $a$ and $b$ are *conjugate along $\gamma$* if $a, b \in [0, 1]$, $a \ne b$, and some Jacobi field vanishes at $a$ and at $b$ without vanishing identically on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · IsConjugate⟧. The space of fields of $\mathcal J(\gamma)$ vanishing at $a$ and $b$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · conjSpace⟧ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · mem_conjSpace⟧ has finite dimension, being a subspace of $\mathcal J(\gamma)$, and that dimension is the *multiplicity* ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · conjMultiplicity⟧. For $a \ne b$ in $[0, 1]$, $a$ and $b$ are conjugate if and only if the multiplicity is positive ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · isConjugate_iff⟧. Below, $\nu(t)$ is the multiplicity of $0$ and $t$, which is $0$ when $t$ is not conjugate to $0$. Milnor's footnote on p. 77 advises speaking of conjugate parameter values when $\gamma$ has self-intersections, and the formal notion follows that advice: in §15 the points $\gamma(t)$ conjugate to $\gamma(0)$ are read as the parameters $t$ conjugate to $0$.

**Theorem 14.1, the null space and the nullity (Milnor pp. 77–79; V9.6).** The fields of $T\Omega_\gamma$ are functions on $\mathbb R$ whose values off $[0, 1]$ play no role. So the literal kernel of the formal $E_{**}$ contains every field vanishing on $[0, 1]$, and it is infinite-dimensional. The *null space* is therefore read on $[0, 1]$: it is the image of that kernel under $X \mapsto X \circ \pi$, restriction to $[0, 1]$ followed by constant extension ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · restrictIcc⟧ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · hessNullSpace⟧. The *nullity* is its dimension ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · hessNullity⟧. If $\gamma \in \Omega(N; p, q)$ is an unbroken geodesic, the null space is the space of fields of $\mathcal J(\gamma)$ vanishing at $0$ and $1$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · hessNullSpace_eq⟧. This follows from Theorem 14.1 in both directions ⟦Chapter8/Jacobi · theorem_14_1⟧ together with the normalization. Hence the nullity is the multiplicity of $0$ and $1$, in particular finite ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · theorem_14_1_nullity⟧, and $E_{**}$ is degenerate if and only if $0$ and $1$ are conjugate ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · hessNullity_pos_iff⟧. Milnor's remark that the nullity is always finite (p. 79) needs the identification with a subspace of the $2n$-dimensional $\mathcal J(\gamma)$.

### VII.15 Remark 14.2

**Remark 14.2 (Milnor p. 79; V9.7).** Let $\gamma \in \Omega(N; p, q)$ be an unbroken geodesic and $n \ge 1$. Then the nullity satisfies $\nu < n$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · remark_14_2⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Let $\mathcal J_0 = \{J \in \mathcal J(\gamma) : J(0) = 0\}$. The map $J \mapsto \frac{DJ}{dt}(0)$ is an isomorphism of $\mathcal J_0$ onto $T_{\gamma(0)}N$, injective by uniqueness and onto by existence ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · exists_jacobi⟧, so $\dim\mathcal J_0 = n$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · finrank_jacobiSpace_zero⟧. The null space is contained in $\mathcal J_0$. The inclusion is strict as soon as some Jacobi field vanishes at $0$ but not at $1$, and two cases produce one.

1. If $V(1) \ne 0$, take $J(t) = tV(t)$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · isJacobiField_smul_vel⟧. Indeed $\frac{D}{dt}(tV) = V + tP\gamma'' = V$, then $\frac{D}{dt}V = P\gamma'' = 0$, and $R(V, tV)V = tR(V, V)V = 0$ by antisymmetry.
2. If $V(1) = 0$, then $\gamma$ is constant on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · eq_const_of_velAlong_one_eq_zero⟧. Its speed is constant on $(0, 1)$ ⟦Chapter7/Geodesic · IsGeodesicOn.norm_deriv_eq⟧, and by continuity of $V$ on $[0, 1]$ at $1$ it equals $\|V(1)\| = 0$, so $\gamma' = 0$ on $(0, 1)$. Along a constant curve, $t \mapsto ty$ is a Jacobi field for every tangent vector $y$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · isJacobiField_const_curve⟧, and $n \ge 1$ provides some $y \ne 0$.

*Hidden hypotheses.* Milnor's witness $J_t = tV_t$ has $J_1 = V_1$, which is non-zero only if $\gamma$ is non-constant. For the constant geodesic ($p = q$) the Jacobi fields are $t \mapsto x + ty$ and another witness is needed. And $\nu < n$ needs $n \ge 1$, since for $n = 0$, $\nu = 0 = n$. *Deviation (stronger).* The constant geodesic is included.

### VII.16 Lemma 14.3, and Lemma 14.4 from initial data

**Lemma 14.3 (Milnor p. 80; V9.8).** Let $\varepsilon > 0$ and let $\alpha(u, t)$ be $C^\infty$ on $(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon) \times [0, 1]$, with one-sided derivatives along the edges $t = 0, 1$ and values in $N$ there, such that each $t \mapsto \alpha(u, t)$ is a geodesic on $(0, 1)$. Then $W(t) = \frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial u}(0, t)$ is a Jacobi field along $\alpha(0, \cdot)$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiVariation · lemma_14_3⟧. Being a Jacobi field along $\gamma$ depends only on $\gamma$ on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiVariation · IsJacobiField.congr_curve⟧.

*The proof as formalized* is Milnor's. $W$ is smooth on $[0, 1]$, as $\partial_u\alpha(0, \cdot)$ on the closed strip ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · contDiffOn_pdU⟧, and tangent, as the derivative of a curve in $N$ ⟦Chapter7/Geodesic · hasDerivAt_mem_tangentSubspace⟧. For the Jacobi equation at $t \in (0, 1)$, apply the curvature identity ⟦Chapter8/SecondVariation · curvature_identity⟧ at $(0, t)$ on the open strip $(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon) \times (0, 1)$. The left side is $P\partial_u(P\partial_t\partial_t\alpha)$, and $P\partial_t\partial_t\alpha$ vanishes identically on the open strip because each $\alpha(u, \cdot)$ is a geodesic, so the left side is $0$. On the right side, $\partial_t\alpha(0, t) = \gamma'(t)$, and $P\partial_t\partial_u\alpha(0, \cdot) = \frac{DW}{dt}$ near $t$, so the right side is $\frac{D^2W}{dt^2} + R(V, W)V$. Milnor applies Lemmas 9.2 and 8.7 to a map defined on a strip and smooth up to its edges; the curvature identity on the open strip is what makes that legitimate.

**Lemma 14.4 (Milnor p. 81; V9.9).** Let $\gamma$ be a smooth curve in $N$ on $[0, 1]$ that is a geodesic on $(0, 1)$, and $J$ a Jacobi field along $\gamma$. There are $\varepsilon > 0$ and a map $\alpha$, $C^\infty$ on $(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon) \times [0, 1]$ with values in $N$, such that each $\alpha(u, \cdot)$ is a geodesic on $(0, 1)$, $\alpha(0, t) = \gamma(t)$, and $\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial u}(0, t) = J(t)$ for $t \in [0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter9/GeodesicVariation · lemma_14_4⟧. This is the converse of Lemma 14.3.

*Milnor's proof, and the step it leaves unargued.* Milnor takes a neighbourhood $U$ of $\gamma(0)$ in which any two points are joined by a unique minimal geodesic depending differentiably on its end points, and $\delta$ with $\gamma([0, \delta]) \subseteq U$. Moving the end points along curves with prescribed velocities and joining them by minimal geodesics gives variations through geodesics whose fields take arbitrary values at $0$ and $\delta$. So $J \mapsto (J(0), J(\delta))$ is onto, hence an isomorphism, both spaces having dimension $2n$, and every Jacobi field along $\gamma|[0, \delta]$ arises. Compactness of $[0, 1]$ then extends the geodesics. The construction returns $\bar\alpha(0) = \gamma|[0, \delta]$ only if $\gamma|[0, \delta]$ is *the* minimal geodesic from $\gamma(0)$ to $\gamma(\delta)$, and $\gamma([0, \delta]) \subseteq U$ does not say this. For Milnor's $U$ it is true, by an argument the book omits and the exposition supplies. Two further steps are left implicit. The compactness step is the smooth dependence of geodesics on their initial data over a compact interval, together with the openness of the domain of $\exp$, and neither is among the statements of §10. And identifying the field of the extended variation with $J$ needs the uniqueness of Jacobi fields.

*The formal proof builds the variation from initial data*, as $\alpha(u, t) = \exp_{c(u)}(t\,w(u))$. It needs three tools.

*(i) The local smooth flow, uniformly* ⟦Chapter9/GeodesicVariation · exists_smooth_ray⟧. For $x_1 \in N$ and $R > 0$ there are an open set $U \ni x_1$, a time $h > 0$ and a map $\Phi$, smooth on $U \times \{\|w\| < R\} \times (-h, h)$, such that for $y \in N \cap U$ and $w \in T_yN$ with $\|w\| < R$, $t \mapsto \exp_y(tw)$ is a geodesic on $(-h, h)$ and equals $\Phi(y, w, t)$ there. *Proof.* Lemma 10.2 (Part VI, §VI.5) ⟦Chapter7/GeodesicLocal · lemma_10_2⟧ gives $\Gamma$ smooth on $U \times \{\|v\| < \varepsilon\} \times (-2, 2)$, with $\Gamma(y, v, \cdot)$ the geodesic through $y$ with velocity $v$. Put $c = \varepsilon/R$, $\Phi(y, w, t) = \Gamma(y, cw, t/c)$ and $h = 2c$. Since $\Gamma(y, cw, \cdot)$ is a geodesic with initial velocity $cw$, $\exp_y(s\,cw) = \Gamma(y, cw, s)$ ⟦Chapter7/GeodesicFlow · IsGeodesicOn.exp_smul_eq⟧, which is the claim with $s = t/c$. The rescaled curve is again a geodesic.

*(ii) A geodesic segment extends past its end points* ⟦Chapter9/GeodesicVariation · exists_midpoint_ray⟧ ⟦Chapter9/GeodesicVariation · exists_initial_ray⟧. A smooth curve $\gamma$ in $N$ on $[0, 1]$ that is a geodesic on $(0, 1)$ agrees on $[0, 1]$ with $t \mapsto \exp_{\gamma(0)}(tV(0))$, which is a geodesic on an open interval containing $[0, 1]$. Part VI leaves this extension unformalized. *Proof.* Start at the midpoint. With $x = \gamma(1/2)$ and $v = \gamma'(1/2)$, the curve $\gamma(\cdot + 1/2)$ equals the ray $t \mapsto \exp_x(tv)$ on $(-1/2, 1/2)$ ⟦Chapter7/GeodesicFlow · IsGeodesicOn.eqOn_exp_smul⟧. To continue past an end $e \in \{0, 1\}$, apply (i) at $\gamma(e)$ with $R = \|V(e)\| + 1$, obtaining $U$ and $h$. By continuity of $(\gamma, V)$ on $[0, 1]$ at $e$, choose $t \in (0, 1)$ within $h/2$ of $e$ with $\gamma(t) \in U$ and $\|\gamma'(t)\| < R$. The geodesic flow of $(x, v)$ at time $t - 1/2$ is $(\gamma(t), \gamma'(t))$ ⟦Chapter7/GeodesicFlow · geoFlow⟧ ⟦Chapter9/GeodesicVariation · geoFlow_eq_exp⟧, and the ray it starts is a geodesic on $(-h, h)$. The gluing lemma of Part VI (§VI.16) ⟦Chapter7/GeodesicFlow · isGeodesicOn_exp_smul_union⟧, used first at the right end and then at the left, makes the ray from the midpoint a geodesic on $(-1/2 - \eta, 1/2 + \eta)$, and it equals $\gamma$ on $[0, 1]$ by continuity at the ends. Recentred at $0$, $g(t) = \exp_x((t - 1/2)v)$ is a geodesic on $(-\eta, 1 + \eta)$ that agrees with $\gamma$ on $[0, 1]$. Hence $g(0) = \gamma(0)$, and $g'(0) = V(0)$, the one-sided velocity, so that $g(t) = \exp_{\gamma(0)}(tV(0))$.

*(iii) Smooth dependence on initial data over a compact interval.* Let $c, w$ be smooth on an open set $U_0 \ni 0$, with $c(u) \in N$ and $w(u) \in T_{c(u)}N$. Write $\mathrm{rays}(u, t) = \exp_{c(u)}(t\,w(u))$ ⟦Chapter9/GeodesicVariation · rays⟧. Say the rays are *good up to $b$* if there are $\varepsilon, \eta > 0$ with $(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon) \subseteq U_0$ such that $\mathrm{rays}$ is smooth on $(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon) \times (-\eta, b + \eta)$ and every ray is a geodesic on $(-\eta, b + \eta)$ ⟦Chapter9/GeodesicVariation · RaysGood⟧ ⟦Chapter9/GeodesicVariation · RaysGood.mono⟧. **Claim:** if the ray of $(c(0), w(0))$ is a geodesic on $(-\delta, B + \delta)$ with $B \ge 0$, the rays are good up to $B$ ⟦Chapter9/GeodesicVariation · raysGood_of_isGeodesicOn⟧.

1. *Start* ⟦Chapter9/GeodesicVariation · raysGood_zero⟧. Apply (i) ⟦Chapter9/GeodesicVariation · exists_smooth_ray⟧ at $c(0)$ with $R = \|w(0)\| + 1$. For small $u$, $c(u) \in U_1$ and $\|w(u)\| < R$, so $\mathrm{rays}(u, t) = \Phi(c(u), w(u), t)$ for $|t| < h$. This is smooth, and each ray is a geodesic on $(-h/2, h/2)$.
2. *One step* ⟦Chapter9/GeodesicVariation · RaysGood.step⟧. Suppose the rays are good up to $b \ge 0$, and the position and velocity of the base ray at time $b$ lie in the domain $U_1 \times \{\|v\| < R\}$ of a local flow $\Phi$ of time $h$. The position $F(u) = \mathrm{rays}(u, b)$ and the velocity $G(u) = \partial_t\mathrm{rays}(u, b)$ ⟦Chapter9/GeodesicVariation · deriv_rays_eq⟧ are smooth in $u$, and for small $u$ they stay in that domain. Each ray continues, by the gluing lemma ⟦Chapter7/GeodesicFlow · isGeodesicOn_exp_smul_union⟧, as the ray of $(F(u), G(u))$, and there it equals $\Phi(F(u), G(u), t - b)$, which is smooth in $(u, t)$. Smoothness is a local property, so $\mathrm{rays}$ is smooth on the union, and with $\eta' = \min(\eta, h/4)$ the rays are good up to $b + h/2$.
3. *Supremum.* Let $b_0$ be the supremum of the $b \in [0, B]$ up to which the rays are good; $0$ is such a $b$ by step 1. Take the local flow at the base ray's point at time $b_0$, and $r > 0$ such that its position and velocity at times within $r$ of $b_0$ lie in the domain of that flow. Pick an admissible $s > b_0 - \min(r, h/4)$. Step 2 from $s$ ⟦Chapter9/GeodesicVariation · RaysGood.step⟧ makes the rays good up to $s + h/2 > b_0$, hence up to $\min(s + h/2, B)$. That number is admissible and at most $b_0 < s + h/2$, so it equals $B$.

The supremum argument replaces Milnor's appeal to the compactness of $[0, 1]$.

*The proof of Lemma 14.4 as formalized.*

1. *Initial points.* $c(u) = \exp_{\gamma(0)}(uJ(0))$ is smooth for $|u| < h$, by (i) at $\gamma(0)$ with $R = \|J(0)\| + 1$. Moreover $c(0) = \gamma(0)$ and $c'(0) = J(0)$ ⟦Chapter7/GeodesicFlow · hasDerivAt_exp_smul_zero⟧.
2. *Initial velocities.* $w(u) = P_{c(u)}\big(V(0) + u\frac{DJ}{dt}(0)\big)$ is smooth and tangent, and $w(0) = V(0)$.
3. *The variation.* By (ii) ⟦Chapter9/GeodesicVariation · exists_initial_ray⟧, the ray of $(c(0), w(0))$ is $\gamma$ on $[0, 1]$ and a geodesic on $(-\eta, 1 + \eta)$. By (iii) with $B = 1$ ⟦Chapter9/GeodesicVariation · raysGood_of_isGeodesicOn⟧, $\alpha(u, t) = \exp_{c(u)}(t\,w(u))$ is smooth on $(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon) \times (-\eta', 1 + \eta')$, which contains $(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon) \times [0, 1]$, takes values in $N$, consists of geodesics, and satisfies $\alpha(0, \cdot) = \gamma$ on $[0, 1]$.
4. *Its field is a Jacobi field.* $W = \frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial u}(0, \cdot)$ is a Jacobi field along $\gamma$, by Lemma 14.3 ⟦Chapter9/JacobiVariation · lemma_14_3⟧ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiVariation · IsJacobiField.congr_curve⟧.
5. *Same value at $0$.* $\alpha(u, 0) = c(u)$ ⟦Chapter7/Exp · exp_zero⟧, so $W(0) = c'(0) = J(0)$.
6. *Same covariant derivative at $0$.* $\frac{DW}{dt}(0) = P\,\partial_t\partial_u\alpha(0, 0) = P\,\partial_u\partial_t\alpha(0, 0)$ by the symmetry of mixed partials at an interior point ⟦Chapter8/SecondVariation · partialX_partialY_of_contDiffOn⟧. Since $\partial_t\alpha(u, 0) = w(u)$, the velocity at $0$ of the ray ⟦Chapter7/GeodesicFlow · hasDerivAt_exp_smul_zero⟧, this equals $P\,w'(0)$. Now $w'(0) = (P_{c})'(0)V(0) + P_{c(0)}\frac{DJ}{dt}(0)$. The projection of the first term vanishes: differentiating $P_c \circ P_c = P_c$ gives $(P_c)' = (P_c)'P_c + P_c(P_c)'$, so $P(P_c)'P = 0$, and $V(0)$ is tangent. Hence $\frac{DW}{dt}(0) = \frac{DJ}{dt}(0)$.
7. *Uniqueness.* $W$ and $J$ have the same initial data at $0$, so on $[0, 1]$ both are the first component of the same solution of the Jacobi system ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsJacobiField.eqOn_jacSol⟧. Hence $W = J$ there.

*Why this avoids the gap.* No end-point data are used, so no minimal geodesic enters, and the question whether $\gamma|[0, \delta]$ is minimal never arises. The formal statement has no minimality hypothesis, and neither the restriction to $[0, \delta]$ nor the dimension count is needed. The only global ingredient is the smooth dependence (iii), which is exactly the compactness step that Milnor's proof also needs.

### VII.17 Remark 14.5, and two non-conjugate parameters

**Two non-conjugate parameters (Milnor p. 81, a parenthetical remark; V9.10).** If $a \ne b$ in $[0, 1]$ are not conjugate along $\gamma$, then $J \mapsto (J(a), J(b))$ is a linear isomorphism $\mathcal J(\gamma) \to T_{\gamma(a)}N \times T_{\gamma(b)}N$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · jacEvalEnds⟧ ⟦Chapter9/GeodesicVariation · jacEvalEnds_bijective_of_not_isConjugate⟧. Injectivity is the definition of non-conjugacy together with the normalization. Surjectivity follows from the dimension count $2n = n + n$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · finrank_jacobiSpace⟧. Milnor states that the values determine the field; the formal statement adds that they can be prescribed.

**Remark 14.5 (Milnor pp. 81–82; V9.11).** (i) Let $\varepsilon$ be a normal radius at $q$, meaning that $\exp_q$ maps the $\varepsilon$-ball of $T_qN$ diffeomorphically onto an open subset of $N$, with smooth extensions $F$ of $\exp_q$ and $G$ of its inverse ⟦Chapter7/NormalNbhd · NormalCoord⟧ ⟦Chapter7/NormalNbhd · IsNormalRadius⟧. Let $v \in T_qN$ with $\|v\| < \varepsilon$. Then $\sigma(t) = \exp_q(tv)$ is a geodesic on an open interval containing $[0, 1]$, hence a smooth curve in $N$ on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiVariation · IsNormalRadius.isGeodesicOn_ray⟧ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiVariation · IsNormalRadius.isSmoothCurveIn_ray⟧. A Jacobi field along $\sigma$ that vanishes at $0$ and $1$ vanishes on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiVariation · remark_14_5_of_isNormalRadius⟧, and $J \mapsto (J(0), J(1))$ is an isomorphism ⟦Chapter9/JacobiVariation · remark_14_5_bijective⟧. (ii) Every $p \in N$ has a neighbourhood $O$ in $V$, with some $\varepsilon > 0$, such that any $q_1, q_2 \in N \cap O$ are joined by $t \mapsto \exp_{q_1}(tv)$ with $\|v\| < \varepsilon$, where $\varepsilon$ is a normal radius at $q_1$, and Jacobi fields along this segment vanishing at both ends vanish ⟦Chapter9/JacobiVariation · remark_14_5⟧. (ii) follows from (i) and Lemma 10.3 (Part VI, §VI.8) ⟦Chapter7/NormalNbhd · lemma_10_3⟧.

*The proof of (i) as formalized.* Let $w = \frac{DJ}{dt}(0) \in T_qN$. Since $J(0) = 0$, it suffices to show $w = 0$, by uniqueness ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsJacobiField.eqOn_zero⟧.

1. *A variation through geodesics.* For $|u| < \varepsilon_1 = (\varepsilon - \|v\|)/(\|w\| + 1)$ one has $\|t(v + uw)\| < \varepsilon$ for $t \in [0, 1]$. So $\alpha(u, t) = \exp_q(t(v + uw)) = F(t(v + uw))$ is smooth, and each $\alpha(u, \cdot)$ is a geodesic on an interval containing $[0, 1]$.
2. *Its field.* By Lemma 14.3 ⟦Chapter9/JacobiVariation · lemma_14_3⟧, $J'(t) = \frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial u}(0, t) = t\,dF_{tv}(w)$ is a Jacobi field along $\sigma$. It has $J'(0) = 0$ and $\frac{DJ'}{dt}(0) = P\,dF_0(w) = w$, because $dF_0$ is the identity on $T_qN$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiVariation · fderiv_extension_exp_zero⟧, as $d(\exp_q)_0$ is.
3. *It is $J$.* By uniqueness $J = J'$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsJacobiField.eqOn_jacSol⟧, and $J(1) = 0$ gives $dF_v(w) = 0$.
4. *The local inverse.* $G \circ F$ is the identity on the $\varepsilon$-ball of $T_qN$. Differentiating along $u \mapsto v + uw$ gives $dG(dF_v(w)) = w$, so $w = 0$.

*Relation with Milnor's argument.* Milnor reads Remark 14.5 off his proof of Lemma 14.4: surjectivity comes from the end-point construction, and injectivity from the dimension count. The formal proof goes the other way, with injectivity from the local inverse of $\exp_q$ and surjectivity from the count. Milnor's geodesic segments inside $U$ become the segment $t \mapsto \exp_{q_1}(tv)$, $\|v\| < \varepsilon$, of Lemma 10.3, which is what his argument actually uses. The index theorem itself does not use Remark 14.5. It needs only the form of §VII.19, no conjugate parameters closer than $h_0$, which is proved by an estimate.

### VII.18 The index of a bilinear form

Milnor defines the index of $E_{**}$ as the largest dimension of a subspace of $T\Omega_\gamma$ on which $E_{**}$ is negative definite (p. 83). $T\Omega_\gamma$ is infinite-dimensional, so this maximum need not exist a priori, and its finiteness is part of Theorem 15.1. Mathlib's signature of a quadratic form is meaningful only in finite dimension. The file `Chapter9/FormIndex` therefore defines the index for any bilinear form and proves the linear algebra that §15 uses without comment.

**Definitions (V9.13; §0.9).** Let $B$ be a bilinear form on a real vector space $X$. $B$ is *negative definite* on a subspace $S$ if $B(x, x) < 0$ for all non-zero $x \in S$, and *positive definite* if $B(x, x) > 0$ for all such $x$ ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · IsNegDefOn⟧ ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · IsPosDefOn⟧. The *index* of $B$ is the supremum, in $\{0, 1, 2, \ldots\} \cup \{\infty\}$, of the dimensions of the finite-dimensional subspaces on which $B$ is negative definite ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · formIndex⟧. Each such subspace bounds the index from below ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · le_formIndex⟧, and a bound valid for all of them bounds it from above ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · formIndex_le⟧. A finite index is attained by some subspace ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · exists_isNegDefOn_of_formIndex_eq⟧, since otherwise every negative definite subspace would have dimension at most $k - 1$. In finite dimension the index is Mathlib's negative index of inertia of $x \mapsto B(x, x)$ ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · formIndex_eq_sigNeg⟧ ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/QuadraticForm/Signature · sigNeg⟧ ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/QuadraticForm/Signature · sigNeg_isGreatest⟧. The *nullity* is the dimension of the null space $\{x : B(x, y) = 0 \text{ for all } y\}$, read on $[0, 1]$ for $E_{**}$ (§VII.14).

**Form-preserving maps.** If $L$ satisfies $B'(Lx, Ly) = B(x, y)$, then the index of $B$ is at most that of $B'$ ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · formIndex_le_of_map⟧. The point is that $L$ is automatically injective on every negative definite subspace: $Lx = 0$ would give $B(x, x) = B'(0, 0) = 0$. So the image of such a subspace is negative definite of the same dimension ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · finrank_map_of_injOn⟧. Consequently restriction to a subspace does not increase the index ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · formIndex_restrict_le⟧, a linear isomorphism carrying one form to the other preserves it ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · formIndex_eq_of_equiv⟧, and so does a positive factor ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · formIndex_smul⟧.

**Lemma 15.B of the exposition (V9.14 (4)).** Let $X = A \oplus C$ with $B(a, c) = B(c, a) = 0$ for $a \in A$, $c \in C$, and $B$ positive definite on $C$. Then $B$ and its restriction to $A$ have the same index ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · formIndex_eq_restrict_of_isCompl⟧, and the null space of $B$ is that of its restriction to $A$ ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · ker_eq_map_ker_restrict⟧.

*Proof.* Let $\pi$ be the projection onto $A$ along $C$. Then $B(x, x) = B(\pi x, \pi x) + B(x - \pi x, x - \pi x) \ge B(\pi x, \pi x)$. On a negative definite subspace $S$ this makes $\pi$ injective and $\pi(S)$ negative definite for $B|_A$, which gives one inequality; restriction gives the other. For the null space, if $x = a + c$ lies in it, then $B(c, c) = B(x, c) - B(a, c) = 0$, so $c = 0$; and a null vector of $B|_A$ is null for $B$ because $A \perp C$. This is what lies behind Milnor's remark that the proof of Lemma 15.4 is "straightforward".

**Sylvester's law of inertia, in the form of p. 86 (V9.14 (2)).** A symmetric form on an $m$-dimensional space is positive definite on a subspace of dimension $m - \mathrm{index} - \mathrm{nullity}$ ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · exists_isPosDefOn_add⟧. The proof combines Mathlib's positive definite subspace of dimension equal to the positive index ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/QuadraticForm/Signature · exists_finrank_eq_sigPos_and_posDef⟧, the identity $\mathrm{sig}^+ + \mathrm{sig}^- + \dim\mathrm{radical} = m$ ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/QuadraticForm/Signature · QuadraticForm.sigPos_add_sigNeg_add_radical⟧, and the fact that the radical of the quadratic form of a symmetric bilinear form is its null space ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · radical_toQuadraticMap⟧. **Lemma 15.A (1).** In finite dimension, a negative definite and a positive definite subspace meet only in $0$, so their dimensions add up to at most $m$ ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · finrank_add_finrank_le⟧.

**Lemma 15.A (3): definiteness persists (V9.14 (3)).** Let $\tau \mapsto H_\tau$ be a family of bilinear forms indexed by a topological space, $S$ a finite-dimensional subspace, and suppose $\tau \mapsto H_\tau(x, y)$ is continuous at $\tau_0$ for $x, y \in S$. If $H_{\tau_0}$ is negative (positive) definite on $S$, so is $H_\tau$ for all $\tau$ near $\tau_0$ ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · eventually_isNegDefOn⟧ ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · eventually_isPosDefOn⟧. *Proof.* In a basis $b_i$ of $S$, $(\tau, c) \mapsto H_\tau(\sum c_ib_i, \sum c_ib_i) = \sum c_ic_jH_\tau(b_i, b_j)$ is continuous at every $(\tau_0, c)$. It is negative on the compact unit sphere of coefficients at $\tau_0$, hence negative on the whole sphere for $\tau$ near $\tau_0$, and by homogeneity on all of $S \setminus 0$. The versions within a set of parameters ⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · eventually_isNegDefOn_within⟧ ⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · eventually_isPosDefOn_within⟧ follow by restricting to the subspace. This is the perturbation step that Milnor uses without proof on p. 86.

**Milnor's compound matrix as a lemma (p. 87; V9.14 (5)).** Let $B$ be symmetric on a space $\mathcal P$, $U : \mathbb R^k \to \mathcal P$ linear with $B(Ua, Ua) < 0$ for $a \ne 0$, and $J, X : \mathbb R^\nu \to \mathcal P$ linear with
$$
B(Jb, Ua) = 0, \qquad B(Jb, Jb') = 0, \qquad B(Jb, Xb') = 2\langle b, b'\rangle .
$$
Then the index of $B$ is at least $k + \nu$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexAssertions · le_formIndex_of_compound⟧. Here $\langle b, b'\rangle = \sum_hb_hb'_h$ is the standard dot product on $\mathbb R^\nu$.

*Proof.* For $c \ne 0$ let $\Phi_c(a, b) = Ua + c^{-1}Jb - cXb$. Expanding with the three hypotheses,
$$
B\big(\Phi_c(a, b), \Phi_c(a', b')\big) = B(Ua, Ua') - c\big(B(Ua, Xb') + B(Xb, Ua')\big) - 4\langle b, b'\rangle + c^2B(Xb, Xb') =: G_c\big((a, b), (a', b')\big),
$$
the two cross terms $c^{-1}(-c)B(Jb, Xb')$ and $(-c)c^{-1}B(Xb, Jb')$ each contributing $-2\langle b, b'\rangle$. The form $G_0$ is negative definite on $\mathbb R^k \times \mathbb R^\nu$: if $a \ne 0$, then $B(Ua, Ua) < 0$ and $-4\langle b, b\rangle \le 0$, and if $a = 0$ and $b \ne 0$, then $-4\langle b, b\rangle < 0$. $G_c$ depends continuously on $c$, so by Lemma 15.A (3) it stays negative definite for small $c$ ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · eventually_isNegDefOn⟧; choose such a $c \ne 0$. Then $\Phi_c$ carries $G_c$ to $B$, and the index of $B$ is at least that of $G_c$ ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · formIndex_le_of_map⟧, namely $k + \nu$ ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · le_formIndex⟧. The linear independence of the $k + \nu$ fields, which Milnor's argument needs and does not mention, comes for free from the injectivity of form-preserving maps on negative definite subspaces.

### VII.19 Broken Jacobi fields in the second variation; positivity by a Poincaré estimate

**The second variation formula for piecewise Jacobi fields (Milnor pp. 84, 87; V9.15).** Let $\gamma \in \Omega(N; p, q)$, $X, Y \in T\Omega_\gamma$ and $B$ a finite set such that near every $t \in (0, 1) \setminus B$ the field $X$ agrees with some Jacobi field along $\gamma$. Then
$$
E_{**}(X, Y) = -2\sum_{b \in B \cap (0, 1)}\langle Y(b), P\Delta_bX'\rangle
$$
⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · energyHessian_eq_jumps⟧. $\gamma$ need not be a geodesic. *Proof.* In $\Phi$ the integrand vanishes off $B \cup \{1\}$. The Jacobi operator depends only on $X$ near $t$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · jacobiExpr_congr⟧, and the local Jacobi field satisfies the equation. The sum reduces to $B$, since $X$ is differentiable wherever it agrees with a smooth field. The zero field counts as a Jacobi field ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · isJacobiField_zero⟧, which is how fields that vanish on part of the interval are handled.

**The mesh $h_0$ (V9.16).** Along a smooth curve $\gamma$ in $N$ on $[0, 1]$, let $C \ge 0$ bound $\|P'(t)\|^2 + \|K(t)\|$ on $[0, 1]$; such a bound exists because the function is continuous on a compact interval ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · exists_jacBound⟧ ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · jacBound⟧ ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · jacBound_nonneg⟧ ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · le_jacBound⟧. Put $h_0 = 1/(C + 1) > 0$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · fineMesh⟧ ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · fineMesh_pos⟧. It depends only on $\gamma$, through bounds for $P'$ and for the curvature along $\gamma$.

**The integrand bound** ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · integrand_ge⟧. For a field $X$ tangent along $\gamma$ on $[0, 1]$ and differentiable at $t \in (0, 1)$,
$$
\Big\|\frac{DX}{dt}\Big\|^2 - \langle R(V, X)V, X\rangle \ \ge\ \|X'\|^2 - C\|X\|^2 .
$$
Since $X = PX$, $X' = P'X + PX'$, so the normal part of $X'$ is $P'X$. Pythagoras gives $\|X'\|^2 = \|PX'\|^2 + \|P'X\|^2 \le \|\frac{DX}{dt}\|^2 + \|P'\|^2\|X\|^2$, and $\langle R(V, X)V, X\rangle = \langle KX, X\rangle \le \|K\|\,\|X\|^2$.

**Poincaré's inequality on short pieces** ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · integral_norm_sq_le⟧. Let $X$ be piecewise smooth on $[0, 1]$, $h \ge 0$, and suppose every $t \in [0, 1]$ has a zero $s \in [0, t]$ of $X$ with $t - s \le h$. Then $\int_0^1\|X\|^2 \le h\int_0^1\|X'\|^2$. Indeed $\|X(t)\| = \|X(t) - X(s)\| \le L_s^t(X)$ ⟦Chapter7/Path · IsAdmissible.norm_sub_le_pathLength⟧, and Lemma 12.A gives $L_s^t(X)^2 \le (t - s)E_s^t(X) \le hE_0^1(X)$. Integrating over $[0, 1]$ gives the claim.

**The estimate (V9.16 (i)).** For $X \in T\Omega_\gamma$ with a zero within $h_0$ before every point of $[0, 1]$,
$$
\int_0^1\|X\|^2\,dt \ \le\ \int_0^1\Big(\Big\|\frac{DX}{dt}\Big\|^2 - \langle R(V, X)V, X\rangle\Big)dt = \tfrac12E_{**}(X, X)
$$
⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · hessForm_ge⟧. By the integrand bound, the integral is at least $\int\|X'\|^2 - C\int\|X\|^2$. By Poincaré, with $h_0(C + 1) = 1$, $\int\|X'\|^2 \ge (C + 1)\int\|X\|^2$. Hence the integral is at least $\int\|X\|^2$. So if moreover $E_{**}(X, X) \le 0$, then $X = 0$ on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · eqOn_zero_of_hessForm⟧: the continuous function $\|X\|^2$ has integral $0$.

**No two parameters closer than $h_0$ are conjugate (V9.16 (ii)).** If $J \in \mathcal J(\gamma)$ vanishes at $a$ and $b$ with $0 \le a < b \le 1$ and $b - a \le h_0$, then $J = 0$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · eq_zero_of_short⟧.

*Proof.* Let $X$ equal $J$ on $[a, b]$ and $0$ elsewhere. It is piecewise smooth with break points $\{a, b\}$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · isPiecewiseSmooth_indicator⟧ and lies in $T\Omega_\gamma$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · indicator_mem_pathTangent⟧. Off $\{a, b\}$ it agrees locally with a Jacobi field, $J$ or $0$, so the formula above ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · energyHessian_eq_jumps⟧ gives $E_{**}(X, X) = -2\sum\langle X(\cdot), \ldots\rangle = 0$, since $X$ vanishes at $a$ and $b$. Every point has a zero of $X$ within $h_0$ before it: $a$ for points of $[a, b]$, the point itself elsewhere. So $X = 0$ on $[0, 1]$ by the estimate ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · secondVariation_eq_hessForm⟧ ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · eqOn_zero_of_hessForm⟧. Then $J$ vanishes near the midpoint of $[a, b]$, so $J$ and $DJ/dt$ vanish there, and $J = 0$ by uniqueness ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsJacobiField.eqOn_zero⟧.

*Consequence: two-point boundary values on short segments* ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · jacEvalEnds_bijective⟧ ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · exists_jacobi_ends⟧. For $b - a \le h_0$, $J \mapsto (J(a), J(b))$ is an isomorphism $\mathcal J(\gamma) \to T_{\gamma(a)}N \times T_{\gamma(b)}N$. It is injective by the previous result ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · eq_zero_of_short⟧ and surjective by the dimension count ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · finrank_jacobiSpace⟧. This is the form of Remark 14.5 that the index theorem uses.

*Relation with Milnor's proof.* Milnor chooses the subdivision "sufficiently fine" (p. 83) that each segment lies in one of his neighbourhoods $U$ and is minimal. He then uses Remark 14.5 on each segment and the minimality of the segments (p. 84). Behind this lie a Lebesgue number for a finite cover and a length bound for minimality (Theorem 10.4, Part VI, §VI.12). The formal proof replaces all of it by the explicit mesh $h_0$, which comes from bounds on $P'$ and on the curvature along $\gamma$. Neither $\exp$ nor minimality is used, and $\gamma$ may be any smooth curve.

### VII.20 Fine subdivisions; Lemma 15.3 and Lemma 15.4

Milnor proves Lemmas 15.3 and 15.4 for $\gamma$ on $[0, 1]$ and applies them on p. 86 to the restrictions $\gamma_\tau = \gamma|[0, \tau]$. The formal proof works along $\gamma|[0, \tau]$ from the start, for every $\tau \in (0, 1]$. It represents a field along $\gamma_\tau$ by a field along $\gamma$ that vanishes beyond $\tau$.

**Definition (V9.17).** The *fields along $\gamma|[0, \tau]$* are the elements of $T\Omega_\gamma$ that vanish outside $[0, \tau]$ ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · fieldsUpTo⟧. They increase with $\tau$ ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · fieldsUpTo_mono⟧, and for $0 \le \tau \le 1$ they vanish at $\tau$ ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · fieldsUpTo_apply_self⟧, by continuity from the right, or from $X(1) = 0$ if $\tau = 1$. These are Milnor's fields along $\gamma_\tau$ vanishing at $0$ and $\tau$, extended by zero, which is the extension he uses in Assertion (1). By Proposition 13.A, $E_{**}$ on them is $2\int_0^\tau(\ldots)$, the integral that represents $(E_0^\tau)_{**}$. The Hessian on $[0, \tau]$ itself is never formalized and is not needed.

**Fine subdivisions (V9.16 (iii)).** A finite set $B \subseteq (0, \tau)$ is *fine for $[0, \tau]$* if every interval $[s, s'] \subseteq [0, \tau]$ whose interior contains no point of $B$ has length at most $h_0$ ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · IsFine⟧, that is, consecutive points of $\{0\} \cup B \cup \{\tau\}$ are at most $h_0$ apart. Every $t \in [0, \tau]$ lies between two consecutive points of this set ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · exists_node_piece⟧. Fine subdivisions exist, with the last point of $\{0\} \cup B$ within $h_0/2$ of $\tau$: take the grid points $kh_0/2$ in $(0, \tau)$ ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · exists_isFine⟧.

**Broken Jacobi fields (V9.17).** A map $X$ is a *broken Jacobi field on $[0, \tau]$* for $B$ if on every piece it agrees with a field of $\mathcal J(\gamma)$ ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · IsBrokenJacobi⟧. A piece is thus the restriction of a Jacobi field along all of $\gamma$. By §§VII.12–VII.13 this is equivalent to being a Jacobi field along $\gamma|[t_{i-1}, t_i]$, an equivalence not stated formally.

- *Uniqueness* (for which Milnor refers to 14.5): a broken Jacobi field vanishing at $0$, at $\tau$ and at all points of $B$ vanishes on $[0, \tau]$ ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · IsBrokenJacobi.eqOn_zero⟧. On each piece between consecutive division points ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · exists_node_piece⟧ it is a Jacobi field vanishing at both ends of a segment of length at most $h_0$, hence zero ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · eq_zero_of_short⟧.
- *Existence* with arbitrary tangent values at $0$, $\tau$ and the points of $B$ ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · exists_isBrokenJacobi⟧. The proof is an induction that adds the largest point $m$ of $B$. $B \setminus \{m\}$ is fine for $[0, m]$, so the induction hypothesis gives a broken field $X_0$ on $[0, m]$. On $[m, \tau]$, of length at most $h_0$, take the Jacobi field with the prescribed values at $m$ and $\tau$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · exists_jacobi_ends⟧. Glue them: $X_0$ for $t \le m$, the new field for $t > m$, both having the value $x(m)$ at $m$.

The broken Jacobi fields along $\gamma|[0, \tau]$, Milnor's $T\Omega_\gamma(t_0, \ldots, t_k)$, form the space ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · brokenSpace⟧, and the fields along $\gamma|[0, \tau]$ vanishing at the points of $B$, Milnor's $T'$, form ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · nodeZero⟧. Both lie among the fields along $\gamma|[0, \tau]$ ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · brokenSpace_le_fieldsUpTo⟧ ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · nodeZero_le_fieldsUpTo⟧. A broken Jacobi field vanishing at $0$, at $\tau$ and outside $[0, \tau]$ lies in $T\Omega_\gamma$, with break points $B \cup \{\tau\}$ ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · IsBrokenJacobi.mem_pathTangent⟧. Near every point of $(0, 1)$ other than $\tau$ and the points of $B$, it agrees with a Jacobi field, namely $0$ beyond $\tau$ ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · IsBrokenJacobi.eventuallyEq⟧. Hence, by the jump formula of §VII.19 ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · energyHessian_eq_jumps⟧, for a broken $X$ and any $Y$ along $\gamma|[0, \tau]$,
$$
E_{**}(X, Y) = -2\sum_{b \in B}\langle Y(b), P\Delta_bX'\rangle
$$
⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · energyHessian_broken_eq⟧. The term at $\tau$ drops out because $Y(\tau) = 0$ ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · fieldsUpTo_apply_self⟧.

**Lemma 15.3 (Milnor p. 84; V9.18).** Let $\gamma$ be a smooth curve in $N$ on $[0, 1]$, $0 < \tau \le 1$, and $B$ fine for $[0, \tau]$. (i) The fields along $\gamma|[0, \tau]$ are the direct sum of the broken Jacobi fields and $T'$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · isCompl_broken_nodeZero⟧. (ii) $E_{**}(W_1, W_2) = 0$ for $W_1$ broken and $W_2 \in T'$ ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · energyHessian_broken_nodeZero⟧. (iii) $E_{**}$ is positive definite on $T'$ ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · energyHessian_nodeZero_pos⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* (i) Every $X$ along $\gamma|[0, \tau]$ is $W_1 + (X - W_1)$, with $W_1$ the broken Jacobi field taking the values $X(b)$ at the points of $B$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · exists_brokenSpace_eq⟧. That field is built by the existence result with value $0$ at $0$ and $\tau$ ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · exists_isBrokenJacobi⟧, then cut off outside $[0, \tau]$ by an indicator, which keeps it in $T\Omega_\gamma$ ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · IsBrokenJacobi.mem_pathTangent⟧. The intersection of the two summands is $0$ by uniqueness ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · IsBrokenJacobi.eqOn_zero⟧. (ii) is the jump formula, with $W_2(b) = 0$. (iii) departs from Milnor's proof. A field of $T'$ has a zero within $h_0$ before every point: the point of $\{0\} \cup B$ that starts its piece, or the point itself beyond $\tau$. So the estimate of §VII.19 ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · eqOn_zero_of_hessForm⟧ gives $E_{**}(W, W) > 0$ unless $W = 0$ on $[0, 1]$, and such a $W$ vanishes everywhere, since it vanishes outside $[0, \tau]$. Milnor argues instead that $E_{**}(W, W) \ge 0$ because each segment is minimal, and that $E_{**}(W, W) = 0$ puts $W$ in the null space, so that $W$ is a Jacobi field (Theorem 14.1), hence $0$ by Remark 14.5. The formal proof uses no minimal geodesics, no variations and no Theorem 14.1.

**Lemma 15.4 (Milnor p. 85; V9.19).** In the same situation, the index of $E_{**}$ on the fields along $\gamma|[0, \tau]$ equals its index on the broken Jacobi fields ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · lemma_15_4⟧. The null space of $E_{**}$ on the fields along $\gamma|[0, \tau]$ is that of its restriction to the broken Jacobi fields ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · lemma_15_4_ker⟧.

*Proof.* This is Lemma 15.B ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · formIndex_eq_restrict_of_isCompl⟧ ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · ker_eq_map_ker_restrict⟧ applied to Lemma 15.3, with $A$ the broken fields and $C = T'$, both viewed inside the fields along $\gamma|[0, \tau]$. Perpendicularity in the other order follows from the symmetry of $E_{**}$ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · energyHessian_isSymm⟧. A linear isomorphism then identifies the two descriptions of the broken fields ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · formIndex_eq_of_equiv⟧.

**The finite-dimensional space $\Sigma$ (Milnor p. 86).** $\Sigma = \bigoplus_{b \in B}T_{\gamma(b)}N$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · nodeSpace⟧. The map $W \mapsto (W(b))_{b \in B}$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · nodeEval⟧ is an isomorphism from the broken Jacobi fields onto $\Sigma$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · nodeEval_bijective⟧ ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · nodeEquiv⟧, by uniqueness and existence ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · IsBrokenJacobi.eqOn_zero⟧ ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · exists_brokenSpace_eq⟧. So the broken Jacobi fields form a finite-dimensional space ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · finiteDimensional_brokenSpace⟧.

**The nullity on the broken Jacobi fields is $\nu(\tau)$ (V9.19)** ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · finrank_ker_broken⟧. Map $J \mapsto \mathbf 1_{[0,\tau]}J$, from the Jacobi fields vanishing at $0$ and $\tau$ into $T\Omega_\gamma$. It is injective, because a field of $\mathcal J(\gamma)$ that vanishes on an interval vanishes ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · jacobiSpace_eq_zero_of_eqOn⟧. Its range is the null space of $E_{**}$ on the broken fields, by two inclusions.

1. $\mathbf 1_{[0,\tau]}J$ is broken, being one Jacobi field on every piece, and it has no jumps at the points of $B$, where it is differentiable. So it is null by the jump formula ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · energyHessian_broken_eq⟧.
2. Let $X$ be broken and null. Test it against the broken field $Y$ with $Y(b) = P\Delta_bX'$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · exists_brokenSpace_eq⟧: the jump formula gives $-2\sum_b\|P\Delta_bX'\|^2 = 0$, so $X$ has no tangential jumps. A broken Jacobi field without tangential jumps is a single Jacobi field on $[0, \tau]$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · eq_jacobi_of_jumps_zero⟧. The proof is an induction on the largest point $m$ of $B$. If $X = J_1$ on $[0, m]$ and $X = J_2$ on $[m, \tau]$, then $J_2 - J_1$ vanishes at $m$ and has covariant derivative $P(J_2'(m) - J_1'(m)) = P\Delta_mX' = 0$ there, so $J_1 = J_2$ by uniqueness ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsJacobiField.eqOn_zero⟧. That Jacobi field vanishes at $0$ and at $\tau$, so $X = \mathbf 1_{[0,\tau]}J$.

Milnor obtains this nullity on p. 86 from Theorem 14.1 applied to $\gamma_\tau$. The formal proof gets it directly, along any smooth curve.

### VII.21 $\lambda(\tau)$: monotonicity, finiteness, small $\tau$

**Definition (Milnor p. 85; V9.20).** For a smooth curve $\gamma$ in $N$ on $[0, 1]$ and $\tau \in \mathbb R$, $\lambda(\tau)$ is the index of $E_{**}$ restricted to the fields along $\gamma|[0, \tau]$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · indexUpTo⟧. In particular $\lambda(1)$ is the index of $E_{**}$ on all of $T\Omega_\gamma$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · indexUpTo_one⟧. One inequality holds because restriction does not increase the index ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · formIndex_restrict_le⟧. For the other, $X \mapsto \mathbf 1_{[0,1]}X$ maps $T\Omega_\gamma$ into the fields along $\gamma|[0, 1]$ and preserves $E_{**}$, which depends only on the values on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · secondVariation_congr⟧ ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · formIndex_le_of_map⟧. Milnor's $\lambda(\tau)$ is the index of the Hessian $(E_0^\tau)_{**}$ of $\gamma_\tau$. Defining it by restriction makes Assertion (1) immediate, and no rescaling of $\gamma_\tau$ to $[0, 1]$ (the exposition's Lemma 13.B) is needed.

**Assertion (1) (Milnor p. 85).** $\lambda(\tau) \le \lambda(\tau')$ for $\tau \le \tau'$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · indexUpTo_mono⟧: the inclusion of fields preserves $E_{**}$, so the index can only grow ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · formIndex_le_of_map⟧. Milnor's argument extends fields by zero and needs this extension to preserve the Hessian. That is the integral form, and here it is built into the definition.

**Finiteness (Lemma 15.4).** For $0 < \tau \le 1$ and $B$ fine for $[0, \tau]$, $\lambda(\tau)$ equals the index on the broken Jacobi fields ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · lemma_15_4⟧, so it is at most their finite dimension ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · indexUpTo_le_finrank⟧. Since fine subdivisions exist ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · exists_isFine⟧, $\lambda(\tau) < \infty$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · indexUpTo_lt_top⟧.

**Assertion (2) (Milnor p. 85).** $\lambda(\tau) = 0$ for $0 < \tau \le h_0$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · indexUpTo_eq_zero⟧. A field along $\gamma|[0, \tau]$ with $\tau \le h_0$ has a zero within $h_0$ before every point: $0$ for points up to $\tau$, the point itself beyond. By the estimate of §VII.19 ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · eqOn_zero_of_hessForm⟧, $E_{**}(X, X) < 0$ forces $X = 0$, so every negative definite subspace is $0$. Milnor's argument is that $\gamma_\tau$ is a minimal geodesic for small $\tau$, which needs Theorem 10.4, so that $\lambda(\tau) = 0$ by Lemma 13.6. Here $E_{**}$ is even positive definite on these fields, and minimality is not used.

### VII.22 The forms $H_\tau$ and their continuity

On p. 86 Milnor chooses the subdivision with $t_i < \tau < t_{i+1}$. He observes that $\lambda(\tau)$ is the index of a quadratic form $H_\tau$ on the broken Jacobi fields along $\gamma_\tau$, a space isomorphic to $\Sigma = TM_{\gamma(t_1)} \oplus \cdots \oplus TM_{\gamma(t_i)}$, which does not depend on $\tau$, and that "evidently" $H_\tau$ varies continuously with $\tau$. The formal proof makes $H_\tau$ precise and proves the continuity.

**Definitions (V9.21).** Fix a finite set $B$. A parameter $\tau$ is *valid* if $0 < \tau \le 1$ and $B$ is fine for $[0, \tau]$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · validFor⟧. For valid $\tau$ and $x \in \Sigma$, $W^\tau_x$ is the broken Jacobi field along $\gamma|[0, \tau]$ with values $x$ at the points of $B$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · brokenOf⟧ ⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · brokenOf_mem⟧ ⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · brokenOf_apply_node⟧, and
$$
H_\tau(x, y) = E_{**}(W^\tau_x, W^\tau_y)
$$
⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · Hform⟧ ⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · Hform_apply⟧, with $H_\tau = 0$ for $\tau$ not valid. $H_\tau$ is symmetric ⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · Hform_isSymm⟧. For valid $\tau$, its index is $\lambda(\tau)$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · formIndex_Hform⟧, by Lemma 15.4 ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · lemma_15_4⟧ and the isomorphism with $\Sigma$ ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · formIndex_eq_of_equiv⟧, and its nullity is $\nu(\tau)$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · finrank_ker_Hform⟧, the null space being transported by the same isomorphism ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · finrank_ker_broken⟧. By the jump formula ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · energyHessian_broken_eq⟧, $H_\tau(x, y) = -2\sum_b\langle y_b, P\Delta_b(W^\tau_x)'\rangle$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · Hform_eq_jumps⟧.

**Validity near $\tau$** ⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · isFine_of_isFine⟧ ⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · mem_validFor_of_near⟧. Suppose $B$ is fine for $[0, \tau]$ and the last point $m$ of $\{0\} \cup B$ is within $h_0/2$ of $\tau$. Then every $\tau' \in (m, \tau + h_0/2)$ with $\tau' \le 1$ is valid. The pieces before $m$ are unchanged, and the last piece $[m, \tau']$ is shorter than $h_0$. Milnor's argument silently needs the subdivision to remain admissible for $\tau'$ near $\tau$, and this is that fact.

**Lemma 15.C of the exposition (Milnor p. 86, "evidently"; V9.21).** For $x, y \in \Sigma$, $\tau \mapsto H_\tau(x, y)$ is continuous on the set of valid $\tau$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · continuousWithinAt_Hform⟧.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. *Reduction to the jumps.* In $H_\tau(x, y) = -2\sum_b\langle y_b, P\Delta_b(W^\tau_x)'\rangle$ the vectors $y_b$ are fixed. It suffices that $\tau \mapsto P\Delta_b(W^\tau_x)'$ be continuous on the valid $\tau$ for each $b \in B$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · continuousWithinAt_jump⟧.
2. *Agreement up to the last division point* ⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · brokenOf_eqOn⟧. For valid $\tau$ and $\tau'$, $W^\tau_x$ and $W^{\tau'}_x$ agree on $[0, m]$, where $m = \max B$. Their difference is a broken Jacobi field on $[0, m]$ for $B \setminus \{m\}$, which is fine for $[0, m]$, and it vanishes at $0$, at $m$ and at the other points, so it is $0$ ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · IsBrokenJacobi.eqOn_zero⟧. Hence for $b < m$ the jump does not depend on $\tau$. At $b = m$ the left derivative is fixed, and only the right derivative depends on $\tau$.
3. *The right derivative at $m$ solves a linear system.* On $[m, \tau]$, $W^\tau_x$ is the Jacobi field $Y^\tau$ with $Y^\tau(m) = x_m$ and $Y^\tau(\tau) = 0$. Let $z(\tau) = \frac{DY^\tau}{dt}(m)$, the projected right derivative. $Y^\tau$ is the first component of the solution with data $(x_m, z(\tau))$ at $m$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsJacobiField.eqOn_jacSol⟧, which by linearity is the solution with data $(x_m, 0)$ plus the solution with data $(0, z(\tau))$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · jacSol_add⟧. Evaluating at $\tau$ gives $L(\tau)z(\tau) = c(\tau)$. Here $L(\tau)z$ is the first component at time $\tau$ of the solution with data $(0, z)$ at $m$, and $c(\tau)$ is minus the first component at time $\tau$ of the solution with data $(x_m, 0)$. Both $L$ and $c$ are continuous in $\tau$, by the continuity of the propagator ⟦Chapter9/JacobiODE · continuous_linFlow⟧ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiODE · continuous_linSol⟧.
4. *$L(\tau_0)$ is injective.* If $L(\tau_0)w = 0$, the Jacobi field with $J(m) = 0$ and $\frac{DJ}{dt}(m) = w$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · isJacobiField_jacSol⟧ vanishes at $m$ and at $\tau_0$, which are at most $h_0$ apart by fineness. So $J = 0$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · eq_zero_of_short⟧, and $w = 0$.
5. *Continuity of the solution of an injective linear system* ⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · continuousWithinAt_of_injective⟧. Suppose $L(\tau)z(\tau) = c(\tau)$, with $L$ and $c$ continuous at $\tau_0$ and $L(\tau_0)$ injective on a finite-dimensional space. Then $\|v\| \le K\|L(\tau_0)v\|$ for some $K$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Normed/Module/FiniteDimension · LinearMap.exists_antilipschitzWith⟧. For $\tau$ near $\tau_0$, $\|L(\tau) - L(\tau_0)\| < 1/(2K)$, hence $\|v\| \le 2K\|L(\tau)v\|$. Then $\|z(\tau) - z(\tau_0)\| \le 2K\|c(\tau) - L(\tau)z(\tau_0)\|$, which tends to $2K\|c(\tau_0) - L(\tau_0)z(\tau_0)\| = 0$. This replaces Cramer's rule in the exposition's proof.

*What "evidently" hides:* the non-conjugacy of $t_i$ and $\tau$, without which $Y^\tau$ is not even defined; the linear dependence of Jacobi fields on their initial data; and the continuity of the solution of an injective linear system.

### VII.23 Assertions (3) and (4): the jumps at conjugate points

**Assertion (3) (Milnor p. 85; V9.22).** For $0 < \tau \le 1$, $\lambda(\tau') = \lambda(\tau)$ for all $\tau' < \tau$ close enough to $\tau$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexAssertions · assertion_3⟧.

*Proof* (Milnor's). Take $B$ fine for $[0, \tau]$ with last point $m < \tau$ within $h_0/2$ of $\tau$ ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · exists_isFine⟧ ⟦Chapter9/IndexAssertions · max_lt_of_isFine⟧. Then $\lambda(\tau) = k$ is finite ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · indexUpTo_lt_top⟧ and attained by a subspace $S \subseteq \Sigma$ on which $H_\tau$ is negative definite ⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · formIndex_Hform⟧ ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · exists_isNegDefOn_of_formIndex_eq⟧. By continuity ⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · continuousWithinAt_Hform⟧ and Lemma 15.A (3) ⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · eventually_isNegDefOn_within⟧, $H_{\tau'}$ is negative definite on $S$ for valid $\tau'$ near $\tau$, and every $\tau' \in (m, \tau)$ is valid ⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · mem_validFor_of_near⟧. So $\lambda(\tau') \ge k$, and Assertion (1) gives $\lambda(\tau') \le \lambda(\tau)$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · indexUpTo_mono⟧. *Hidden hypothesis:* that $\lambda(\tau)$ is finite and attained (§§VII.18, VII.21).

**Assertion (4), the upper bound (Milnor p. 86).** For $0 < \tau < 1$, $\lambda(\tau') \le \lambda(\tau) + \nu(\tau)$ for all $\tau' > \tau$ close enough to $\tau$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexAssertions · assertion_4_le⟧.

*Proof.* By Sylvester's law ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · exists_isPosDefOn_add⟧, $H_\tau$ is positive definite on a subspace $P$ of dimension $\dim\Sigma - \lambda(\tau) - \nu(\tau)$, its index being $\lambda(\tau)$ ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · formIndex_eq_sigNeg⟧ and its nullity $\nu(\tau)$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · finrank_ker_Hform⟧. For valid $\tau'$ near $\tau$, which includes all $\tau' \in (\tau, \min(\tau + h_0/2, 1))$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · mem_validFor_of_near⟧, $P$ stays positive definite for $H_{\tau'}$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexContinuity · eventually_isPosDefOn_within⟧. A negative definite subspace for $H_{\tau'}$ then has dimension at most $\dim\Sigma - \dim P = \lambda(\tau) + \nu(\tau)$ by Lemma 15.A (1) ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · finrank_add_finrank_le⟧. This is Milnor's count with $\dim\Sigma = ni$. *Hidden hypotheses:* Sylvester's law, and the fact that the nullity of $H_\tau$ is $\nu(\tau)$, which needs Lemma 15.4 and Theorem 14.1 for $\gamma_\tau$ in the book and §VII.20 here.

**The Hessian against a cut-off Jacobi field (Milnor p. 87; V9.23 (ii)).** Let $0 < \tau < 1$, let $J \in \mathcal J(\gamma)$ vanish at $0$ and $\tau$, and let $\hat J = \mathbf 1_{[0,\tau]}J \in T\Omega_\gamma$. For every $Z \in T\Omega_\gamma$,
$$
E_{**}(\hat J, Z) = 2\Big\langle Z(\tau), \frac{DJ}{dt}(\tau)\Big\rangle
$$
⟦Chapter9/IndexAssertions · energyHessian_cut⟧. Off $\tau$, $\hat J$ is locally $J$ or $0$. At $\tau$ its left derivative is $J'(\tau)$, and its right derivative is $0$, since $\hat J$ vanishes from $\tau$ on. So $P\Delta_\tau\hat J' = -\frac{DJ}{dt}(\tau)$, and the jump formula ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · energyHessian_eq_jumps⟧ gives $E_{**}(\hat J, Z) = -2\langle Z(\tau), -\frac{DJ}{dt}(\tau)\rangle$.

**The fields $X_k$ (Milnor p. 87; V9.23 (iii))** ⟦Chapter9/IndexAssertions · exists_dual⟧. Linearly independent tangent vectors $d_1, \ldots, d_\nu$ at a point of $N$ have tangent vectors $x_1, \ldots, x_\nu$ at that point with $\langle d_h, x_k\rangle = \delta_{hk}$. The map $u \mapsto (\langle d_h, u\rangle)_h$ from the span of the $d_h$ to $\mathbb R^\nu$ is injective, since a vector orthogonal to all $d_h$ in their span is orthogonal to itself. It is therefore onto, and the preimages of the standard basis are the $x_k$.

**Assertion (4), the lower bound, for every $\tau' > \tau$ (Milnor pp. 86–87; V9.23 (iv)).** For $0 < \tau < \tau' \le 1$, $\lambda(\tau) + \nu(\tau) \le \lambda(\tau')$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexAssertions · assertion_4_ge⟧.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. *The negative directions up to $\tau$.* $\lambda(\tau) = k$ is attained ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · exists_isNegDefOn_of_formIndex_eq⟧ by a subspace $S$ of fields along $\gamma|[0, \tau]$ on which $E_{**}$ is negative definite. A basis $W_1, \ldots, W_k$ of $S$ gives the linear map $U : \mathbb R^k \to \{\text{fields along } \gamma|[0, \tau']\}$.
2. *The new directions.* Let $J_1, \ldots, J_\nu$ be a basis of the Jacobi fields vanishing at $0$ and $\tau$, and $d_h = \frac{DJ_h}{dt}(\tau)$. The $d_h$ are linearly independent, because $J \mapsto \frac{DJ}{dt}(\tau)$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · jacEval⟧ is injective on these fields by uniqueness ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsJacobiField.eqOn_zero⟧, as $J(\tau) = 0$. Take the dual vectors $x_k$ at $\gamma(\tau)$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexAssertions · exists_dual⟧ and a bump function $\varphi$ at $\tau$ ⟦Chapter8/Critical · bumpAt⟧ of radius $r = \min(\tau, \tau' - \tau)$, equal to $1$ at $\tau$. Then $X_k = \varphi\,P x_k$ is smooth, tangent, zero at $0$, at $1$ and outside $[0, \tau']$, and satisfies $X_k(\tau) = x_k$. The cut-off fields $\hat J_h = \mathbf 1_{[0,\tau]}J_h$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · indicator_mem_pathTangent⟧ are fields along $\gamma|[0, \tau] \subseteq$ fields along $\gamma|[0, \tau']$, and $\hat J_h(\tau) = 0$.
3. *The compound matrix.* Apply the lemma of §VII.18 ⟦Chapter9/IndexAssertions · le_formIndex_of_compound⟧ to $E_{**}$ on the fields along $\gamma|[0, \tau']$, with $U$ as above, $Jb = \sum_hb_h\hat J_h$ and $Xb = \sum_kb_kX_k$. By the cut-off formula ⟦Chapter9/IndexAssertions · energyHessian_cut⟧, $E_{**}(Jb, Z) = 2\langle Z(\tau), \sum_hb_hd_h\rangle$. Hence $E_{**}(Jb, Ua) = 0$ because fields along $\gamma|[0, \tau]$ vanish at $\tau$ ⟦Chapter9/BrokenJacobi · fieldsUpTo_apply_self⟧; $E_{**}(Jb, Jb') = 0$ because $\hat J_h(\tau) = 0$; and $E_{**}(Jb, Xb') = 2\langle\sum_kb'_kx_k, \sum_hb_hd_h\rangle = 2\langle b, b'\rangle$. So the index on the fields along $\gamma|[0, \tau']$ is at least $k + \nu$.

*What Milnor leaves implicit:* the linear independence of the $DJ_h/dt(\tau)$ (uniqueness), the construction of the $X_k$, and the linear independence of the $\lambda + \nu$ fields $W_i$, $c^{-1}J_h - cX_h$, which the compound-matrix lemma supplies. *Deviation (stronger).* The lower bound holds for every $\tau' \in (\tau, 1]$, not only for $\tau'$ near $\tau$.

**Assertion (4) (Milnor p. 86; V9.23).** For $0 < \tau < 1$, $\lambda(\tau') = \lambda(\tau) + \nu(\tau)$ for all $\tau' > \tau$ close enough to $\tau$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexAssertions · assertion_4⟧, by the upper bound and the lower bound ⟦Chapter9/IndexAssertions · assertion_4_le⟧ ⟦Chapter9/IndexAssertions · assertion_4_ge⟧. Milnor's $\nu$ is the nullity of $(E_0^\tau)_{**}$. Here it is the multiplicity $\nu(\tau)$ of $0$ and $\tau$, which by §§VII.20 and VII.22 is the nullity of $E_{**}$ along $\gamma|[0, \tau]$. So $\lambda$ jumps by $\nu(\tau)$ just after a conjugate parameter $\tau$ and is continuous from the left.

### VII.24 Theorem 15.1, Corollary 15.2 and Remark 14.6

Milnor writes that the index theorem "clearly follows" from Assertions (2), (3) and (4) (p. 87). The formal proof isolates the deduction as a statement about integer-valued functions.

**The deduction from the Assertions (V9.24 (i)).** Let $\ell, \nu : \mathbb R \to \{0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$ and $h_0 > 0$. Suppose $\ell$ is non-decreasing on $(0, 1]$, vanishes on $(0, h_0]$, satisfies $\ell(\tau') = \ell(\tau)$ for $\tau' < \tau$ near $\tau$ when $0 < \tau \le 1$, and satisfies $\ell(\tau') = \ell(\tau) + \nu(\tau)$ for $\tau' > \tau$ near $\tau$ when $0 < \tau < 1$. Then $\nu(t) \ne 0$ for only finitely many $t \in (0, 1)$, and $\ell(1) = \sum_{0 < t < 1}\nu(t)$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexTheorem · eq_finsum_of_jumps⟧.

*Proof.*

1. *The jumps before $\tau$ add up to at most $\ell(\tau)$.* For a finite $F \subseteq (0, 1)$ with all points below $\tau \le 1$, $\sum_F\nu \le \ell(\tau)$. By induction, adding the largest point $a$ of $F$: there is $\tau' \in (a, \tau)$ with $\ell(\tau') = \ell(a) + \nu(a)$, so $\ell(\tau) \ge \ell(a) + \nu(a) \ge \sum_{F \setminus a}\nu + \nu(a)$.
2. *Finiteness.* If infinitely many $t \in (0, 1)$ had $\nu(t) \ne 0$, then $\ell(1) + 1$ of them would give $\sum\nu \ge \ell(1) + 1$, contradicting step 1.
3. *No jumps near $0$.* For $t < \min(h_0, 1)$, take $\tau' \in (t, h_0)$ with $\ell(\tau') = \ell(t) + \nu(t)$. Both values of $\ell$ are $0$, so $\nu(t) = 0$.
4. *A locally constant function.* With $C$ the finite set of jumps, $g(\tau) = \ell(\tau) - \sum_{t \in C,\, t < \tau}\nu(t)$ is locally constant on $(0, 1]$. From the left this follows from the left continuity, the sum being unchanged just below $\tau$. From the right, $\ell$ gains $\nu(\tau)$ and so does the sum; if $\tau \notin C$, then $\nu(\tau) = 0$. A locally constant integer-valued function on the connected set $(0, 1]$ is constant. Since $g(\min(h_0, 1)/2) = 0$, we get $g(1) = 0$, that is, $\ell(1) = \sum_C\nu$.

*What "clearly" hides.* The conjugate parameters must first be shown to be finite in number, from Assertions (1) and (4) and the finiteness of $\lambda(1)$. Without that, the jumps could not be added up. Near $0$, Assertion (4) also excludes conjugate parameters, which Assertion (2) alone does not.

**Theorem 15.1 along any smooth curve (V9.24 (ii)).** For every smooth curve $\gamma$ in $N$ on $[0, 1]$, $\nu(t) \ne 0$ for only finitely many $t \in (0, 1)$, and $\lambda(1) = \sum_{0 < t < 1}\nu(t)$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexTheorem · indexUpTo_one_eq⟧. Apply the deduction ⟦Chapter9/IndexTheorem · eq_finsum_of_jumps⟧ to $\ell = \lambda$, which takes natural-number values on $(0, 1]$ by finiteness ⟦Chapter9/IndexTheorem · indexUpTo_toNat⟧, and to $h_0$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · fineMesh_pos⟧, using Assertions (1)–(4) ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · indexUpTo_mono⟧ ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · indexUpTo_eq_zero⟧ ⟦Chapter9/IndexAssertions · assertion_3⟧ ⟦Chapter9/IndexAssertions · assertion_4⟧.

**Theorem 15.1 (the Morse index theorem, Milnor p. 83; V9.24).** Let $\gamma \in \Omega(N; p, q)$ be an unbroken geodesic. The parameters $t \in (0, 1)$ conjugate to $0$ along $\gamma$ form a finite set, and the index of $E_{**}$ on $T\Omega_\gamma$ is
$$
\lambda = \sum_{0 < t < 1}\nu(t),
$$
the number of these parameters, each counted with its multiplicity. In particular $\lambda$ is finite ⟦Chapter9/IndexTheorem · theorem_15_1⟧.

*Proof.* Apply the previous theorem ⟦Chapter9/IndexTheorem · indexUpTo_one_eq⟧ to the smooth curve $\gamma$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsUnbrokenGeodesic.isSmoothCurveIn⟧. The conjugate parameters in $(0, 1)$ are those with $\nu(t) \ne 0$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · isConjugate_iff⟧, and the index of $E_{**}$ is $\lambda(1)$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · indexUpTo_one⟧. Milnor's statement concerns the Hessian of the energy at a critical point, and that is where the geodesic hypothesis enters. The proof uses only the integral form of $E_{**}$ along a smooth curve. Along a curve that is not a geodesic, the same equality holds for the form $E_{**}$ of §VII.9, which is then not a Hessian.

**Corollary 15.2 (Milnor p. 83; V9.25).** For an unbroken geodesic $\gamma \in \Omega(N; p, q)$, the set of parameters conjugate to $0$ along $\gamma$ is finite ⟦Chapter9/IndexTheorem · corollary_15_2⟧. It consists of the finitely many conjugate parameters in $(0, 1)$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexTheorem · theorem_15_1⟧, together with possibly $1$.

**Remark 14.6 (Milnor p. 82; V9.12).** For every smooth curve $\gamma$ in $N$ on $[0, 1]$, no $t \in (0, \delta)$ is conjugate to $0$, where $\delta = \min(h_0, 1)$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexTheorem · remark_14_6⟧. A Jacobi field vanishing at $0$ and at $t \le h_0$ vanishes ⟦Chapter9/IndexForm · eq_zero_of_short⟧, while conjugacy means a non-zero one exists ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · isConjugate_iff⟧. Milnor's neighbourhood $U$ is not used. Two parts of the remark are not formalized: negative parameters, and the absence of cluster points of conjugate points along an entire geodesic, since the geodesics of $\Omega$ are given on $[0, 1]$.

### VII.25 What the formalization taught us

- **$T\Omega_\omega$ is a definition.** It is connected to $\Omega$ through variations in both directions, and the direction "every field is a variation field" (Lemma 11.A) needs a radius uniform along $\omega$, obtained from compactness. It does not need completeness. Every derivative on $\Omega$ is a derivative of a function of finitely many real variables.
- **The variation formulas rest on calculus on closed strips.** Differentiation under the integral sign needs the compactness of closed strips. The jump terms need the symmetry of mixed partials on the edges of the strips, where the $t$-derivatives are one-sided. (12.2′) is needed at every $u$, since §13 differentiates it. Milnor's reference to "Lemma 8.5" should be to Lemma 8.7. The scan loses a minus sign in the displayed statements of 12.2 and 13.1.
- **Test fields need care.** In Corollary 12.3 and Theorem 14.1 the test fields exist only by Lemma 11.A. They must be continuous at the break points and must be constructed explicitly. The step "by uniqueness" needs a derivative-extension argument at a former break point.
- **Symmetry of $E_{**}$** follows in the book from mixed partials of $E \circ \bar\alpha$, a function never shown to be $C^2$. The integral form gives symmetry and bilinearity directly.
- **Jacobi fields need a global theory.** Solutions defined along all of $\gamma$ need global existence for linear equations. Remark 14.2 fails for a constant geodesic and needs $n \ge 1$.
- **Lemma 14.4** needs $\gamma|[0, \delta]$ to be *the* minimal geodesic between its end points, which Milnor does not argue, and a compactness step that is really smooth dependence on initial data. Building the variation from initial data avoids the first issue and makes the second explicit.
- **One estimate replaces minimality three times.** The two properties of a fine subdivision and Assertion (2) all follow from the Poincaré estimate of §VII.19, with an explicit mesh depending only on bounds for $P'$ and the curvature. "Evidently continuous" needs non-conjugacy on the last segment and a perturbation lemma. Lemma 15.4's "straightforward" proof is an abstract splitting lemma.
- **Theorem 15.1** needs the conjugate parameters to be finite in number before the jumps of $\lambda$ can be added up. Assembled correctly, the proof works along any smooth curve, with $\lambda$ defined by restriction to the fields vanishing beyond $\tau$.
