## Part 0. The formal definitions

This part makes the book self-contained. It defines, in ordinary mathematical language and exactly as the formal proof defines them, the notions on which every later part is built: critical points and their indices, submanifolds of Euclidean space with their induced geometry, sublevel sets and deformation retracts, cells and cell complexes, singular homology, homotopy groups, path spaces with Milnor's metric, geodesics, Jacobi fields and the index form, and flows of vector fields. Each definition carries a trace tag. A tag whose path begins with `Mathlib/` points into Mathlib; one beginning with `Milnor/` points into the formalization of *Topology from the Differentiable Viewpoint*, called the companion project below, which is a Lake dependency of this one; every other tag points into this project. The parts that follow add the special definitions of their chapters (Milnor's function $\mu$ and the Morse charts of Theorem 3.2, focal points, broken geodesics, Clifford modules, and so on). Everything they build on is here, and for each definition the text says why it is the right formal counterpart of Milnor's notion and where it is not literally his.

Three settings recur, and it is worth separating them at once. Milnor's notions of §2 (critical points, the Hessian, its index and nullity) are defined on abstract manifolds in Mathlib's sense (§0.2), with no Hausdorff or countability hypothesis. Almost every theorem of the book from Theorem 3.1 on, the index theorem and the fundamental theorem of §17 included, is proved for a submanifold $N$ of a Euclidean space in the sense of the companion project, with the Riemannian metric induced by the ambient inner product (§§0.1, 0.3). Every Riemannian manifold is isometric to one of these, by Nash's embedding theorem, which is quoted for orientation and used in no proof. The topology (deformation retracts, cell attachments, homology, homotopy groups, telescopes) is done for arbitrary topological spaces (§§0.4–0.7). A formal statement carries every hypothesis it uses, so Hausdorffness, compactness, finite dimension, connectedness and completeness appear exactly where they are needed.

### 0.1 The companion foundations

The project rests on the foundations laid in Part 0 of the companion volume. They are recalled here in outline, with tags into the companion project and into Mathlib; nothing is re-derived.

**Calculus.** All vector spaces are real normed spaces. A map $f : E \to F$ has derivative $L$ at $x$, a continuous linear map, when $f(x + h) = f(x) + Lh + o(\|h\|)$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/FDeriv/Defs · HasFDerivAt⟧. The formal derivative $Df(x)$ is this $L$ when it exists and $0$ otherwise ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/FDeriv/Defs · fderiv⟧, and the derivative *within* a set uses only increments that stay in the set ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/FDeriv/Defs · fderivWithin⟧. For a function of one real variable the derivative is the vector $f'(t)$, and the derivative within $[t, \infty)$ or $(-\infty, t]$ is a one-sided derivative ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/Deriv/Basic · deriv⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/Deriv/Basic · derivWithin⟧; the jumps $\Delta_t$ of §12 and the derivatives of a path at its ends are read this way. Smooth means $C^\infty$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/ContDiff/Defs · ContDiff⟧. Smoothness at a point and on a set are local, order-by-order notions ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/ContDiff/Defs · ContDiffAt⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/ContDiff/Defs · ContDiffOn⟧; for a function on $\mathbb R$, "smooth on $[a, b]$" includes one-sided derivatives of all orders at $a$ and $b$. $\mathbb R^n$ is the space of functions $\{0, \dots, n-1\} \to \mathbb R$ with the Euclidean norm ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/InnerProductSpace/PiL2 · EuclideanSpace⟧, so the formal text numbers coordinates from $0$. The gradient $\nabla F(x)$ of a real function on an inner product space is the vector with $\langle \nabla F(x), h \rangle = DF(x)h$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/Gradient/Basic · gradient⟧. Milnor's $\mathbb R^K$ is usually replaced by a finite-dimensional real inner product space $V$, which changes nothing but the notation.

**Manifolds.** A charted space modelled on $H$ is a topological space $M$ with an atlas of charts, each an open partial homeomorphism from an open subset of $M$ onto an open subset of $H$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/OpenPartialHomeomorph/Defs · OpenPartialHomeomorph⟧, and with a *preferred chart* at each point ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/ChartedSpace · ChartedSpace⟧. The model is embedded in a normed space $E$ by a model with corners ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/IsManifold/Basic · ModelWithCorners⟧. In this book the model is always the identity of $E$, written $\mathcal I(\mathbb R, E)$ ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/IsManifold/Basic · modelWithCornersSelf⟧, which is boundaryless ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/IsManifold/Basic · ModelWithCorners.Boundaryless⟧: every manifold of the formal text is a manifold without boundary. $M$ is a smooth manifold when any two of its charts are smoothly compatible ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/IsManifold/Basic · IsManifold⟧; the charts compatible with all of them form the maximal atlas ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/IsManifold/Basic · IsManifold.maximalAtlas⟧. Nothing else is required: unless a statement says so, a manifold need not be Hausdorff, second countable or finite-dimensional. The extended chart $\varphi_x$ is the preferred chart at $x$, read in $E$ ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/IsManifold/ExtChartAt · extChartAt⟧, and the local representative of $f : M \to N$ at $x$ is $\varphi_{f(x)} \circ f \circ \varphi_x^{-1}$ ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/IsManifold/ExtChartAt · writtenInExtChartAt⟧. $f$ is smooth at $x$ when it is continuous at $x$ and its local representative is smooth at $\varphi_x(x)$ ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/ContMDiff/Defs · ContMDiffAt⟧, and smooth on a set, or everywhere, in the same way ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/ContMDiff/Defs · ContMDiffOn⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/ContMDiff/Defs · ContMDiff⟧. The tangent space $T_xM$ is, by definition, the model space $E$ ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/IsManifold/Basic · TangentSpace⟧, and the derivative $df_x : E \to F$ is the derivative of the local representative at $\varphi_x(x)$, or $0$ when $f$ is not differentiable at $x$ ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/MFDeriv/Defs · mfderiv⟧; the companion project restates it with the type "continuous linear map $E \to F$" and nothing else ⟦Milnor/Chapter5/Orient · mfd⟧. So $df_x$ depends on the preferred charts at $x$ and at $f(x)$, but only through composition with the invertible derivatives of chart transitions. A diffeomorphism is a smooth bijection with smooth inverse ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/Diffeomorph · Diffeomorph⟧, and a partial diffeomorphism is a smooth bijection between open subsets with smooth inverse ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/LocalDiffeomorph · PartialDiffeomorph⟧. The inverse function theorem between manifolds is the companion project's ⟦Milnor/Chapter1/InverseFunction · isLocalDiffeomorphAt_of_isInvertible_mfderiv⟧.

**Slice submanifolds.** From Theorem 3.1 on, the manifolds of the formal text are, with few exceptions, subsets of a Euclidean space, as Milnor's are from §6 on, and the companion project describes such a subset by slice charts. Let $S$ be a subset of a smooth manifold $M$ (here $M$ is always a normed space $V$ with its standard model) and let $Z$, $W$ be real normed spaces. A **slice chart** for $S$ is a partial diffeomorphism $\varphi$ from an open subset of $M$ onto an open subset of $Z \times W$ such that, on its domain, $p \in S$ if and only if the $W$-component of $\varphi(p)$ vanishes; it comes with a base point of $S$ in its domain ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · SliceChart⟧. $S$ is a **slice submanifold** modelled on $Z$, with complement $W$, if every point of $S$ lies in the domain of some slice chart ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · IsSliceSubmanifold⟧. Such an $S$ is a smooth manifold modelled on $Z$. Its charts are the restrictions to $S$ of the maps $p \mapsto \varphi(p)_Z$, the $Z$-component of $\varphi(p)$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · SliceChart.toChart⟧, and its preferred chart at a point is the one induced by a slice chart chosen at that point by the axiom of choice ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · IsSliceSubmanifold.chartedSpace⟧. The transitions are smooth ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · IsSliceSubmanifold.isManifold⟧, the inclusion $\iota : S \to M$ is smooth ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · IsSliceSubmanifold.contMDiff_val⟧ and, for $M$ finite-dimensional and without boundary, has injective derivative ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/LevelSet · injective_mfderiv_val⟧, and a smooth map into $M$ with values in $S$ is smooth as a map into $S$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Corestrict · IsSliceSubmanifold.contMDiff_codRestrict⟧. Since one subset may carry several such structures, the formal statements say which one they use, "the manifold structure given by the proof $h_N$ that $N$ is a slice submanifold"; this book suppresses that and writes simply $N$.

Throughout the book, **Milnor's "manifold $M \subseteq \mathbb R^K$ of dimension $n$"** is a set $N \subseteq V$ together with a proof $h_N$ that it is a slice submanifold of $V$ modelled on a space $Z$ with complement $W$. Here $V$, $Z$, $W$ are finite-dimensional wherever dimensions are counted, $\dim Z = n$, and $V$ carries an inner product as soon as lengths or angles enter. Such an $N$ is Hausdorff and second countable, as a subspace of $V$. It need not be closed in $V$: an open subset of $V$ is a slice submanifold. Compactness, closedness, connectedness and nonemptiness are separate hypotheses, stated where used. A function on $N$ is usually the restriction $f = F|N = F \circ \iota$ of a smooth $F : V \to \mathbb R$. For compact nonempty $N \subseteq \mathbb R^{K+1}$ every smooth function on $N$ is such a restriction ⟦Chapter3/EulerChar · exists_contDiff_comp_val_eq⟧; for noncompact $N$ the theorems assume it. Other slice submanifolds come from the companion project: the level set of a regular value ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/LevelSet · isSliceSubmanifold_preimage⟧, the unit sphere $S^n = \{x \in \mathbb R^{n+1} : \langle x, x\rangle = 1\}$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter5/HairyBall · Sphere.Sph⟧, and the tangent bundle $TN = \{(x, v) : x \in N,\ v \in T_xN\}$, a slice submanifold of $V \times V$ modelled on $Z \times Z$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter8/TangentBundle · Problems.tangentBundleSet⟧ ⟦Milnor/Chapter8/TangentBundle · Problems.isSliceSubmanifold_tangentBundle⟧. If $0$ is a regular value of a smooth $g$, the region $\{g \ge 0\}$ is a manifold with boundary $g^{-1}(0)$ in the companion project's sense ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/WithBoundary · isHalfSliceSubmanifold_of_regularValue⟧; applied to $g = a - f$, this is Milnor's remark on p. 4 that $M^a$ is a manifold with boundary when $a$ is a regular value, and it is the only place where a boundary occurs.

**Results imported from the companion project.** Critical points and regular values in its sense, where $x$ is critical when $df_x$ is not surjective ⟦Milnor/Chapter1/RegularValue · IsCriticalPoint⟧ ⟦Milnor/Chapter1/RegularValue · IsRegularValue⟧; Sard's theorem ⟦Milnor/Chapter3/Sard · sard⟧; Hadamard's lemma ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/Hadamard · exists_hadamard⟧; and, for a compact nonempty slice submanifold $N \subseteq \mathbb R^{K+1}$: the slice chart chosen at each point and its parametrization ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/Tubular1 · Tub.σ⟧ ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/Tubular1 · Tub.φ⟧, the tangent space as a subspace of $\mathbb R^{K+1}$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/ChartIndex · Tub.tanN⟧, the $\varepsilon$-neighbourhood theorem ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/Tubular3 · Tub.tubular⟧ with its radius, its nearest-point retraction and its neighbourhood $N_\varepsilon$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/Tubular4 · Tub.tubε⟧ ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/Tubular4 · Tub.tubr⟧ ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/Tubular4 · Tub.Nε⟧, the index of an isolated zero of a tangent vector field ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/PoincareHopf · Tub.indexN⟧, the Poincaré–Hopf theorem for $K \ge 1$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/PoincareHopf · Tub.poincare_hopf⟧, and the index of a non-degenerate zero as the sign of a determinant ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/ChartIndex · Tub.indexN_eq_sgn_det⟧. Part III uses the last three to identify $\sum_\lambda (-1)^\lambda C_\lambda$ with the Euler number.

**Conventions of the formal language.** Four features recur, as in the companion volume.

- *Every function is total.* Where mathematics leaves a value undefined, the formal definition picks one, and this book says which. A derivative that does not exist is $0$. The dimension of an infinite-dimensional space is $0$ ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/Dimension/Finrank · Module.finrank⟧, and so is the number of elements of an infinite set ⟦Mathlib/Data/Set/Card · Set.ncard⟧ and a finite sum over an infinite support ⟦Mathlib/Algebra/BigOperators/Finprod · finsum⟧. The index of a quadratic form on an infinite-dimensional space is $0$ (§0.2). The tangent projection, the tangent space and the curvature at a point of $V$ off $N$ are $0$ (§0.3). $\exp_q(v)$ is $q$ when there is no geodesic (§0.9), and the flow of a field without global integral curves is the identity (§0.10). Every theorem is stated where these values play no role, and the parts say so when a hypothesis is there only to exclude them.
- *Definitions by choice.* The preferred charts of a submanifold, the geodesic that defines $\exp$ and the integral curve that defines a flow are chosen by the axiom of choice; a theorem then shows that what matters does not depend on the choice.
- *Subsets as spaces.* A subset $S$ of a space is also a space, whose points are points of the ambient space together with a proof that they lie in $S$. A sublevel set $M^a$ is first a subset of $M$ and then a space, and "$M^a$ inside $M^b$" is the preimage of $M^a$ under the inclusion of $M^b$, a subset of the space $M^b$ (§0.4).
- *Universes.* Spaces built by pushouts live in a category of topological spaces of a fixed universe, a size bound on types, and a space compared with one of them must lie in the same universe; Betti numbers over a field need the field and the space in the same universe. The condition has no mathematical content.

### 0.2 Critical points, the Hessian, nullity and index, Morse functions

Milnor's definitions (pp. 4–5), for a smooth $f : M \to \mathbb R$: $p$ is critical when $f_* : TM_p \to T\mathbb R_{f(p)}$ is zero; at a critical point the Hessian $f_{**}$ is the bilinear form $f_{**}(v, w) = \tilde v_p(\tilde w(f))$, where $\tilde v$, $\tilde w$ are vector fields extending $v$, $w$; its matrix in local coordinates is $(\partial^2 f/\partial x^i\partial x^j(p))$, and the critical point is non-degenerate when this matrix is non-singular; the index of a bilinear form is the largest dimension of a subspace on which it is negative definite, and its nullity is the dimension of its null space. The formal proof defines everything first on a normed space, which is Milnor's situation after a choice of coordinates, and then on a manifold by reading the function in the preferred chart.

**On a normed space.** Let $E$ be a real normed space and $f : E \to \mathbb R$.

- $p$ is a **critical point** of $f$ if $f$ is differentiable at $p$ with derivative $0$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · IsCriticalPoint⟧. A point where $f$ is not differentiable is not critical in this sense, unlike in the manifold version below; for smooth $f$ the difference is empty.
- The **Hessian** of $f$ at $p$ is the second derivative $d^2f_p = D(Df)(p)$, the continuous bilinear form $d^2f_p(v, w) = D\big(x \mapsto Df(x)w\big)(p)\,v$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · hessian⟧. It is defined at every point, and Milnor's $\tilde v_p(\tilde w(f))$ is exactly this when $\tilde v$, $\tilde w$ are the constant extensions of $v$, $w$. It is symmetric wherever $f$ is $C^2$, critical or not ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · hessian_symm⟧, and depends only on the germ of $f$ at $p$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · hessian_congr⟧. Milnor's proof of the symmetry, $\tilde v_p(\tilde w(f)) - \tilde w_p(\tilde v(f)) = [\tilde v, \tilde w]_p(f) = 0$, uses that $p$ is critical; here the symmetry is that of second derivatives, and criticality is needed only for the independence of coordinates.
- For a continuous bilinear form $H$ on $E$: the **null space** of $H$ is $\{v : H(v, w) = 0 \text{ for all } w\}$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · nullSpace⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · mem_nullSpace⟧, and the **nullity** is its dimension ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · nullity⟧, by the convention above $0$ if it is infinite-dimensional. $H$ is **non-degenerate** if its null space is $0$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · IsNondegenerate⟧, which for finite-dimensional $E$ is nullity $0$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · isNondegenerate_iff_nullity_eq_zero⟧. The **index** of $H$ is Mathlib's negative index of inertia of the quadratic form $v \mapsto H(v, v)$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · quadForm⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · index⟧.
- A **non-degenerate critical point** is a critical point at which $d^2f_p$ is non-degenerate ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · IsNondegenerateCriticalPoint⟧.

*Why the index is Mathlib's `sigNeg`.* For a quadratic form $Q$ on a real vector space of dimension $r$ (so $r = 0$ for an infinite-dimensional space, by the convention of §0.1), Mathlib defines $\operatorname{sigPos}(Q)$ as the largest $k \le r$ such that $Q$ is positive definite on some subspace of dimension $k$, and $\operatorname{sigNeg}(Q) = \operatorname{sigPos}(-Q)$ ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/QuadraticForm/Signature · sigPos⟧ ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/QuadraticForm/Signature · sigNeg⟧; its defining property is that it is the greatest dimension of a negative definite subspace ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/QuadraticForm/Signature · sigNeg_isGreatest⟧. For finite-dimensional $E$ this is literally Milnor's index: $\operatorname{index}(H)$ is the largest dimension of a subspace $W$ with $H(v, v) < 0$ for every $0 \ne v \in W$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · index_isGreatest⟧. Taking Mathlib's notion brings Sylvester's law of inertia with it. The index is invariant under a linear change of variables ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · index_bilinearComp⟧, and for a diagonal form $\sum_i s_i y_i^2$ it is the number of negative $s_i$, which is how the $\lambda$ of the Morse lemma is identified with the index (Part I). The price is that the definition is useless in infinite dimension, where the rank is $0$ and so is the index. The path space of §§13–15 therefore needs a different index, defined in §0.9.

*Matrices and changes of coordinates.* For $f$ of class $C^2$ at $p \in \mathbb R^n$, non-degeneracy of $d^2f_p$ is Milnor's condition that the matrix $(\partial^2 f/\partial x^i\partial x^j(p))$ be non-singular ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · isNondegenerate_hessian_iff_det_ne_zero⟧. The second-order chain rule
$$d^2(f\circ\psi)_q(v, w) = d^2f_{\psi(q)}(d\psi_q v, d\psi_q w) + df_{\psi(q)}\big(d^2\psi_q(v, w)\big)$$
holds for $\psi$ of class $C^2$ at $q$ and $f$ of class $C^2$ at $\psi(q)$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · hessian_comp_apply⟧. At a critical point of $f$ the last term vanishes, so there the Hessian transforms as a bilinear form ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · hessian_comp⟧, and non-degeneracy and index are unchanged when $d\psi_q$ is a linear isomorphism ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · isNondegenerate_hessian_comp_iff⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · index_hessian_comp⟧; so is the nullity ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · nullity_bilinearComp⟧. This is Milnor's remark (p. 4) that non-degeneracy "can be checked directly" not to depend on the coordinates.

**On a manifold.** Let $M$ be a charted space modelled on a real normed space $E$, with the boundaryless model $\mathcal I(\mathbb R, E)$. The definitions need nothing more; most theorems about them add that $M$ is a smooth manifold. The model space is an explicit argument of every definition, because a charted space does not determine its model. Let $f : M \to \mathbb R$ and $p \in M$.

- The **local representative** of $f$ at $p$ is $f \circ \varphi_p^{-1}$, a function on $E$ that is meaningful near $\varphi_p(p)$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · localRep⟧. Its derivative at $\varphi_p(p)$ is $df_p$, with no hypothesis on $f$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · mfderiv_eq_fderiv_localRep⟧.
- $p$ is a **critical point** of $f$ if $df_p = 0$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsCriticalPointM⟧. Since the formal $df_p$ is $0$ where $f$ is not differentiable, such points count as critical; Milnor's $f$ is smooth, so this never matters. The definition agrees with the companion project's, where $p$ is critical when $df_p$ is not surjective, because a linear functional is surjective exactly when it is nonzero ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · isCriticalPointM_iff_milnor⟧.
- The **Hessian** $f_{**}$ at $p$ is the Hessian of the local representative at $\varphi_p(p)$, a continuous bilinear form on $T_pM = E$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · hessianM⟧. It is symmetric wherever $f$ is $C^2$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · hessianM_symm⟧, and on $M = E$ with its standard chart it is $d^2f_p$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · hessianM_model⟧.
- $p$ is a **non-degenerate critical point** if it is critical and $f_{**}$ is non-degenerate ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsNondegenerateCriticalPointM⟧. The **index** and the **nullity** of $f$ at $p$ are those of $f_{**}$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · indexM⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · nullityM⟧. The index is at most $\dim E$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · indexM_le_finrank⟧, and on $M = E$ it is the index of $d^2f_p$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · indexM_model⟧.
- $f$ is a **Morse function** if it is smooth and all its critical points are non-degenerate ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsMorseFunction⟧: Milnor's "differentiable function with no degenerate critical points" (§§3, 6). Compact sublevel sets and distinct critical values are not part of the definition; they are hypotheses of the theorems that need them.
- The **Morse number** $C_\lambda(f)$ is the number of critical points of $f$ of index $\lambda$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerChar · critCount⟧, counted by Mathlib's cardinality of sets, hence $0$ if there are infinitely many. A Morse function on a compact smooth manifold modelled on a finite-dimensional space has finitely many critical points ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · IsMorseFunction.finite_setOf_isCriticalPointM⟧, with no Hausdorff hypothesis, so on compact manifolds the convention plays no role.
- The **Euler number** of an abstract manifold $M$ is $\sum_{\lambda=0}^{\dim M}(-1)^\lambda C_\lambda(f)$ for a Morse function $f$ chosen once and for all, and $0$ if $M$ has none ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · eulerNumberM⟧. This is Milnor's formula $\chi(M) = C_0 - C_1 + \dots \pm C_n$ (p. 29) read as a definition; on a compact Hausdorff manifold every Morse function gives the same value ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · sum_critCount_eq_eulerNumberM⟧, by the Poincaré–Hopf theorem on an embedded copy. For a compact nonempty submanifold $N \subseteq \mathbb R^{K+1}$, the integer of the Poincaré–Hopf theorem is recorded as a property of an integer $\chi$: the indices of the zeros of every smooth tangent vector field on $N$ with finitely many zeros add up to $\chi$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerChar · IsEulerNumber⟧. Its identification with the homological Euler characteristic $\sum_i (-1)^i b_i$ (§0.6) is a theorem of Part IV.

*Why the Hessian is defined in one chart, and why that is enough.* Milnor defines $f_{**}$ without coordinates and then computes it in coordinates. The formal definition goes the other way, fixing the preferred chart, so that the well-definedness of $f_{**}$ *is* its independence of the chart, and that is a theorem. The coordinate systems allowed are the **smooth coordinate systems**: open partial homeomorphisms $y$ from $M$ to any normed space $E'$ (for instance $\mathbb R^n$, as in Milnor) that are smooth on their domain, with inverse smooth on their image ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord⟧. Such a $y$ is a partial diffeomorphism ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.toPartialDiffeomorph⟧, and for $E' = E$ these are exactly the charts of the maximal atlas ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · isSmoothCoord_iff_mem_maximalAtlas⟧. Let $M$ be a smooth manifold, $y$ a smooth coordinate system whose domain contains $p$, and $f$ of class $C^2$ at $p$; only $C^2$ is ever used.

- For $f$ differentiable at $p$, $p$ is critical iff $y(p)$ is a critical point of $f \circ y^{-1}$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.isCriticalPointM_iff⟧; for $y$ with values in $\mathbb R^n$, iff every $\partial f/\partial y^i(p)$ vanishes ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.isCriticalPointM_iff_forall_partial⟧.
- At a critical point, the Hessian of $f\circ y^{-1}$ at $y(p)$ is $f_{**}$ pulled back by $d(y^{-1})$: the matrix $(\partial^2 f/\partial y^i\partial y^j(p))$ represents $f_{**}$ in the basis $\partial/\partial y^i|_p$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.hessian_comp_symm⟧. Hence non-degeneracy, index and nullity can be computed in any coordinate system ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.isNondegenerate_iff⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.index_eq⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.nullity_eq⟧, and non-degeneracy is the non-vanishing of $\det(\partial^2 f/\partial y^i\partial y^j(p))$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.isNondegenerate_iff_det_ne_zero⟧.

Away from critical points $f_{**}$ depends on the chart, and no statement uses it there. The same invariance holds under diffeomorphisms: if $g : M \to M'$ is a diffeomorphism, the critical points, indices, nullities, the Morse property and the Morse numbers of $f$ and of $f \circ g^{-1}$ correspond ⟦Chapter4/WhitneyInvariance · indexM_comp_symm⟧ ⟦Chapter4/WhitneyInvariance · isMorseFunction_comp_symm_iff⟧ ⟦Chapter4/WhitneyInvariance · critCount_comp_symm⟧. Milnor uses this without saying so when he replaces a manifold by an embedded copy. With Mathlib's Whitney embedding, every compact Hausdorff manifold is diffeomorphic to a compact slice submanifold of some $\mathbb R^{K+1}$ ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · exists_diffeomorph_sliceSubmanifold⟧, and the two facts together carry the results proved for submanifolds to abstract compact manifolds (Part III).

**Functions on a submanifold.** For a slice submanifold $N \subseteq V$ modelled on $Z$ and $F : V \to \mathbb R$, the function of the book is $f = F|N$ on the manifold $N$, with model $Z$, and its critical points, Hessian and index are those just defined. They are computed in a slice chart $\sigma$ through Milnor's local parametrization $x(u) = \sigma^{-1}(u, 0)$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · sliceParam⟧. For $q$ in the domain of $\sigma$ and $F$ differentiable at $q$, $q$ is critical for $F|N$ iff the corresponding parameter is a critical point of $F \circ x$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · isCriticalPointM_comp_val_iff_sliceParam⟧, and for $F$ of class $C^2$ at a critical point the index is that of the Hessian of $F \circ x$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · index_hessian_comp_sliceParam⟧. Critical points can also be recognized without charts: $q$ is critical iff $DF(q)$ vanishes on $T_qN$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · isCriticalPointM_comp_val_iff_forall_tangent⟧, iff the ambient gradient $\nabla F(q)$ is normal to $N$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · isCriticalPointM_comp_val_iff_gradient_mem_orthogonal⟧, which is how Milnor recognizes them on p. 35. The Hessian of $F|N$ is *not* the restriction of the ambient Hessian of $F$: in $F \circ x$ the second-order chain rule adds the term $DF(\partial^2 x/\partial u^i\partial u^j)$, which for $L_p = \|\cdot - p\|^2$ is the second fundamental form term of Milnor's formula on p. 36 ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · hessian_normSq_sub_comp⟧.

### 0.3 Submanifolds of a Euclidean space: tangent spaces, the tangent projection, the induced metric

From §6 on, and throughout §§8–19, the manifold of the formal text is a slice submanifold $N$ of a finite-dimensional real inner product space $V$, modelled on $Z$ with complement $W$, both finite-dimensional. Its Riemannian metric is induced from $V$: the inner product of two tangent vectors at $q$ is their inner product in $V$. In this setting Milnor's coordinate constructions of §§8–10 have global formulas, all derived from one object, the orthogonal projection onto the tangent space.

**Tangent spaces and the tangent projection.**

- The **tangent space** $T_qN \subseteq V$ at $q \in N$ is the range of the derivative of the inclusion $N \to V$ at $q$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · tangentSubspace⟧. It is the range of $dx$ for the parametrization $x$ of any slice chart around $q$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · tangentSubspace_eq_range_fderiv_sliceParam⟧, it is the companion project's tangent space when $N \subseteq \mathbb R^{K+1}$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · tangentSubspace_eq_tanN⟧, and its dimension is $\dim Z$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · finrank_tanAt⟧. For a point $x$ of $V$ the text also uses $T_xN$, set to $0$ off $N$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · tanAt⟧. Milnor's tangent spaces are abstract. Identifying them with subspaces of $V$ is what makes vector fields along curves, and the derivatives of §8, ordinary vector-valued functions.
- The **tangent projection** $P_q : V \to V$ is the orthogonal projection onto $T_qN$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · tanProj⟧. For the parametrization $x$ of a slice chart, $P_{x(u)} = A(A^{\mathsf T}A)^{-1}A^{\mathsf T}$ with $A = dx_u$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · gramProj⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · tanProj_eq_gramProj⟧, where $A^{\mathsf T}A$ is invertible because $A$ is injective ⟦Chapter6/Projection · gramL_bijective⟧. Hence **$q \mapsto P_q$ is a smooth map** from $N$ to the operators on $V$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · contMDiff_tanProj⟧. Milnor never needs this, because he works in coordinates; here every smoothness statement of §§8–10 is reduced to it. It is not automatic: the projection onto the range of $A$ depends smoothly on $A$ only while the rank does not drop. For integrands along paths it is convenient to have $P_x$ for every $x \in V$, defined to be $0$ off $N$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · prj⟧; a vector $v$ is tangent at $x \in N$ iff $P_xv = v$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · prj_eq_self_iff⟧.
- **Derivatives along $N$.** The slice chart chosen at $q$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · sliceAt⟧ defines the chart of $N$ at $q$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · chartAt_eq_sliceAt⟧ and the chart coordinates $v \mapsto (d\sigma_q v)_Z$ of an ambient vector ⟦Chapter6/Projection · tanCoord⟧. For a map $F : N \to E$ into a normed space, the derivative is then read on ambient vectors: $d^NF_q(v) = dF_q(\text{coordinates of } v)$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN⟧. Its values on vectors not tangent to $N$ depend on the chart and are never used. On tangent vectors, if $F = G|N$ near $q$ with $G : V \to E$ differentiable at $q$, then $d^NF_q(v) = DG(q)v$ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_eq_fderiv⟧, and all calculus along $N$ is reduced to ambient calculus this way.

**Connection, second fundamental form, curvature.** A vector field on $N$ is a map $X : N \to V$ with $X_q \in T_qN$ for every $q$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · IsTangentField⟧. The Levi-Civita connection of the induced metric is Milnor's $X \vdash Y$, with $(X \vdash Y)_q = P_q\big(d^NY_q(X_q)\big)$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · leviCivita⟧; Part V proves Milnor's rules and Lemma 8.6 for it. The bracket is $[X, Y]_q = d^NY_q(X_q) - d^NX_q(Y_q)$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · bracket⟧. The **second fundamental form** is $\mathrm{II}_q(v, w) = (d^NP_q\,v)\,w$, the derivative of the projection in the direction $v$, applied to $w$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · secondFundForm⟧; for tangent $v$, $w$ it is normal to $N$ and symmetric. Milnor's **curvature tensor** (p. 51),
$$R(X, Y)Z = -X \vdash (Y \vdash Z) + Y \vdash (X \vdash Z) + [X, Y] \vdash Z$$
⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvature⟧, is at each point $R_q(x, y) = D_yD_x - D_xD_y$ with $D_v = d^NP_q\,v$, that is, $R(x, y)z = \mathrm{II}(y, \mathrm{II}(x, z)) - \mathrm{II}(x, \mathrm{II}(y, z))$ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvOp⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvOp_apply⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvature_eq⟧. Milnor's sign is kept, so that the unit sphere has sectional curvature $+1$ ⟦Chapter6/SphereCurvature · sectionalCurvature_sph⟧. For paths, which lie in $N$ only for $t \in [0, 1]$, the curvature is used at every point of $V$, set to $0$ off $N$ ⟦Chapter8/SecondVariation · curvR⟧.

**Curves.** For a curve $c$ in $N$, a vector field along $c$ is a map $X : \mathbb R \to V$ with $X(t) \in T_{c(t)}N$ ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · IsFieldAlong⟧, and its **covariant derivative** is $DX/dt = P_{c(t)}\big(X'(t)\big)$ ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · covDeriv⟧, Milnor's $D/dt$ (p. 46) for the induced metric. For paths given as functions $\mathbb R \to V$ the same formula is written with the total projection, $DX/dt = P_{c(t)}X'(t)$ ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · covD⟧, and the **acceleration** of $c$ is $D/dt\,(dc/dt)$ ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · accel⟧. A vector field along $c$ is **parallel** if it is differentiable with $DX/dt = 0$ ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · IsParallel⟧; parallel transport is constructed in Part V ⟦Chapter6/Parallel · parallelTransport⟧.

**The induced metric.** Lengths and distances are those of Milnor's §10, computed with the norm of $V$.

- The **length** of $c$ on $[a, b]$ is $\int_a^b \|c'(t)\|\,dt$ ⟦Chapter7/Path · pathLength⟧.
- $c$ is **piecewise smooth** on $[a, b]$ if $a \le b$, $c$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, and there is a finite set $B$ of break points such that $c$ is smooth, with one-sided derivatives at the ends, on every closed subinterval $[s, t] \subseteq [a, b]$ whose interior contains no point of $B$ ⟦Chapter7/Path · IsPiecewiseSmooth⟧. For Milnor's subdivision $a = t_0 < \dots < t_k = b$ (p. 67) take $B = \{t_i\}$; formulating it with a set rather than an ordered list makes restriction and concatenation easy. Lower bounds for lengths are proved for the larger class of **admissible** paths, continuous on $[a, b]$, differentiable off a countable set and with integrable speed ⟦Chapter7/Path · IsAdmissible⟧; every piecewise smooth path is admissible ⟦Chapter7/Path · IsPiecewiseSmooth.isAdmissible⟧.
- The **distance** $\rho(p, q)$ is the infimum of the lengths of the piecewise smooth paths in $N$ from $p$ to $q$ ⟦Chapter7/Distance · pathLengths⟧ ⟦Chapter7/Distance · riemDist⟧. It lies in $[0, \infty]$ and is $\infty$ when there is no such path, for instance between two components of $N$, or when $q \notin N$. Milnor assumes $M$ connected (p. 55); the formal $\rho$ needs no such assumption and is finite on a connected $N$ ⟦Chapter7/Metric · riemDist_ne_top⟧. It is symmetric ⟦Chapter7/Distance · riemDist_comm⟧, satisfies the triangle inequality ⟦Chapter7/Distance · riemDist_triangle⟧ and separates points ⟦Chapter7/Distance · eq_of_riemDist_eq_zero⟧, so it makes $N$ an extended metric space whose topology is, by construction, that of $N$ as a subspace of $V$ ⟦Chapter7/Metric · riemEMetricSpace⟧; for connected $N$ it is a metric space ⟦Chapter7/Metric · riemMetricSpace⟧ ⟦Chapter7/Metric · riemMetricSpace_topology⟧. These are not declared as *the* metric of $N$, which as a subspace already carries the distance of $V$; statements name them explicitly. The metric balls about a point form a basis of its neighbourhoods ⟦Chapter7/Distance · hasBasis_nhdsWithin_riemDist⟧, and $\rho$ is continuous ⟦Chapter7/Metric · continuous_riemDist⟧.
- $N$ is **complete** if every $\rho$-Cauchy sequence of points of $N$ converges for $\rho$ to a point of $N$ ⟦Chapter7/Metric · IsRiemComplete⟧; for connected $N$ this is completeness of the metric space $(N, \rho)$ in Mathlib's sense ⟦Chapter7/Metric · isRiemComplete_iff_completeSpace⟧. A set is **bounded** if it has finite $\rho$-diameter ⟦Chapter7/Metric · IsRiemBounded⟧. A closed submanifold of $V$ is complete ⟦Chapter7/Metric · isRiemComplete_of_isClosed⟧, and this is where the complete examples of the book, spheres and compact groups of matrices, come from.

### 0.4 Sublevel sets, strong deformation retracts, homotopy equivalences

**Sublevel sets.** For any function $F : T \to \mathbb R$ on any set $T$ and any $c \in \mathbb R$, $T^c = \{x : F(x) \le c\}$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · sublevel⟧, increasing in $c$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · sublevel_mono⟧. This is Milnor's $M^a$ (p. 12). For $f = F|N$ on a submanifold, $N^a$ is the sublevel set of $F \circ \iota$, a subset of $N$, and as a space it carries the subspace topology. When a statement concerns $M^a \subseteq M^b$, $M^a$ is viewed inside the space $M^b$ as the preimage of $M^a$ under the inclusion $M^b \to M$.

**Homotopies.** A homotopy from $f_0$ to $f_1 : X \to Y$ is a continuous $H : [0, 1] \times X \to Y$ with $H(0, \cdot) = f_0$ and $H(1, \cdot) = f_1$, time coming first ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Homotopy/Basic · ContinuousMap.Homotopy⟧, and $f_0$, $f_1$ are homotopic if one exists ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Homotopy/Basic · ContinuousMap.Homotopic⟧. Here $[0, 1]$ is Mathlib's unit interval, written $I$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/UnitInterval · unitInterval⟧. A homotopy *relative to* $S \subseteq X$ is one with $H(t, x) = f_0(x)$ for all $t$ and all $x \in S$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Homotopy/Basic · ContinuousMap.HomotopyRel⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Homotopy/Basic · ContinuousMap.HomotopicRel⟧, a special case of homotopies all of whose stages have a given property ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Homotopy/Basic · ContinuousMap.HomotopyWith⟧.

**Strong deformation retracts.** $A \subseteq X$ is a strong deformation retract of $X$ if there are a continuous $r : X \to X$ with values in $A$ and a homotopy from the identity of $X$ to $r$ relative to $A$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsStrongDeformationRetract⟧. In Milnor's notation (p. 13) the homotopy is $(t, x) \mapsto r_t(x)$ with $r_0 = \mathrm{id}$, $r_1 = r$, $r_1(X) \subseteq A$ and every $r_t$ the identity on $A$; $r$ is then a retraction onto $A$. Milnor says "deformation retract", but his deformations fix $A$ at all times, and the formal definition records this because the homology of pairs in §5 needs it: to compare $H_*(M^b, A)$ with $H_*(M^a, A)$ for $A \subseteq M^a$, the deformation must be a homotopy of pairs, keeping $A$ in place (Part IV). The inclusion of a strong deformation retract is a homotopy equivalence ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsStrongDeformationRetract.exists_homotopyEquiv⟧; that statement is made for subsets $A \subseteq B$ of a space, with $A$ a strong deformation retract of the space $B$, which is the form in which sublevel sets occur.

**Homotopy equivalences.** Mathlib's homotopy equivalence between $X$ and $Y$ is a structure: a pair of continuous maps $f : X \to Y$, $g : Y \to X$ with $g\circ f$ and $f \circ g$ homotopic to the identities ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Homotopy/Equiv · ContinuousMap.HomotopyEquiv⟧. "$X$ has the homotopy type of $Y$" is the existence of one. The project also uses the corresponding property of a given map: $f$ is a homotopy equivalence if it has a homotopy inverse ⟦Appendix/Telescope · IsHomotopyEquiv⟧; the two notions correspond ⟦Appendix/Telescope · IsHomotopyEquiv.of_homotopyEquiv⟧ ⟦Appendix/Telescope · IsHomotopyEquiv.exists_homotopyEquiv⟧. Milnor's Theorem 3.1 states that $M^a$ is diffeomorphic to $M^b$ and a deformation retract of it, so that the inclusion is a homotopy equivalence; the formal theorem has the three conclusions separately: a diffeomorphism of the whole manifold carrying $M^a$ onto $M^b$, $M^a$ a strong deformation retract of $M^b$, and the inclusion $M^a \to M^b$ a homotopy equivalence ⟦Chapter2/Regular · theorem_3_1⟧ ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · theorem_3_1_submanifold⟧.

**Telescopes and homotopy direct limits** (Milnor, Appendix, p. 149). For an increasing sequence $X_0 \subseteq X_1 \subseteq \cdots$ of subsets of $X$, the telescope is $X_\Sigma = \bigcup_n X_n \times [n, n+1] \subseteq X \times \mathbb R$, with the subspace topology ⟦Appendix/Telescope · telescope⟧, and $X$ is the **homotopy direct limit** of the $X_n$ if the projection $X_\Sigma \to X$ ⟦Appendix/Telescope · telescopeProj⟧ is a homotopy equivalence ⟦Appendix/Telescope · IsHomotopyDirectLimit⟧. If $X^{a_n} = \{E \le a_n\}$ for a continuous $E : X \to \mathbb R$ and $a_0 \le a_1 \le \cdots \to \infty$, then $X$ is the homotopy direct limit of the $X^{a_n}$, with no paracompactness hypothesis ⟦Appendix/Examples · isHomotopyDirectLimit_sublevel⟧. This is how the path space of §17 is exhausted by its sublevel sets (Parts IX and XI), and Milnor's Theorem A is stated in these terms ⟦Appendix/TheoremA · theoremA⟧.

### 0.5 Cell attachments and cell complexes

**The cell.** Mathlib's disk $\mathbb D^n$ is the closed unit ball of $\mathbb R^n$ and $\partial \mathbb D^n$ its unit sphere, both as objects of the category of topological spaces, with the inclusion $\partial\mathbb D^n \to \mathbb D^n$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Category/TopCat/Sphere · TopCat.disk⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Category/TopCat/Sphere · TopCat.diskBoundary⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Category/TopCat/Sphere · TopCat.diskBoundaryInclusion⟧. These are the basic cells of Mathlib's CW complexes. For $n = 0$ the sphere is empty and the disk is a point, which is Milnor's convention for $0$-cells.

**Attaching a cell.** For a space $X$ and a continuous $\varphi : \partial\mathbb D^n \to X$, the space $X \cup_\varphi e^n$ is the pushout, in the category of topological spaces, of $\varphi$ and the inclusion $\partial\mathbb D^n \to \mathbb D^n$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · AttachCell⟧: the quotient of $X \sqcup \mathbb D^n$ by $u \sim \varphi(u)$ for $u \in \partial\mathbb D^n$, with the quotient topology. It comes with the inclusion of $X$ and the characteristic map of the cell ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · cellInl⟧ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · cellInr⟧. This is Milnor's definition (pp. 2–3). Working with the categorical pushout gives the universal property for free: a map out of $X \cup_\varphi e^n$ is a pair of maps on $X$ and on $\mathbb D^n$ that agree along $\varphi$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · cellDesc⟧. A homotopy out of it is given in the same way ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · cellHomotopy⟧, which needs one fact more than Milnor states: the product of the quotient map $X \sqcup \mathbb D^n \to X \cup_\varphi e^n$ with $[0, 1]$ is again a quotient map, because $[0, 1]$ is locally compact (J. H. C. Whitehead's lemma, here the exponential law). Lemmas 3.6 and 3.7, that homotopic attaching maps give homotopy equivalent spaces and that a homotopy equivalence extends over an attached cell, are proved for this construction ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · lemma_3_6⟧ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · lemma_3_7⟧.

**Cells inside a space.** What the proof of Theorem 3.2 produces is not a pushout but a subspace $M^{c-\varepsilon} \cup e^\lambda$ of $M$: a map $\chi$ from the closed unit ball of $\mathbb R^\lambda$ to $M$, continuous and injective on the ball, with $\chi(z) \in M^{c-\varepsilon}$ exactly when $\|z\| = 1$, such that $M^{c-\varepsilon} \cup \chi(\text{ball})$ is a strong deformation retract of $M^{c+\varepsilon}$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAttachSubmanifold · theorem_3_2_submanifold⟧. The two forms are identified by Lemma D of the exposition. Let a Hausdorff space $W$ be the union of a closed subset $A$ and the image of a compact space $D$ under a continuous injective map $\chi$, let $i : S \to D$ be such that $\chi(d) \in A$ only for $d$ in the image of $i$, and let $\varphi : S \to A$ satisfy $\varphi(s) = \chi(i(s))$ for every $s \in S$. Then $W$ is the pushout of $A \xleftarrow{\varphi} S \xrightarrow{i} D$ ⟦Chapter2/CellAdjunction · isPushout_of_cell⟧. This is Milnor's sentence "so that $e^\lambda$ is attached to $M^{c-\varepsilon}$ as required" (p. 16). With it, Theorem 3.2 gives a homotopy equivalence from $N^{c+\varepsilon}$ to the pushout along an injective attaching map ⟦Chapter2/CellAttachSubmanifold · theorem_3_2_attach_submanifold⟧. The homology computations of Part IV use the subspace form, the homotopy computations of Part XII the pushout form.

**Finite cell complexes.** $K$ is an **iterated adjunction space** with cells of dimensions $(n_1, \dots, n_k)$ if it is obtained from the empty space by attaching cells of these dimensions one at a time, in this order, each along an arbitrary continuous map of its boundary sphere into the space built so far ⟦Chapter2/CWType · IsCellAttachment⟧. A space $X$ **has the homotopy type of a finite cell complex with cells $(n_1, \dots, n_k)$** if it is homotopy equivalent to such a $K$, in the same universe ⟦Chapter2/CWType · IsFiniteCellComplexType⟧; the property is invariant under homotopy equivalence ⟦Chapter2/CWType · IsFiniteCellComplexType.of_homotopyEquiv⟧. Milnor's Theorem 3.5 concludes that $M$ has the homotopy type of a CW complex with one $\lambda$-cell for each critical point of index $\lambda$. The formal Theorem 3.5 concludes this property for the list of the indices of the critical points in order of critical value, hence with exactly $C_\lambda$ cells of dimension $\lambda$ ⟦Chapter2/CWType · theorem_3_5_count'⟧. The difference is twofold. The attaching maps are arbitrary continuous maps, not cellular ones: Milnor makes them cellular with the cellular approximation theorem, which Mathlib does not have, and then applies Lemma 3.6; the formal induction uses only Lemma 3.7. And the cells are attached in order of critical value, not of dimension. The number of cells in each dimension is what Milnor's theorem is used for (the Morse inequalities, the Euler characteristic, the homotopy groups), and that is exactly what the formal statement records.

**Relative cell complexes.** $K$ is obtained from $L$ by attaching cells $(n_1, \dots, n_k)$ along $j : L \to K$ if it is reached from $L$ by attaching these cells one at a time, $j$ being the composite of the inclusions $K' \to K' \cup_\varphi e^n$ ⟦Appendix/MonotoneUnion · IsRelCellAttachment⟧. A continuous $\iota : A \to X$ is **up to homotopy the inclusion of $A$ with cells attached** if there are such a $j : A \to K$ and a homotopy equivalence $e : X \to K$ with $e \circ \iota$ homotopic to $j$ ⟦Chapter10/RelativeCells · IsRelCellComplexType⟧. This is the form in which one step $M^{a_n} \subseteq M^{a_{n+1}}$ of the relative Theorem 3.5, used for Theorem 17.3, is recorded, and it is transported through homotopy equivalences by an iterated, relative Lemma 3.7 ⟦Chapter10/RelativeCells · IsRelCellAttachment.transport⟧.

**Countable cell complexes.** A **cell tower** with cells $c_0, c_1, \dots$, each a finite list of dimensions, is a sequence of spaces and maps $K_0 \to K_1 \to \cdots$ in which $K_0$ is an iterated adjunction space with cells $c_0$ and $K_{n+1}$ is obtained from $K_n$ by attaching the cells $c_{n+1}$, the map being the inclusion ⟦Appendix/MonotoneUnion · CellTower⟧; each stage is a finite cell complex with the cells of the stages up to it ⟦Appendix/MonotoneUnion · CellTower.isCellAttachment⟧. The **mapping telescope** of a sequence of spaces $F_0 \to F_1 \to \cdots$ with maps $c_n$ is the quotient of $\bigsqcup_n F_n \times [0, 1]$ by $(k, 1)_n \sim (c_n(k), 0)_{n+1}$ ⟦Appendix/MappingTelescope · MapTelRel⟧ ⟦Appendix/MappingTelescope · MappingTelescope⟧, the point $(k, t)_n$ lying at level $n + t$ ⟦Appendix/MappingTelescope · MappingTelescope.level⟧. A space **has the homotopy type of a countable cell complex with cells $c_0, c_1, \dots$** if it is homotopy equivalent to the mapping telescope of a cell tower with these cells ⟦Appendix/MonotoneUnion · IsCountableCellComplexType⟧. Milnor's Theorem 17.3 speaks of a CW complex $K = \bigcup K_i$ with the weak topology. Two facts make the mapping telescope the right formal model. The stages of a formal tower are pushouts connected by maps, not subspaces of one space, so there is no union to take. And the union with the weak topology is homotopy equivalent to the mapping telescope by Example 2 of the Appendix, which rests on Whitehead's theorem and is not formalized, whereas the telescope is exactly what the Appendix handles directly. The topological half of Theorem 17.3 is then: if $X$ is exhausted by the sublevel sets $X^{a_n}$ of a continuous function, $a_n \to \infty$, and homotopy equivalences $X^{a_n} \to K_n$ to the stages of a tower extend one another, $X$ has the homotopy type of the countable complex ⟦Appendix/MonotoneUnion · isCountableCellComplexType_of_sublevel⟧.

### 0.6 Singular chains, homology, relative homology, Betti numbers

Mathlib has the singular chain complex and the singular homology of a space, with coefficients in an object $R$ of a category with coproducts ⟦Mathlib/AlgebraicTopology/SingularHomology/Basic · AlgebraicTopology.singularChainComplexFunctor⟧ ⟦Mathlib/AlgebraicTopology/SingularHomology/Basic · AlgebraicTopology.singularHomologyFunctor⟧: its $n$-chains are a coproduct of copies of $R$ indexed by the singular $n$-simplices, the continuous maps from the standard simplex. It has no relative homology, no long exact sequence of a pair and no excision. The project supplies them.

**Pairs.** A pair of spaces is an embedding $A \to X$ in the category of topological spaces, the morphisms of pairs being the commutative squares ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Category/TopPair · TopPair⟧. For subsets $A \subseteq B$ of a space $E$, the pair $(B, A)$ is the inclusion of the subspace $A$ in the subspace $B$ ⟦Homology/Relative · subsetPair⟧; this is the form of Milnor's $(M^b, M^a)$.

**Relative chains and relative homology.** Let the coefficients $R$ lie in an abelian category $\mathcal C$ with coproducts. The singular chain functor ⟦Homology/Relative · singularChains⟧ sends the inclusion of a pair to a monomorphism $C(A) \to C(X)$, and the relative chain complex $C(X, A)$ is defined as its cokernel. So $0 \to C(A) \to C(X) \to C(X, A) \to 0$ is a short exact sequence of chain complexes, natural in the pair ⟦Homology/Relative · relShortComplex⟧ ⟦Homology/Relative · relShortComplex_shortExact⟧ ⟦Homology/Relative · relChains⟧. **Relative homology** $H_n(X, A; R)$ is the $n$-th homology of $C(X, A)$, a functor on pairs ⟦Homology/Relative · relHomology⟧, and the connecting morphism $H_{n+1}(X, A) \to H_n(A)$ is Mathlib's connecting morphism of that short exact sequence ⟦Homology/Relative · relδ⟧. The long exact sequence of the pair is exact at every place ⟦Homology/Relative · relHomology_exact₁⟧ ⟦Homology/Relative · relHomology_exact₂⟧ ⟦Homology/Relative · relHomology_exact₃⟧, and $H_n(X) \cong H_n(X, \emptyset)$ naturally ⟦Homology/Relative · relHomologyInclIso⟧. Defining $C(X, A)$ as a cokernel in an abelian category, rather than as a quotient of free modules, is what makes the exact sequence and its naturality available at once from Mathlib's homological algebra.

**Concrete chains.** For subdivision and excision the textbook description is used: an $n$-chain is a finite formal combination $\sum r_i\sigma_i$ of continuous maps $\sigma_i : \Delta^n \to X$ from the standard simplex ⟦Homology/Simplex · Simplex⟧ ⟦Homology/Simplex · Chains⟧, with boundary $\partial\sigma = \sum_i (-1)^i\,\sigma\circ\delta^i$ ⟦Homology/Simplex · sbd⟧. These form a chain complex ⟦Homology/Simplex · singChains⟧ naturally isomorphic to Mathlib's ⟦Homology/Simplex · singChainsIso⟧, so that results proved with one description transfer to the other.

**The axioms.** With these definitions, singular homology is an instance of Mathlib's Eilenberg–Steenrod homology pretheory ⟦Homology/Pretheory · singularHomologyPretheory⟧; homotopic maps of pairs induce the same maps in relative homology ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · relHomology_map_eq_of_homotopy⟧; and excision holds ⟦Homology/Excision · excision⟧. A triple $Z \subseteq Y \subseteq X$ is a pair of composable embeddings ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple⟧, with its own connecting morphism and exact sequence ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.δ⟧. For subsets $A \subseteq B \subseteq X$ with $B$ a strong deformation retract of $X$, $H_*(B, A) \cong H_*(X, A)$ ⟦Homology/RelativeRetract · relHomologyIsoOfIsStrongDeformationRetract⟧.

**Field coefficients and Betti numbers.** Milnor's §5 works with homology over a field (p. 28). The formal text takes the coefficients in a field $\Bbbk$, with homology groups as $\Bbbk$-vector spaces: $H_i(B, A; \Bbbk)$ for subsets $A \subseteq B$ ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · subsetHomology⟧, and the absolute $H_i(X; \Bbbk)$, Mathlib's singular homology with coefficients $\Bbbk$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · spaceHomology⟧. The **Betti number** $b_i(X) = \dim_\Bbbk H_i(X; \Bbbk)$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · bettiNumber⟧ is Milnor's $R_i(X)$. By the convention on dimensions it is $0$ when $H_i(X; \Bbbk)$ is infinite-dimensional; for the spaces of the book finiteness is proved, not assumed (Part IV). Betti numbers are invariant under homeomorphisms ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · bettiNumber_eq_of_homeomorph⟧. The field and the space must lie in the same universe. The Euler characteristic $\sum_i (-1)^i b_i$ and the Morse inequalities are in Part IV ⟦Homology/MorseHomology · bettiNumber_le_critCount⟧ ⟦Homology/MorseHomology · sum_bettiNumber_eq_sum_critCount⟧.

### 0.7 Homotopy groups and induced maps

Milnor uses homotopy groups in §19 and throughout his Part IV (§§22–24). Mathlib defines them; the project adds the maps they induce and the exact sequence of a fibration.

- **Generalized loops.** For an index type $N$, $I^N$ is the cube of functions $N \to [0, 1]$, and its boundary is the set of points with some coordinate equal to $0$ or $1$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Homotopy/HomotopyGroup · Cube.boundary⟧. A generalized loop at $x \in X$ is a continuous $\gamma : I^N \to X$ equal to $x$ on the boundary ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Homotopy/HomotopyGroup · GenLoop⟧, and two generalized loops are equivalent if they are homotopic relative to the boundary ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Homotopy/HomotopyGroup · GenLoop.Homotopic⟧.
- **Homotopy groups.** $\pi_N(X, x)$ is the set of equivalence classes ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Homotopy/HomotopyGroup · HomotopyGroup⟧, and $\pi_n(X, x)$ is the case $N = \{0, \dots, n-1\}$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Homotopy/HomotopyGroup · HomotopyGroup.Pi⟧. For nonempty $N$ it is a group ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Homotopy/HomotopyGroup · HomotopyGroup.group⟧, abelian when $N$ has at least two elements ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Homotopy/HomotopyGroup · HomotopyGroup.commGroup⟧, and $\pi_1$ is the fundamental group ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Homotopy/HomotopyGroup · HomotopyGroup.pi1EquivFundamentalGroup⟧. A path is a continuous map from $[0, 1]$ with prescribed ends ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Path · Path⟧, and the loop space $\Omega X$ at $x$ is the space of paths from $x$ to $x$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Homotopy/HomotopyGroup · LoopSpace⟧, with the compact-open topology.
- **Induced maps.** A continuous $f : X \to Y$ carries a generalized loop $\gamma$ at $x$ to the generalized loop $f\circ\gamma$ at $f(x)$ ⟦Homotopy/Basic · genLoopMap⟧, and homotopies relative to the boundary to homotopies relative to the boundary, so it induces $f_* : \pi_N(X, x) \to \pi_N(Y, f(x))$ ⟦Homotopy/Basic · homotopyGroupMap⟧, functorially ⟦Homotopy/Basic · homotopyGroupMap_comp⟧ ⟦Homotopy/Basic · homotopyGroupMap_id⟧. It is defined as a map of sets, and most results about induced maps are stated as bijections or surjections of the underlying sets; for nonempty $N$ it is a group homomorphism ⟦Homotopy/ExactSequenceIso · homotopyGroupMap_mul⟧ ⟦Homotopy/ExactSequenceIso · homotopyGroupHom⟧, so a bijective $f_*$ is an isomorphism of groups. For finite $N$, a path $\omega$ from $y_0$ to $y_1$ gives a bijection $\pi_N(Y, y_0) \to \pi_N(Y, y_1)$ ⟦Homotopy/ChangeOfBase · changeBaseEquiv⟧, and a homotopy equivalence $e$ gives bijections $\pi_N(X, x) \to \pi_N(Y, e(x))$ ⟦Homotopy/ChangeOfBase · homotopyGroupEquivOfHomotopyEquiv⟧.
- **Compressions.** An embedding $j : Y \to X$ compresses maps from $P$ if every map $P \to X$ is homotopic, relative to the preimage of $j(Y)$, to a map into $j(Y)$ ⟦Homotopy/Basic · Compresses⟧. Compressing maps from the cube gives surjectivity on $\pi_N$, and compressing maps from $I \times I^N$ gives injectivity ⟦Homotopy/Basic · surjective_homotopyGroupMap_of_compresses⟧ ⟦Homotopy/Basic · injective_homotopyGroupMap_of_compresses⟧. This is how attaching a cell of dimension $\lambda$ is shown to induce bijections on $\pi_i$ for $i \le \lambda - 2$ ⟦Homotopy/CellAttach · bijective_homotopyGroupMap_cellInl⟧ (Part XII).
- **Loop spaces.** $\pi_{i+1}(\Omega X) \cong \pi_{i+2}(X)$ as groups ⟦Homotopy/LoopSpace · homotopyGroupPiLoopMulEquiv⟧, and in a path-connected space the space of paths from $p$ to $q$ is homotopy equivalent to the loop space at $p$ ⟦Homotopy/LoopSpace · pathHomotopyEquivLoop⟧. These pass between Milnor's path spaces $\Omega(M; p, q)$ and his loop spaces.
- **Fibrations.** In §§23–24 Milnor quotes the exact homotopy sequence of a fibre bundle. A map $p : E \to B$ is **locally trivial** with fibre $F$ if every point of $B$ has an open neighbourhood $U$ with a homeomorphism $p^{-1}(U) \cong U \times F$ whose first component is $p$ ⟦Homotopy/Fibration · IsLocallyTrivial⟧; this is a property of an arbitrary map, so that it applies directly to maps between concrete spaces such as a unitary group and a sphere, where Mathlib's fibre bundles are a structure on a total space built for the purpose. $p$ is a **Serre fibration** if it has the homotopy lifting property for cubes relative to the open box $I^n \times \{0\} \cup \partial I^n \times I$ ⟦Homotopy/Fibration · openBox⟧ ⟦Homotopy/Fibration · IsSerreFibration⟧; locally trivial maps, and in particular Mathlib's fibre bundles, are Serre fibrations ⟦Homotopy/HomotopyLifting · IsLocallyTrivial.isSerreFibration⟧ ⟦Homotopy/HomotopyLifting · isSerreFibration_proj⟧. For a continuous Serre fibration $p$ and $e_0 \in E$, with fibre $p^{-1}(p(e_0))$ ⟦Homotopy/ExactSequence · fiber⟧, the boundary map $\partial : \pi_{n+1}(B) \to \pi_n(\text{fibre})$ ⟦Homotopy/ExactSequence · boundaryMap⟧ makes the homotopy sequence exact at the fibre, the total space and the base ⟦Homotopy/ExactSequence · exact_fiber⟧ ⟦Homotopy/ExactSequence · exact_total⟧ ⟦Homotopy/ExactSequence · exact_base⟧, as pointed sets (Part XII).

### 0.8 Paths, energy, the path spaces $\Omega$ and $\Omega^*$, Milnor's metric

**Paths as functions on $\mathbb R$.** A path in the formal text is a function $c : \mathbb R \to V$ whose values matter only on $[0, 1]$, or on whatever interval is named. This is a deliberate choice. Derivatives at interior points are ordinary derivatives of functions on $\mathbb R$; one-sided derivatives at the ends and at break points are derivatives within half-lines; and paths can be added, rescaled, reparametrized and concatenated without subtypes. The cost is that two paths that agree on $[0, 1]$ are different functions. The book then speaks of paths that "agree on $[0, 1]$", and a few notions normalize a function by extending its restriction to $[0, 1]$ as a constant outside (§0.9).

- **The path space** $\Omega(N; p, q)$ (Milnor, p. 67) is the set of paths $c$ that are piecewise smooth on $[0, 1]$ in the sense of §0.3, with $c(t) \in N$ for $t \in [0, 1]$, $c(0) = p$ and $c(1) = q$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · pathSpace⟧.
- The **energy** of $c$ from $a$ to $b$ is $E_a^b(c) = \int_a^b \|c'(t)\|^2\,dt$, and Milnor's $E$ is $E_0^1$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · energy⟧; at the finitely many break points $c'$ takes a junk value, which does not affect the integral. Schwarz's inequality $L^2 \le (b - a)E$ holds for piecewise smooth $c$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · pathLength_sq_le_energy⟧, with equality exactly when the speed $\|c'(t)\|$ is the same at every point of $(a, b)$ where $c$ is differentiable ⟦Chapter8/Energy · pathLength_sq_eq_energy_iff⟧.
- The **jump** of a velocity at $t$ is $\Delta_t c' = c'(t^+) - c'(t^-)$, computed with one-sided derivatives ⟦Chapter8/Energy · jump⟧; this is Milnor's $\Delta_t$ (p. 71).
- **Milnor's "tangent space"** $T\Omega_c$ (p. 67) is the vector space of fields $X$, piecewise smooth on $[0, 1]$, tangent along $c$ ($P_{c(t)}X(t) = X(t)$ for $t \in [0, 1]$), with $X(0) = X(1) = 0$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · pathTangent⟧. As in Milnor's §§11–15, $\Omega$ carries no manifold structure: $T\Omega_c$ is a definition, related to $\Omega$ only through variations.
- A **variation** with fixed end points (Milnor, p. 68) has its parameters in an open set $U \ni 0$ of a normed space $P$; $P = \mathbb R$ gives Milnor's $(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$, and $P = \mathbb R^2$ the two-parameter variations of §13. It is a map $\alpha$ from $P$ to paths with $\alpha(u)(t) \in N$ for $u \in U$ and $t \in [0, 1]$, with end points independent of $u$, and with $(u, t) \mapsto \alpha(u)(t)$ smooth on $U \times [s, t]$ for every closed strip whose interior contains no point of a finite set $B$ of break points ⟦Chapter8/Energy · IsVariation⟧. It is a variation of the path $\alpha(0)$. Its **variation field** is $W_t = \partial\alpha/\partial u\,(0, t)$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · varField⟧, which lies in $T\Omega$, as Milnor says "clearly" (p. 68) ⟦Chapter8/Energy · IsVariation.varField_mem⟧. Conversely, a piecewise smooth family of tangent fields depending linearly on a parameter $u \in P$ is the derivative at $u = 0$ of a variation built with the exponential map (Lemma 11.A of the exposition) ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · exists_variation⟧; in particular every finite family of elements of $T\Omega_c$ is the family of variation fields of one variation. Derivatives "of $E$ on $\Omega$" are always derivatives of $u \mapsto E(\alpha(u))$, functions of finitely many real variables.

**Milnor's metric.** In §16 (p. 88) Milnor topologizes $\Omega$ by
$$d(\omega, \omega') = \max_{t} \rho\big(\omega(t), \omega'(t)\big) + \Big[\int_0^1 \Big(\frac{ds}{dt} - \frac{ds'}{dt}\Big)^2 dt\Big]^{1/2},$$
$s$ and $s'$ being the arc lengths.

- $\Omega(N; p, q)$ as a space is the type of its elements ⟦Chapter10/PathMetric · PathSp⟧. For two paths $\gamma$, $\eta$ the formal $d$ is built from the speed $ds/dt = \|\gamma'(t)\|$ ⟦Chapter10/PathMetric · PathSp.speed⟧, the uniform part $\sup_{t \in [0, 1]}\rho(\gamma(t), \eta(t)) \in [0, \infty]$ ⟦Chapter10/PathMetric · PathSp.supDist⟧, and the $L^2$ part $\big(\int_0^1 (\|\gamma'(t)\| - \|\eta'(t)\|)^2\,dt\big)^{1/2}$ ⟦Chapter10/PathMetric · PathSp.l2Dist⟧. Their sum is an extended **pseudometric** on $\Omega$ ⟦Chapter10/PathMetric · PathSp.instPseudoEMetricSpace⟧. It is only a pseudometric because paths are functions on $\mathbb R$: two paths that agree on $[0, 1]$ are at distance $0$. So the formal $\Omega$ is not Hausdorff. This matters in the proof of Theorem 17.1, where Example 1 of the Appendix (for paracompact Hausdorff spaces) is applied in $\Omega^*$ and pulled back to $\Omega$ (Part IX). The triangle inequality for the $L^2$ part is Minkowski's inequality, proved in the project for piecewise continuous integrands.
- The point of the $L^2$ term is that it makes the energy continuous: $\big|\sqrt{E_a^b(\gamma)} - \sqrt{E_a^b(\eta)}\big|$ is at most the $L^2$ part of $d(\gamma, \eta)$ for $0 \le a \le b \le 1$ ⟦Chapter10/PathMetric · PathSp.abs_sqrt_energy_sub_le⟧. So $E$ is continuous on $\Omega$ ⟦Chapter10/PathMetric · PathSp.continuous_energy⟧ ⟦Chapter10/FundamentalTheorem · PathSp.energyC⟧, $\Omega^c = E^{-1}[0, c]$ is closed ⟦Chapter10/PathMetric · PathSp.isClosed_sublevel⟧, and $\operatorname{Int}\Omega^c = E^{-1}[0, c)$ is open ⟦Chapter10/PathMetric · PathSp.isOpen_sublevel_lt⟧.

**The full path space.** Milnor's $\Omega^* = \Omega^*(M; p, q)$ (p. 93) is the space of all continuous paths from $p$ to $q$ with the compact-open topology. Formally it is the set of continuous maps $\gamma : [0, 1] \to V$ with values in $N$, $\gamma(0) = p$ and $\gamma(1) = q$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathSpace · pathCSet⟧ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathSpace · PathC⟧, as a subspace of the space of continuous maps $[0, 1] \to V$ with the compact-open topology ⟦Mathlib/Topology/CompactOpen · ContinuousMap.compactOpen⟧, which, $[0, 1]$ being compact, is the topology of uniform convergence. Here paths are genuine maps on $[0, 1]$, so $\Omega^*$ is Hausdorff. Restricting a piecewise smooth path to $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathSpace · PathSp.toC⟧ gives the inclusion $i : \Omega \to \Omega^*$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathSpace · PathSp.toPathC⟧, which does not increase distances ⟦Chapter10/FullPathSpace · PathSp.edist_toPathC_le⟧ and is therefore continuous ⟦Chapter10/FullPathSpace · PathSp.continuous_toPathC⟧. Theorem 17.1 says that it is a homotopy equivalence ⟦Chapter10/FullPathTheorem · pathIncl⟧ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathTheorem · theorem_17_1⟧.

### 0.9 Geodesics, the exponential map, Jacobi fields, conjugate points, the index form $E_{**}$

Throughout this section $N \subseteq V$ is a slice submanifold of a finite-dimensional inner product space, with the induced metric, as in §0.3.

**Geodesics.**

- $\gamma : \mathbb R \to V$ is a **geodesic on an open set** $s \subseteq \mathbb R$, in practice an open interval, if $\gamma$ is smooth on $s$, $\gamma(s) \subseteq N$, and the acceleration $\gamma''(t)$ is normal to $T_{\gamma(t)}N$ for every $t \in s$ ⟦Chapter7/Geodesic · IsGeodesicOn⟧. Since $D/dt = P \circ d/dt$, this is Milnor's $D/dt\,(d\gamma/dt) = 0$ (p. 55) for the induced metric ⟦Chapter7/Geodesic · isGeodesicOn_univ_iff_covDeriv⟧: the motion of a particle constrained to $N$ and subject to no other force. Equivalently $\gamma'' = \mathrm{II}(\gamma', \gamma')$ ⟦Chapter7/Geodesic · IsGeodesicOn.deriv_deriv_eq⟧ ⟦Chapter7/Geodesic · isGeodesicOn_of_deriv_deriv_eq⟧, that is, $(\gamma, \gamma')$ is an integral curve of the geodesic spray $(x, v) \mapsto (v, \mathrm{II}_x(v, v))$ on $TN$ ⟦Chapter7/Geodesic · geodesicSpray⟧ ⟦Chapter7/Geodesic · isGeodesicOn_iff_hasDerivAt_geodesicSpray⟧. A geodesic on an open interval has constant speed ⟦Chapter7/Geodesic · IsGeodesicOn.norm_deriv_eq⟧, and two geodesics on an open interval with the same position and velocity at one time agree on it ⟦Chapter7/Geodesic · IsGeodesicOn.eqOn⟧. Milnor allows any interval, with one-sided derivatives at closed ends; the formal definition uses open sets, which avoids them.
- A path of $\Omega$ is an **unbroken geodesic** if it is smooth on the closed interval $[0, 1]$, with one-sided derivatives at the ends, and a geodesic on $(0, 1)$ ⟦Chapter8/Critical · IsUnbrokenGeodesic⟧. A path is a **critical path** of $E$ if, for every one-parameter variation of it, the derivative of $u \mapsto E(\alpha(u))$ at $0$ exists and is $0$ ⟦Chapter8/Critical · IsCriticalPath⟧ (Milnor, p. 69). Corollary 12.3 says that on $\Omega$ the two notions coincide ⟦Chapter8/Critical · corollary_12_3⟧.
- A **minimal geodesic** on $[a, b]$ is the restriction of a geodesic on an open set containing $[a, b]$ whose length is at most that of every piecewise smooth path in $N$ with the same ends ⟦Chapter7/HopfRinow · IsMinimalGeodesic⟧. A path of $\Omega$ whose length is at most that of every path of $\Omega$ is a **minimal path** ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · IsMinimalPath⟧.

**The exponential map.**

- $\gamma$ is a **geodesic from $q$ with initial velocity $v$** if for some $\delta > 0$ it is a geodesic on $(-\delta, 1 + \delta)$ with $\gamma(0) = q$ and $\gamma'(0) = v$ ⟦Chapter7/Exp · IsGeodesicFrom⟧. The **domain of the exponential map** is the set of pairs $(q, v) \in V \times V$ for which such a geodesic exists ⟦Chapter7/Exp · expDom⟧. For such a pair, $\exp_q(v) = \gamma(1)$ for a geodesic $\gamma$ chosen by the axiom of choice; otherwise $\exp_q(v) = q$ ⟦Chapter7/Exp · exp⟧. The value does not depend on the choice, by uniqueness of geodesics ⟦Chapter7/Exp · IsGeodesicFrom.exp_eq⟧, and $\gamma(t) = \exp_q(tv)$ ⟦Chapter7/Exp · IsGeodesicFrom.exp_smul⟧. Milnor (p. 57) asks for a geodesic on the closed interval $[0, 1]$; the formal domain asks for one on an open interval containing $[0, 1]$, which avoids one-sided derivatives in the definition. The two domains are the same. A geodesic on $[0, 1]$, that is, a curve smooth on $[0, 1]$ with values in $N$ and a geodesic on $(0, 1)$, coincides on $[0, 1]$ with the radial curve $t \mapsto \exp_{\gamma(0)}(t\gamma'(0))$, $\gamma'(0)$ the one-sided velocity, and that curve is a geodesic on an open interval containing $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter9/GeodesicVariation · exists_initial_ray⟧; a radial curve that is a geodesic on such an interval puts its initial data in the domain of $\exp$ ⟦Chapter11/ExpSmooth · mem_expDom_of_isGeodesicOn⟧. So $\exp_q(v)$ is defined in the formal sense exactly when it is defined in Milnor's.
- $\exp$ is defined and smooth on an open neighbourhood of the zero section of $TN$, in the sense that near each point it is the restriction of a smooth map on an open set of $V \times V$ ⟦Chapter7/Exp · exists_isOpen_exp⟧, and $d(\exp_q)_0$ is the inclusion $T_qN \to V$ ⟦Chapter7/Exp · hasFDerivAt_exp_zero⟧. In fact $\exp$ is smooth on its whole domain ⟦Chapter11/ExpSmooth · exists_contDiffOn_exp⟧: for $q \in N$ the domain of $\exp_q$ in $T_qN$ ⟦Chapter11/ExpSmooth · expDomAt⟧ is open ⟦Chapter11/ExpSmooth · isOpen_expDomAt⟧ and star-shaped about $0$ ⟦Chapter11/ExpSmooth · smul_mem_expDomAt⟧, and $\exp_q$ is smooth on it ⟦Chapter11/ExpSmooth · contDiffOn_expAt⟧. As a map into the manifold $N$, $\exp_q : T_qN \to N$ takes the junk value $q$ where $\exp_q(v) \notin N$ ⟦Chapter7/NormalNbhd · expToN⟧.
- $N$ is **geodesically complete** if $(q, v)$ lies in the domain of $\exp$ for every $q \in N$ and every $v \in T_qN$ ⟦Chapter7/GeodesicFlow · IsGeodesicallyComplete⟧ (Milnor, p. 58), equivalently if every geodesic on an open interval is the restriction of a geodesic on all of $\mathbb R$ ⟦Chapter7/GeodesicFlow · isGeodesicallyComplete_iff⟧. For connected nonempty $N$ the Hopf–Rinow theorem makes this equivalent to completeness of $(N, \rho)$ and to compactness of the closures of bounded sets ⟦Chapter7/HopfRinow · hopf_rinow⟧.

**Jacobi fields and conjugate points.**

- A **smooth curve in $N$ on $[0, 1]$** is a $\gamma : \mathbb R \to V$, smooth on $[0, 1]$, with values in $N$ there ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsSmoothCurveIn⟧: Milnor's $\gamma : [0, 1] \to M$.
- The **Jacobi operator** along $\gamma$ is $J \mapsto D^2J/dt^2 + R(V, J)V$, with $V = d\gamma/dt$ and $D/dt$, $R$ as in §0.3 ⟦Chapter8/Jacobi · jacobiExpr⟧. $J$ is a **Jacobi field** along $\gamma$ (Milnor, p. 77) if it is smooth on $[0, 1]$, tangent to $N$ along $\gamma$ there, and satisfies the Jacobi equation on $(0, 1)$, hence by continuity on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter8/Jacobi · IsJacobiField⟧. For a smooth curve $\gamma$, the Jacobi fields normalized to be constant outside $[0, 1]$ form a vector space ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · jacobiSpace⟧ of dimension $2n$, $n = \dim N$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · finrank_jacobiSpace⟧, a field being determined by $J(s)$ and $DJ/dt(s)$ for any $s \in [0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · jacEquiv⟧. The normalization makes "a field along $\gamma|[0, 1]$" a single function; without it, every Jacobi field would have infinitely many representatives, differing off $[0, 1]$.
- Two parameter values $a \ne b$ in $[0, 1]$ are **conjugate along $\gamma$** if some Jacobi field vanishes at $a$ and at $b$ without vanishing identically on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · IsConjugate⟧. Milnor speaks of the points $\gamma(a)$ and $\gamma(b)$, and adds in a footnote that for a curve with self-intersections one should speak of parameter values; the formal definition takes the footnote's form. The **multiplicity** is the dimension of the space of Jacobi fields vanishing at $a$ and $b$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · conjSpace⟧ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · conjMultiplicity⟧; for $a \ne b$ in $[0, 1]$ it is positive exactly when $a$ and $b$ are conjugate ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · isConjugate_iff⟧. The points $q$ conjugate to $p$ along some geodesic from $p$ to $q$ form the conjugate locus of $p$ ⟦Chapter11/NonConjugate · conjLocus⟧.

**The Hessian of the energy.** Milnor defines $E_{**} : T\Omega_\gamma \times T\Omega_\gamma \to \mathbb R$ at a geodesic $\gamma$ (p. 74) as $\partial^2 E(\bar\alpha(u_1, u_2))/\partial u_1\partial u_2$ at $(0, 0)$, for a two-parameter variation with variation fields $W_1$ and $W_2$, and Theorem 13.1 computes it. The formal definition takes the computed formula as the definition and proves the rest.

- The **second variation** at a path $\gamma$ is the right side of Milnor's Theorem 13.1,
$$-\sum_t \big\langle W_2(t), \Delta_t \tfrac{DW_1}{dt}\big\rangle - \int_0^1 \big\langle W_2, \tfrac{D^2W_1}{dt^2} + R(V, W_1)V\big\rangle\,dt,$$
where $\Delta_t\,DW_1/dt = P_{\gamma(t)}(\Delta_t W_1')$ and the sum runs over the finitely many $t \in (0, 1)$ at which the term is not zero ⟦Chapter8/SecondVariation · secondVariation⟧. Its integral form is $\int_0^1 \big(\langle DW_1/dt, DW_2/dt\rangle - \langle R(V, W_1)V, W_2\rangle\big)\,dt$ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · hessForm⟧, and the two agree for every $\gamma \in \Omega$ and all $W_1, W_2 \in T\Omega_\gamma$ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · secondVariation_eq_hessForm⟧.
- **$E_{**}$** is the bilinear form on $T\Omega_\gamma$ given by twice the second variation ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · energyHessian⟧, defined for every $\gamma \in \Omega$. Bilinearity comes from the integral form, and so does symmetry ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · energyHessian_isSymm⟧, together with the symmetry of $\langle R(V, W_1)V, W_2\rangle$ in $W_1$, $W_2$. That this is Milnor's $E_{**}$ is Theorem 13.1: at an unbroken geodesic, for every two-parameter variation with fields $W_1$ and $W_2$, the iterated derivative $\partial/\partial u_1\big(\partial E/\partial u_2\,(u_1, 0)\big)$ at $u_1 = 0$ exists and equals $E_{**}(W_1, W_2)$ ⟦Chapter8/SecondVariation · second_variation⟧ ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · hasDerivAt_energyHessian⟧, and every pair $(W_1, W_2)$ arises in this way ⟦Chapter8/FirstVariation · exists_variation_two⟧. Milnor derives the symmetry from $\partial^2/\partial u_1\partial u_2 = \partial^2/\partial u_2\partial u_1$, which would need $E\circ\bar\alpha$ to be $C^2$ near $(0, 0)$; the integral form avoids this.
- **Null space and nullity.** Elements of $T\Omega_\gamma$ are functions on $\mathbb R$ whose values off $[0, 1]$ play no role, so the null space of the bilinear form $E_{**}$ itself contains every field that vanishes on $[0, 1]$ and is infinite-dimensional. The **null space** of $E_{**}$ in Milnor's sense is therefore its image under restriction to $[0, 1]$, $X \mapsto X\circ\pi$ with $\pi : \mathbb R \to [0, 1]$ the nearest-point projection ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · restrictIcc⟧ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · hessNullSpace⟧, and the **nullity** is the dimension of this space ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · hessNullity⟧. Theorem 14.1 identifies the null space with the space of Jacobi fields vanishing at $0$ and $1$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · hessNullSpace_eq⟧, and the nullity with the multiplicity of $0$ and $1$ as conjugate parameter values ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · theorem_14_1_nullity⟧.

**The index of $E_{**}$.** Milnor defines it (p. 83) as the maximum dimension of a subspace of $T\Omega_\gamma$ on which $E_{**}$ is negative definite; that this maximum is finite is part of Theorem 15.1. Mathlib's index of §0.2 is $0$ on the infinite-dimensional space $T\Omega_\gamma$, so a different definition is needed.

- For a bilinear form $B$ on any real vector space, $B$ is **negative definite** on a subspace $S$ if $B(x, x) < 0$ for every $0 \ne x \in S$ ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · IsNegDefOn⟧, and the **index** of $B$ is the supremum, in $\{0, 1, 2, \dots\} \cup \{\infty\}$, of the dimensions of the finite-dimensional subspaces on which $B$ is negative definite ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · formIndex⟧. This is Milnor's index with the value $\infty$ allowed: if $B$ is negative definite on an infinite-dimensional subspace, that subspace has finite-dimensional subspaces of every dimension, and the supremum is $\infty$. On a finite-dimensional space it is Mathlib's index ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · formIndex_eq_sigNeg⟧. Milnor's $\lambda(\tau)$, the index of $E_{**}$ on the fields along $\gamma|[0, \tau]$, is the same notion for the restricted form ⟦Chapter9/IndexReduction · indexUpTo⟧.
- The **index of a geodesic** $\gamma \in \Omega(N; p, q)$, as a natural number, is the index of $E_{**}$ at $\gamma$, converted to a natural number with $\infty \mapsto 0$, and $0$ if $\gamma \notin \Omega$ ⟦Chapter10/FundamentalTheorem · geodesicIndex⟧. For an unbroken geodesic the index is finite, so this is the index itself ⟦Chapter10/FundamentalTheorem · geodesicIndex_eq⟧: the index theorem, Theorem 15.1, states that the parameter values in $(0, 1)$ conjugate to $0$ are finitely many and that the index of $E_{**}$ equals the sum of their multiplicities ⟦Chapter9/IndexTheorem · theorem_15_1⟧.

### 0.10 Flows of vector fields

Milnor's Lemma 2.4 (p. 10) produces a one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms from a compactly supported vector field on a manifold, quoting the smooth dependence of solutions of differential equations on their initial conditions. The formal flows live on a normed space $E$, in the applications $\mathbb R^K$ or $V$; a flow of a submanifold is the restriction of an ambient flow that preserves it.

- A vector field on $E$ is a map $X : E \to E$, and an integral curve of $X$ is a $\gamma : \mathbb R \to E$ with $\gamma'(t) = X(\gamma(t))$ for every $t$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/ODE/Basic · IsIntegralCurve⟧. The standing hypothesis for global flows is that $X$ is **bounded and globally Lipschitz** ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz⟧, which a $C^1$ field with compact support is ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.of_contDiff⟧.
- The **flow** $\varphi_t(x)$ is the value at time $t$ of an integral curve defined on all of $\mathbb R$ and passing through $x$ at time $0$, chosen by the axiom of choice, and $x$ if there is none ⟦Flow/Global · flow⟧. For bounded Lipschitz $X$ there is exactly one such curve ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flow_spec⟧ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.eq_flow⟧; $\varphi_{s+t} = \varphi_s\circ\varphi_t$ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flow_add⟧; $(t, x) \mapsto \varphi_t(x)$ is continuous ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.continuous_flow⟧; each $\varphi_t$ is a homeomorphism with inverse $\varphi_{-t}$ ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flowHomeomorph⟧; and zeros of $X$ are fixed ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flow_of_eq_zero⟧. A closed set through each point of which there is a local integral curve staying in the set is invariant under the flow ⟦Flow/Global · IsBddLipschitz.flow_mem⟧; this is how a field tangent to a submanifold $N$ gives a flow of $N$ (Part II, ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · flow_mem_of_tangent⟧). Since a bounded field has solutions on time intervals of any length, Milnor's iteration of $\varphi_{\varepsilon_0/2}$, and the check that it is well defined, are not needed.
- **Smoothness.** For $X$ of class $C^n$, $1 \le n \le \infty$, with compact support on a complete space, each $\varphi_t$ is of class $C^n$ ⟦Flow/Smooth · contDiff_flow⟧ and so is $(t, x) \mapsto \varphi_t(x)$ ⟦Flow/Smooth · contDiff_flow_uncurry⟧; each $\varphi_t$ is therefore a $C^n$ diffeomorphism ⟦Flow/Smooth · flowDiffeomorph⟧. Milnor's "well known" smooth dependence is proved, through the inverse function theorem in the Banach space $C([0, 1], E)$ applied to $\gamma \mapsto \gamma - \int_0^{\cdot} X(\gamma)$ ⟦Flow/Volterra · voltHomeomorph⟧ (Part I).
- A **one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms** (Milnor, pp. 9–10) is a family $(\varphi_t)$ such that $(t, x) \mapsto \varphi_t(x)$ is smooth, each $\varphi_t$ is a diffeomorphism, and $\varphi_{s+t} = \varphi_s\circ\varphi_t$ ⟦Flow/Smooth · IsOneParameterGroup⟧; then $\varphi_0$ is the identity ⟦Flow/Smooth · IsOneParameterGroup.zero⟧. It is **generated** by $X$ if $\frac{d}{dh}\varphi_h(x)\big|_{h=0} = X(x)$ for every $x$ ⟦Flow/Smooth · Generates⟧. Milnor's Lemma 2.4, for $M = E$ a complete real normed space (finite-dimensional in every application): a smooth vector field with compact support generates exactly one one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms, its flow ⟦Flow/Smooth · lemma_2_4⟧.
- **Local flows and linear equations.** For a smooth field on an open subset of a finite-dimensional space, solutions exist near any point for a uniform time and depend smoothly on the initial point ⟦Chapter7/ODE · exists_localFlow⟧, and two solutions that agree at one time agree on every open interval on which both are defined ⟦Chapter7/ODE · ode_eqOn⟧; the second-order form of this is Milnor's Theorem 10.1 ⟦Chapter7/ODE · theorem_10_1⟧. A linear equation $x' = A(t)x$ with continuous $A$ has solutions on all of $\mathbb R$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiODE · exists_linearODE⟧, and its propagator sends $(s, \xi)$ to the solution with value $\xi$ at time $s$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiODE · linSol⟧. Jacobi fields (§0.9) are components of the solutions of such a system ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · jacSol⟧.
- **Gradient flows and slabs.** Milnor's field in the proof of Theorem 3.1 is $X = \rho\cdot\operatorname{grad} f$ ⟦Chapter2/Regular · gradField⟧. What the proof uses of its flow is recorded abstractly: a continuous flow $\varphi$ on a space $T$ is a **slab flow** for a continuous $F : T \to \mathbb R$ and an interval $[a, b]$ if $t \mapsto F(\varphi_t(x))$ has derivative $1$ at every time at which its value lies in $[a, b]$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsSlabFlow⟧, and the topological half of Theorem 3.1 is proved for any slab flow (Part II). Finally, the geodesic flow, the position and velocity at time $t$ of $s \mapsto \exp_y(sw)$, is read off from $\exp$ ⟦Chapter7/GeodesicFlow · geoFlow⟧.
