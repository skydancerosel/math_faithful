## Part III. Reeb, the torus, the Euler number and focal points (Milnor §§4–6)

This part retells the formal proofs of Milnor's §4 (Reeb's theorem), of the opening example of §1 (the torus standing on a plane, whose Lean files live with §4 because they use the results of §5), of the half of §5 that needs no homology (the alternating count $\sum_\lambda (-1)^\lambda C_\lambda$ of the critical points of a Morse function is a topological invariant, read through Application 2 of §6), and of the whole of §6: Sard's theorem on manifolds, the normal bundle and its endpoint map, focal points and their multiplicities, principal curvatures, Lemmas 6.3–6.5, Theorem 6.6, Corollaries 6.7 and 6.8, and Lemma 6.9. The homological half of §5, the Morse inequalities in singular homology and the identification of the Euler number with the homological Euler characteristic, is Part IV.

The setting is that of the companion project, the formalization of Milnor's *Topology from the Differentiable Viewpoint* (the companion book below): a manifold is a slice submanifold of a finite-dimensional real normed space, usually of $\mathbb R^n$, with its induced charts (§0.1, §0.3), and critical points, Hessians, indices, nullities and Morse functions are the intrinsic notions of §0.2, read in charts. Most proofs are Milnor's, with every step he leaves to the reader carried out. There are five departures. (1) In Reeb's theorem the gluing of the two cells, which Milnor calls easy, is Alexander's trick followed by the pasting lemma, and Theorem 3.1 is re-run to see that the flow also carries the boundary sphere of one cell onto that of the other. (2) Formula (3) of §5 is proved by running Application 2 of §6 backwards: the index sum of the gradient field of a Morse function is $\sum_\lambda (-1)^\lambda C_\lambda$, and by the Poincaré–Hopf theorem of the companion project every tangent field has the same index sum; no homology is used. (3) In §6 no normal frame is chosen: the tangent space of the normal bundle is the kernel of the derivative of a local defining function, and the principal curvatures are the eigenvalues of a self-adjoint shape operator on the tangent space, which removes Milnor's normalization $g_{ij} = \delta_{ij}$. (4) Corollary 6.8 is proved by a linear perturbation $f + \langle a, \cdot\rangle$, composed with a diffeomorphism of $\mathbb R^n$ onto its unit ball, instead of Milnor's construction with an embedding whose first coordinate is $f$; this removes his hypothesis that $f$ be bounded. (5) The results proved for submanifolds are carried to abstract compact Hausdorff manifolds through Mathlib's Whitney embedding, after proving that the image of an embedding is a submanifold diffeomorphic to the manifold, and that every Morse notion is invariant under diffeomorphisms.

Results carry the labels V3.1–V3.25 and V4.1–V4.25 of the verified edition (`Exposition/verification/ch03.md`, `ch04.md`), so that one can move between the two texts; V3.26–V3.40 belong to Part IV.

### III.1 Extrema: Fermat's rule and the indices $0$ and $n$

Reeb's proof opens with a sentence Milnor does not justify: the two critical points must be the minimum and the maximum, and near each of them the lemma of Morse produces a small closed cell. The second half presupposes that the index is $0$ at a non-degenerate minimum and $n$ at a non-degenerate maximum; for any other index the set $\{f \le f(p) + \varepsilon\}$ is not a ball near $p$. Both facts are proved for a manifold $M$ modelled on a real normed space $E$, finite-dimensional where indices occur, with $n = \dim E$.

*Extrema are critical points.* Let $\varphi_p$ be the extended chart at $p$ and $f_p = f \circ \varphi_p^{-1}$ the local representative of $f$ (§0.2) ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · localRep⟧. If $p$ is a local minimum of $f$, then $\varphi_p(p)$ is a local minimum of $f_p$: the inverse chart is continuous at $\varphi_p(p)$ and sends it back to $p$, so the inequality $f(p) \le f$, valid near $p$, pulls back to a neighbourhood of $\varphi_p(p)$, where $f_p(\varphi_p(p)) = f(p)$ ⟦Chapter3/ReebMorse · isLocalMin_localRep⟧ ⟦Chapter3/ReebMorse · isLocalMax_localRep⟧. Mathlib's form of Fermat's rule makes the derivative of $f_p$ vanish there, and a point is critical exactly when this derivative vanishes ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · isCriticalPointM_iff_fderiv⟧. So local minima and maxima are critical points ⟦Chapter3/ReebMorse · isCriticalPointM_of_isLocalMin⟧ ⟦Chapter3/ReebMorse · isCriticalPointM_of_isLocalMax⟧. No differentiability is needed: where $f_p$ is not differentiable its formal derivative is $0$, and Fermat's rule holds trivially.

*The normal form along an axis.* Write Milnor's normal form on $\mathbb R^n$ as
$$\nu_{c,k}(w) = c - \sum_{i < k} w_i^2 + \sum_{i \ge k} w_i^2$$
⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · morseNormalForm⟧ (coordinates are numbered from $0$, as in the formal text). On the $i$-th axis, $\nu_{c,k}(s e_i)$ is $c - s^2$ if $i < k$ and $c + s^2$ otherwise ⟦Chapter3/ReebMorse · morseNormalForm_single⟧. Let $y$ be a chart around $p$ with $y(p) = 0$ and $f = \nu_{f(p),k} \circ y$ on its domain. Then $y^{-1}(s e_i) \to p$ as $s \to 0$, the inverse chart being continuous at $0 = y(p)$; and for all small $s$ the point $s e_i$ lies in the open target of $y$, so that $f(y^{-1}(s e_i)) = \nu_{f(p),k}(s e_i)$ is $f(p) - s^2$ or $f(p) + s^2$ according to the sign carried by the axis ⟦Chapter3/ReebMorse · tendsto_and_eventually_morse_axis⟧.

**Proposition (V3.1, the index at an extremum).** Let $M$ be a smooth manifold without boundary modelled on a finite-dimensional real space $E$, $n = \dim E$, let $f$ be smooth on an open set $U \ni p$, and let $p$ be a non-degenerate critical point of $f$. If $p$ is a local minimum, the index of $f$ at $p$ is $0$; if it is a local maximum, the index is $n$ ⟦Chapter3/ReebMorse · indexM_eq_zero_of_isLocalMin⟧ ⟦Chapter3/ReebMorse · indexM_eq_finrank_of_isLocalMax⟧.

*Proof as formalized.* The lemma of Morse on manifolds (Part I) gives smooth coordinates $y$ around $p$ with values in $\mathbb R^n$, domain inside $U$, $y(p) = 0$ and $f = \nu_{f(p),\lambda} \circ y$, $\lambda$ the index of $f$ at $p$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · morse_lemmaM⟧. Suppose that $p$ is a local minimum and $\lambda \ne 0$. As $\lambda \le n$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · indexM_le_finrank⟧, $n \ge 1$ and the axis $i = 0$ carries a minus sign: along it $f(y^{-1}(s e_0)) = f(p) - s^2$ for small $s$. Minimality, pulled back along the curve $s \mapsto y^{-1}(s e_0)$, which tends to $p$, gives $f(p) \le f(y^{-1}(s e_0))$ for small $s$. A small $s \ne 0$ exists (the punctured neighbourhoods of $0$ form a proper filter), and for it $f(p) \le f(p) - s^2 < f(p)$, a contradiction. At a local maximum with $\lambda < n$ the last axis $i = n - 1$ carries a plus sign, and $f(p) + s^2 \le f(p)$ is the contradiction.

*Morse charts at the extrema, in Euclidean coordinates (V3.2 (i)).* With $\lambda = 0$ the normal form is $f(p) + \sum_i y_i^2$; with $\lambda = n$ it is $f(p) - \sum_i y_i^2$. The lemma of Morse produces coordinates in $\{0, \dots, n-1\} \to \mathbb R$, whose Mathlib norm is the maximum norm, so $\sum_i y_i^2$ is not the square of that norm and its sublevel sets are not balls for it. The coordinates are therefore followed by the identification with Euclidean $\mathbb R^n$; this is still a smooth coordinate system ⟦Chapter3/ReebMorse · IsSmoothCoord.transHomeomorph_euclidean⟧, and in it $\sum_i y_i^2 = \|y\|^2$ ⟦Chapter3/ReebMorse · norm_sq_toLp⟧. The result: at a non-degenerate local minimum there are smooth Euclidean coordinates $y$ with $y(p) = 0$, domain inside $U$, and $f = f(p) + \|y\|^2$ on that domain; at a non-degenerate local maximum, $f = f(p) - \|y\|^2$ ⟦Chapter3/ReebMorse · exists_morse_chart_of_isLocalMin⟧ ⟦Chapter3/ReebMorse · exists_morse_chart_of_isLocalMax⟧. These are the two cases of Lemma 2.2 that Milnor's appeal to it requires.

### III.2 Small sublevel sets are closed balls

**Proposition (V3.2 (ii)).** Let $X$ be a compact topological space, $F$ a real normed space, $f : X \to \mathbb R$ continuous with a strict global minimum at $p$ (that is, $f(p) < f(x)$ for every $x \ne p$), and $y$ a homeomorphism from an open neighbourhood of $p$ onto an open subset of $F$ with $y(p) = 0$ and $f = f(p) + \|y\|^2$ on its domain. There is $\delta > 0$ such that for every $0 < \varepsilon < \delta$ the map
$$h_\varepsilon(v) = y^{-1}(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\, v)$$
is continuous and injective on the closed unit ball $D$ of $F$, maps $D$ onto the sublevel set $\{f \le f(p) + \varepsilon\}$, and maps the unit sphere $S$ onto the level set $\{f = f(p) + \varepsilon\}$ ⟦Chapter3/ReebMorse · exists_image_closedBall_eq_sublevel⟧.

The lemma of Morse speaks about $f$ near $p$ only. Milnor's proviso that $\varepsilon$ be small enough hides that the *whole* sublevel set has to lie inside the Morse chart, and this is where the compactness of $X$ and the uniqueness of the minimum enter.

*Proof as formalized.*

1. The target of $y$ is open and contains $0$, so it contains a ball $B(0, r)$, $r > 0$. Let $U$ be the set of points of the domain of $y$ with $\|y\| < r$; it is open and contains $p$.
2. There is $\eta > 0$ with $f \ge f(p) + \eta$ off $U$. If $U = X$, take $\eta = 1$. Otherwise $X \setminus U$ is closed in the compact space $X$, hence compact and nonempty, and the continuous $f$ attains its minimum on it at some $x_1$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Order/Compact · IsCompact.exists_isMinOn⟧. As $p \in U$, $x_1 \ne p$, and $\eta = f(x_1) - f(p) > 0$ by the strictness of the minimum.
3. Put $\delta = \min(r^2, \eta)$ and fix $0 < \varepsilon < \delta$, so that $\sqrt\varepsilon < r$ and $\varepsilon < \eta$.
4. For $\|v\| \le 1$, $\|\sqrt\varepsilon\, v\| \le \sqrt\varepsilon < r$, so $\sqrt\varepsilon\, v$ lies in the target of $y$ and $f(h_\varepsilon(v)) = f(p) + \|\sqrt\varepsilon\, v\|^2 = f(p) + \varepsilon \|v\|^2$.
5. Conversely let $f(x) \le f(p) + \varepsilon$. Then $x \in U$, since off $U$ the value is at least $f(p) + \eta > f(p) + \varepsilon$; in particular $x$ lies in the domain of $y$. The vector $v = y(x)/\sqrt\varepsilon$ satisfies $\varepsilon\|v\|^2 = \|y(x)\|^2 = f(x) - f(p) \le \varepsilon$, so $v \in D$, and $h_\varepsilon(v) = y^{-1}(y(x)) = x$.
6. Continuity and injectivity on $D$ come from those of $y^{-1}$ on its target, composed with the scaling by $\sqrt\varepsilon \ne 0$. By 4 the image of $D$ lies in the sublevel set and by 5 it covers it. On the sphere $\|v\| = 1$, step 4 gives the value $f(p) + \varepsilon$; and if $f(x) = f(p) + \varepsilon$, the vector $v$ of step 5 has $\varepsilon\|v\|^2 = \varepsilon$, hence $\|v\| = 1$.

Applied to $-f$, at a strict maximum $q$ with $f = f(q) - \|y\|^2$, the proposition gives the same description of $\{f \ge f(q) - \varepsilon\}$ and of the level $\{f = f(q) - \varepsilon\}$. Milnor only says that the small sublevel sets are closed $n$-cells. The gluing of §III.3 needs more: a parametrization of the cell by the ball that carries the boundary sphere onto the level set, and this is what the proposition provides.

### III.3 Two balls make a sphere: Alexander's trick

Milnor ends the proof of Theorem 4.1 by declaring a homeomorphism with $S^n$ easy to construct, and constructs none. The file `Chapter3/ReebGluing` proves the purely topological statement behind that sentence.

*Alexander's cone.* Let $E$ be a real normed space with unit sphere $S$, and $g : S \to S$ any map. Its cone is
$$G(x) = \|x\|\, g\!\left(\frac{x}{\|x\|}\right) \quad (x \ne 0), \qquad G(0) = 0$$
⟦Chapter3/ReebGluing · alexanderCone⟧. Four identities are proved by direct computation. The cone preserves norms, $\|G(x)\| = \|x\|$, because $g$ takes values in the unit sphere ⟦Chapter3/ReebGluing · norm_alexanderCone⟧. It extends $g$, because $x/\|x\| = x$ on $S$ ⟦Chapter3/ReebGluing · alexanderCone_of_mem_sphere⟧. The cone of a composite is the composite of the cones: for $x \ne 0$ the point $G_{g'}(x)$ is nonzero, its norm being $\|x\|$, and its direction is $g'(x/\|x\|)$, so applying $G_g$ to it gives $\|x\|\, g(g'(x/\|x\|))$ ⟦Chapter3/ReebGluing · alexanderCone_alexanderCone⟧. And the cone of the identity is the identity ⟦Chapter3/ReebGluing · alexanderCone_id⟧. If $g$ is continuous, so is $G$ ⟦Chapter3/ReebGluing · continuous_alexanderCone⟧: on the open set $\{x \ne 0\}$ it is the composite $x \mapsto \|x\|\, g(\|x\|^{-1}x)$ of continuous maps, and at $0$ it is continuous *only* because $\|G(x)\| = \|x\| \to 0$.

**Alexander's trick (V3.5 (i)).** If $g$ is a homeomorphism of $S$, its cone is a homeomorphism of $E$, with inverse the cone of $g^{-1}$ ⟦Chapter3/ReebGluing · alexanderConeHomeomorph⟧. The two inverse identities are the composition rule and the identity rule above. The homeomorphism preserves norms, so it maps the closed unit ball onto itself and the unit sphere onto itself by $g$. It is in general not differentiable at $0$: this is the place in the proof of Reeb's theorem where smoothness is lost, in accordance with Milnor's Remark 2.

*Coordinates on the sphere.* Write a point of $\mathbb R^{n+1} = \mathbb R^n \times \mathbb R$ by its projection to the first $n$ coordinates and its last coordinate, its height ⟦Chapter3/ReebGluing · hemiProj⟧ ⟦Chapter3/ReebGluing · hemiHeight⟧, and conversely form the point $(v, s)$ ⟦Chapter3/ReebGluing · hemiLift⟧. Then $\|z\|^2 = \|\mathrm{proj}\, z\|^2 + \mathrm{height}(z)^2$ ⟦Chapter3/ReebGluing · norm_sq_eq_hemiProj_hemiHeight⟧. On $S^n$ the projection has norm at most $1$ and the height satisfies $\mathrm{height}^2 = 1 - \|\mathrm{proj}\|^2$ ⟦Chapter3/ReebGluing · norm_hemiProj_le⟧ ⟦Chapter3/ReebGluing · hemiHeight_sq⟧; $(v, s)$ lies on $S^n$ when $s^2 = 1 - \|v\|^2$ ⟦Chapter3/ReebGluing · hemiLift_mem_sphere⟧; and two points of $S^n$ with the same projection and heights of the same sign are equal, since the heights have the same square ⟦Chapter3/ReebGluing · eq_of_hemiProj_eq_of_sameSign⟧.

**Proposition (V3.5 (ii), two balls glued along their boundary spheres).** Let $X$ be a Hausdorff space, $n \ge 0$, $D$ and $S$ the closed unit ball and the unit sphere of Euclidean $\mathbb R^n$, and $h_1, h_2 : \mathbb R^n \to X$ maps that are continuous and injective on $D$, such that $h_1(D) \cup h_2(D) = X$, $h_1(D) \cap h_2(D) = h_1(S)$ and $h_2(S) = h_1(S)$. Then $X$ is homeomorphic to the unit sphere $S^n \subseteq \mathbb R^{n+1}$ ⟦Chapter3/ReebGluing · nonempty_homeomorph_sphere_of_union_closedBall⟧.

*Proof as formalized.*

1. *A point of the second ball lying in the first comes from the boundary.* If $x \in D$ and $h_2(x) \in h_1(D)$, then $h_2(x) \in h_1(D) \cap h_2(D) = h_1(S) = h_2(S)$, so $h_2(x) = h_2(w)$ with $w \in S$, and injectivity of $h_2$ on $D$ gives $x = w \in S$.
2. *The boundary identification.* Since $h_1(S) = h_2(S)$, each $v \in S$ has some $g(v) \in S$ with $h_2(g(v)) = h_1(v)$, and each $w \in S$ some $g'(w) \in S$ with $h_1(g'(w)) = h_2(w)$. Injectivity gives $g \circ g' = \mathrm{id}$ and $g' \circ g = \mathrm{id}$.
3. *It is a homeomorphism of $S$.* The restrictions of $h_1$ and $h_2$ to the compact sphere are continuous injections into a Hausdorff space, hence closed embeddings ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Separation/Hausdorff · Continuous.isClosedEmbedding⟧. As $h_2 \circ g = h_1$ is continuous and $h_2|_S$ is an embedding, $g$ is continuous; likewise $g'$. So $g$ is a homeomorphism of $S$.
4. *Alexander's trick.* Let $G$ be the cone of $g$, a homeomorphism of $\mathbb R^n$. It preserves norms, so it maps $D$ into $D$, and on $S$ it is $g$, so $h_2(G(x)) = h_1(x)$ for $x \in S$.
5. *The map $H : S^n \to X$.* Put $H(z) = h_1(\mathrm{proj}\, z)$ if $\mathrm{height}(z) \ge 0$ and $H(z) = h_2(G(\mathrm{proj}\, z))$ otherwise. Both formulas are continuous on all of $S^n$, because the projection of a point of $S^n$ lies in $D$, where $h_1$ and $h_2$ are continuous. Where the height is $0$, the projection has norm $1$, so lies in $S$, and the two formulas agree by step 4. The pasting lemma for a condition $u \le w$ between continuous functions then makes $H$ continuous ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Order/OrderClosed · Continuous.if_le⟧.
6. *$H$ is injective.* If both points are in the closed upper hemisphere, injectivity of $h_1$ on $D$ gives equal projections, and the heights have the same sign, so the points are equal. If $z$ is upper and $z'$ lower with $H(z) = H(z')$, then $h_2(G(\mathrm{proj}\, z')) \in h_1(D)$, so $G(\mathrm{proj}\, z') \in S$ by step 1; $G$ preserves norms, so $\|\mathrm{proj}\, z'\| = 1$ and the height of $z'$ is $0$, contradicting that it is negative. If both are lower, injectivity of $h_2$ on $D$ and of $G$ gives equal projections, and again the heights have the same sign.
7. *$H$ is surjective.* Every point of $X$ is $h_1(v)$ or $h_2(w)$ with $v, w \in D$. The point $h_1(v)$ is $H(v, \sqrt{1 - \|v\|^2})$. If $w \in S$, then $h_2(w) \in h_2(S) = h_1(S)$ is of the first kind. If $\|w\| < 1$, put $v = G^{-1}(w)$; then $\|v\| = \|w\| < 1$, the point $(v, -\sqrt{1 - \|v\|^2})$ has negative height, and $H$ sends it to $h_2(G(v)) = h_2(w)$.
8. A continuous bijection from the compact space $S^n$ to the Hausdorff space $X$ is a homeomorphism ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Homeomorph/Lemmas · Continuous.homeoOfEquivCompactToT2⟧, and its inverse is the required homeomorphism $X \cong S^n$.

The argument works for $n = 0$ as well, where $D$ is a point and $S$ is empty.

### III.4 Reeb's theorem

Two ingredients remain: the extrema of Step 1, and a version of Theorem 3.1 that also matches the boundary levels.

*Slab flows carry levels to levels.* Part II proves Theorem 3.1 for a *slab flow*: a continuous flow $\varphi$ on a space $T$ and a continuous $F : T \to \mathbb R$ such that $t \mapsto F(\varphi_t(x))$ has derivative $1$ at every time at which its value lies in $[a, b]$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsSlabFlow⟧. For such a flow, $F(\varphi_t(x)) = F(x) + t$ whenever $F(x) \in [a, b]$ and $a - F(x) \le t \le b - F(x)$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsSlabFlow.apply_eq_add⟧, and $\varphi_{c'-c}$ carries the sublevel set $T^c$ onto $T^{c'}$ for $a \le c \le c' \le b$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsSlabFlow.image_sublevel⟧. The new statement is that $\varphi_{b-a}$ carries the level $F^{-1}(a)$ onto $F^{-1}(b)$ ⟦Chapter3/Reeb · IsSlabFlow.image_level⟧. If $F(x) = a$, the time $t = b - a$ lies in $[a - F(x), b - F(x)] = [0, b - a]$, so $F(\varphi_{b-a}(x)) = a + (b - a) = b$. Conversely, if $F(y) = b$, the time $a - b$ lies in $[a - b, 0]$, so $F(\varphi_{a-b}(y)) = a$, and $\varphi_{b-a}(\varphi_{a-b}(y)) = \varphi_0(y) = y$ by the group law.

**Theorem 3.1 with the boundary levels (V3.4).** Let $N$ be a slice submanifold of a finite-dimensional real normed space $V$, modelled on a finite-dimensional space $Z$, let $F : V \to \mathbb R$ be smooth and $f = F|_N$, and let $a \le b$ with $f^{-1}[a, b]$ compact and free of critical points of $f$. There is a smooth diffeomorphism $\Phi$ of $N$ with $\Phi(N^a) = N^b$ and $\Phi(f^{-1}(a)) = f^{-1}(b)$ ⟦Chapter3/Reeb · exists_diffeomorph_image_sublevel_level⟧.

The *statement* of Theorem 3.1 says nothing about the level $f^{-1}(a)$; its *proof* does, since the flow moves the levels of the slab at unit speed. The formal statement of Theorem 3.1 on submanifolds records only the sublevel sets ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · theorem_3_1_submanifold⟧, so its construction is repeated here, with one more conclusion. *The proof as formalized*, in the steps of Part II: the set $K = f^{-1}[a, b]$, compact in $V$, carries no critical point, so there is a smooth field $Y$ on $V$, tangent to $N$ along $N$, with $dF(Y) > 0$ on $K$ ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · exists_tangent_field⟧. A smooth, compactly supported $\rho$ with support in the union of the slice-chart domains of $N$ ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · chartDomain⟧ and $\rho\, dF(Y) = 1$ on $K$ exists ⟦Chapter2/Regular · exists_cutoff_mul⟧. The field $X = \rho Y$ is smooth, compactly supported, tangent to $N$, and satisfies $dF(X) = 1$ on $K$. Its global flow ⟦Flow/Global · flow⟧ preserves $N$ ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · flow_mem_of_tangent⟧, and restricted to $N$ it is a slab flow for $f$ on $[a, b]$. Each time-$t$ map is smooth on $N$, as the restriction of a smooth map of $V$ ⟦Flow/Smooth · contDiff_flow⟧ with values in $N$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Corestrict · contMDiff_codRestrict⟧. So $\Phi = \varphi_{b-a}$, with inverse $\varphi_{-(b-a)}$, is a smooth diffeomorphism of $N$; $\Phi(N^a) = N^b$ by the slab-flow lemma on sublevel sets, and $\Phi(f^{-1}(a)) = f^{-1}(b)$ by the one on levels.

**Step 1: the two critical points are the extrema (V3.3).** Let $M$ be a compact smooth manifold without boundary modelled on a finite-dimensional $E$ with $n = \dim E \ge 1$, and $f$ smooth with exactly two critical points, both non-degenerate. Then there are $p, q$ with $f(p) < f(q)$, the critical points are exactly $p$ and $q$, $f(p) < f(x)$ for every $x \ne p$, $f(x) < f(q)$ for every $x \ne q$, the index is $0$ at $p$ and $n$ at $q$ ⟦Chapter3/Reeb · exists_min_max_of_ncard_eq_two⟧.

*Proof as formalized.* *Exactly two* means formally that the set of critical points has cardinality $2$; in particular $M$ is nonempty. By compactness $f$ attains its minimum at some $p$ and its maximum at some $q$. Every point where the minimum (maximum) value is attained is a local minimum (maximum), hence critical, and $p$, $q$ have indices $0$ and $n$ (§III.1, with $U = M$). If $f(p) = f(q)$, then $p$ also attains the maximum value, so its index is also $n$, and $n = 0$, contrary to $n \ge 1$. Hence $f(p) < f(q)$ and $p \ne q$. The set $\{p, q\}$ consists of critical points, has two elements, and the critical set is finite with two elements, so the two sets are equal. Finally, if $x \ne p$ had $f(x) = f(p)$, it would be a minimum, hence critical, hence equal to $q$, whose value is larger; so $f(p) < f(x)$, and symmetrically $f(x) < f(q)$ for $x \ne q$.

Two hypotheses that Milnor does not state appear here. That the minimum and the maximum are *different* points needs $n \ge 1$ (in dimension $0$ every point is critical and $f$ may be constant) and the non-degeneracy, which forbids a point to be at the same time a minimum of index $0$ and a maximum of index $n$. And that *no other point* takes an extreme value is what §III.2 needs.

**Theorem 4.1 (Reeb; V3.6).** Let $N$ be a compact slice submanifold of a finite-dimensional real normed space $V$, modelled on a space $Z$ of dimension $n$, and $f = F|_N$ with $F : V \to \mathbb R$ smooth. If $f$ has exactly two critical points, both non-degenerate, then $N$ is homeomorphic to the unit sphere $S^n \subseteq \mathbb R^{n+1}$ ⟦Chapter3/Reeb · reeb⟧.

*Proof as formalized, for $n \ge 1$* ⟦Chapter3/Reeb · reeb_of_finrank_pos⟧. The function $f$ is smooth on the compact manifold $N$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · contMDiff_comp_val⟧.

1. Step 1 gives the strict minimum $p$ and the strict maximum $q$, of indices $0$ and $n$.
2. §III.1 gives Euclidean Morse coordinates $y_p$ at $p$ with $f = f(p) + \|y_p\|^2$ and $y_q$ at $q$ with $f = f(q) - \|y_q\|^2$. §III.2 applied to $f$ at $p$ gives $\delta_1$, and applied to $-f$ at $q$ (a strict minimum of $-f$, where $-f = -f(q) + \|y_q\|^2$) gives $\delta_2$.
3. Put $\varepsilon = \tfrac14 \min(\delta_1, \delta_2, f(q) - f(p))$. Then $0 < \varepsilon < \delta_1, \delta_2$ and $f(p) + \varepsilon \le f(q) - \varepsilon$. The map $h_0(v) = y_p^{-1}(\sqrt\varepsilon\, v)$ carries $D$ onto $\{f \le f(p) + \varepsilon\}$ and $S$ onto $\{f = f(p) + \varepsilon\}$; the map $h_2(v) = y_q^{-1}(\sqrt\varepsilon\, v)$ carries $D$ onto $\{f \ge f(q) - \varepsilon\}$ and $S$ onto $\{f = f(q) - \varepsilon\}$.
4. The slab $f^{-1}[f(p) + \varepsilon, f(q) - \varepsilon]$ is closed in the compact $N$, hence compact, and contains no critical point, since the only critical values $f(p)$ and $f(q)$ lie outside it. Theorem 3.1 with the boundary levels gives a diffeomorphism $\Phi$ with $\Phi\{f \le f(p) + \varepsilon\} = \{f \le f(q) - \varepsilon\}$ and $\Phi\{f = f(p) + \varepsilon\} = \{f = f(q) - \varepsilon\}$.
5. Apply the gluing proposition of §III.3 to $h_1 = \Phi \circ h_0$ and $h_2$: both are continuous and injective on $D$. The images $h_1(D) = \{f \le f(q) - \varepsilon\}$ and $h_2(D) = \{f \ge f(q) - \varepsilon\}$ cover $N$, they meet in $\{f = f(q) - \varepsilon\} = \Phi\{f = f(p) + \varepsilon\} = h_1(S)$, and $h_2(S) = \{f = f(q) - \varepsilon\} = h_1(S)$.

Milnor's normalization $f(p) = 0$, $f(q) = 1$ is not needed, and the sphere is $S^n$ with $n = \dim N$.

*Dimension $0$* ⟦Chapter3/Reeb · reeb_of_finrank_eq_zero⟧. Milnor's proof does not apply, since $f$ may be constant, but the conclusion holds for every function $f : N \to \mathbb R$. On a $0$-dimensional manifold every point is critical, the derivative being a linear map on the zero space; so $N$ has exactly two points $p \ne q$. The gluing proposition with $n = 0$, the constant maps $h_1 \equiv p$, $h_2 \equiv q$, the one-point ball $D = \{0\}$ and the empty sphere $S$, gives $N \cong S^0$.

*Intrinsic functions* ⟦Chapter3/Reeb · reeb_of_contMDiff⟧. For a compact $N \subseteq \mathbb R^{K+1}$ of dimension $d + 1$ the theorem holds for every smooth $f : N \to \mathbb R$ with exactly two critical points, both non-degenerate: such an $f$ is the restriction of a smooth function on $\mathbb R^{K+1}$ (§III.9) ⟦Chapter3/EulerChar · exists_contDiff_comp_val_eq⟧, and $N$ is nonempty because it carries critical points. For an abstract compact manifold the theorem is §III.21.

*Not formalized.* Milnor's Remark 1, which allows the two critical points to be degenerate, needs M. Brown's theorem on monotone unions of open cells, which is not in Mathlib; Remark 2, a manifold homeomorphic but not diffeomorphic to $S^7$, needs characteristic classes.

### III.5 The torus as a regular level set

Milnor's book opens with a torus touching a plane, drawn upright, and its height function $f$. He gives no equations and does not say why the torus is a smooth compact surface. The formal text fixes the surface. For $0 < r < R$ let
$$T = \{x \in \mathbb R^3 : (\sqrt{x_1^2 + x_2^2} - R)^2 + x_3^2 = r^2\},$$
the torus of revolution about the $x_3$-axis, swept out by the circle of radius $r$ whose centre runs along the circle of radius $R$ in the plane $x_3 = 0$ ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · torus⟧. (The formal coordinates are numbered $0, 1, 2$; here they are $x_1, x_2, x_3$.) The function inside the definition is not differentiable on the $x_3$-axis. Instead of working off the axis, the formal proof uses a quartic polynomial with the same zero set,
$$g(x) = Q(x)^2 - 4R^2(x_1^2 + x_2^2), \qquad Q(x) = \|x\|^2 + R^2 - r^2$$
⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · quad⟧ ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · poly⟧.

*Derivatives.* Explicit formulas are proved for $dQ_x = 2\langle x, \cdot\rangle$ ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · hasFDerivAt_quad⟧, for
$$dg_x(w) = 4(Q - 2R^2)(x_1 w_1 + x_2 w_2) + 4Q\, x_3 w_3$$
⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · dpoly⟧ ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · hasFDerivAt_poly⟧ ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · dpoly_apply⟧, and for the second derivative $d^2g_x$ as the derivative of $x \mapsto dg_x$, computed coefficient by coefficient ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · d2poly⟧ ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · hasFDerivAt_coef⟧ ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · hasFDerivAt_dpoly⟧, so that the Hessian of $g$ is this bilinear form ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · hessian_poly⟧. The polynomial is smooth ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · contDiff_poly⟧.

*The zero set.* With $\rho = \sqrt{x_1^2 + x_2^2}$ one has the factorization
$$g = \big((\rho - R)^2 + x_3^2 - r^2\big)\big((\rho + R)^2 + x_3^2 - r^2\big),$$
a polynomial identity once $\rho^2 = x_1^2 + x_2^2$ is used ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · poly_eq_mul⟧. The second factor is at least $R^2 - r^2 > 0$, so $x \in T$ if and only if $g(x) = 0$ ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · mem_torus_iff⟧. Also $Q > 0$ everywhere, since $R^2 - r^2 > 0$ ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · quad_pos⟧.

**Lemma ($0$ is a regular value).** If $g(x) = 0$, then $dg_x \ne 0$ ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · dpoly_ne_zero⟧.

*Proof as formalized.* Suppose $dg_x = 0$. Its values on the three basis vectors give $(Q - 2R^2)x_1 = 0$, $(Q - 2R^2)x_2 = 0$ and $Q x_3 = 0$; as $Q > 0$, $x_3 = 0$. Then $g(x) = 0$ reads $Q^2 = 4R^2(x_1^2 + x_2^2)$ with $Q = x_1^2 + x_2^2 + R^2 - r^2$. If $Q = 2R^2$, then $4R^4 = 4R^2(x_1^2 + x_2^2)$ forces $x_1^2 + x_2^2 = R^2$, and then $Q = 2R^2 - r^2 = 2R^2$ forces $r = 0$, which is excluded. Otherwise $x_1 = x_2 = 0$, so $Q^2 = 0$, contradicting $Q > 0$.

**Proposition (V3.22).** $T$ is a $2$-dimensional slice submanifold of $\mathbb R^3$ (slice charts onto $\mathbb R^2 \times \mathbb R^1$), compact and nonempty ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · isSliceSubmanifold_torus⟧ ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · isCompact_torus⟧ ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · nonempty_torus⟧.

*Proof as formalized.* The level set theorem of the companion project wants a map into its model space, so $g$ is read with values in $\mathbb R^1$ ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · poly1⟧; its preimage of $0$ is $T$ ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · preimage_poly1⟧. At each point of $T$ its derivative is $dg_x$ followed by the isomorphism $\mathbb R \cong \mathbb R^1$, and a nonzero linear functional is onto, so $0$ is a regular value ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · isRegularValue_poly1⟧. Milnor's Lemma 1 of the companion book, that the preimage of a regular value is a submanifold of dimension $3 - 1$, gives the slice charts ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/LevelSet · isSliceSubmanifold_preimage⟧. $T$ is closed, as the preimage of a point ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · isClosed_torus⟧, and bounded: on $T$, $\rho \le R + r$ and $x_3^2 \le r^2$, so $\|x\|^2 = \rho^2 + x_3^2 \le (R + 2r)^2$ ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · norm_le_of_mem_torus⟧. Closed and bounded in $\mathbb R^3$, it is compact. The point $(R + r, 0, 0)$ lies on it.

*Points on the axis.* The point $(a, 0, 0)$ of the $x_1$-axis ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · onAxis⟧ lies on $T$ exactly when $(|a| - R)^2 = r^2$, in particular for $|a| = R \pm r$ ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · onAxis_mem_iff⟧ ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · onAxis_mem⟧. There
$$dg = 4a(a^2 - R^2 - r^2)\, dx_1, \qquad d^2g(v, w) = 4(a^2 - R^2 - r^2)\, v_2 w_2 + 4(a^2 + R^2 - r^2)\, v_3 w_3 \quad (v_1 = 0)$$
⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · dpoly_onAxis⟧ ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · d2poly_onAxis⟧.

**Proposition (the tangent plane).** For $q \in T$ the tangent plane $T_qT \subseteq \mathbb R^3$, the range of the derivative of the inclusion (§0.3) ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · tangentSubspace⟧, has dimension $2$ and equals $\ker dg_q$ ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · finrank_tangentSubspace_torus⟧ ⟦Chapter3/TorusSurface · mem_tangentSubspace_torus_iff⟧.

*Proof as formalized.* The derivative of the inclusion of a slice submanifold is injective ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/LevelSet · injective_mfderiv_val⟧, so its range has dimension $2$. Since $g$ vanishes on $T$, the restriction $g|_T$ is constant and every point is a critical point of it; a point is critical for the restriction of an ambient function exactly when the ambient derivative vanishes on the tangent space ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · isCriticalPointM_comp_val_iff_forall_tangent⟧. So $T_qT \subseteq \ker dg_q$. The kernel has dimension $3 - 1 = 2$ by rank–nullity, $dg_q$ being a nonzero functional. A subspace contained in another of the same finite dimension is equal to it. This is Lemma 2 of the companion book, re-proved for the set $T$ rather than for the preimage $g^{-1}(0)$.

### III.6 The four critical points by Lagrange's rule

Milnor reads off the picture that the critical points of the height function are the four points $p, q, r, s$ marked on it, and that $f$ has the normal forms $x^2 + y^2$, $\mathrm{const} + x^2 - y^2$ (twice) and $\mathrm{const} - x^2 - y^2$ there; he verifies none of this. The height is formalized as
$$f(x) = x_1 + R + r,$$
the height above the plane $V = \{x_1 = -(R + r)\}$ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · height⟧. The axis of revolution is parallel to $V$, so the torus stands upright, as in Milnor's figure. Lying flat, its height function would have whole circles of degenerate critical points. The four points are
$$p = (-(R + r), 0, 0), \quad q = (-(R - r), 0, 0), \quad r = (R - r, 0, 0), \quad s = (R + r, 0, 0)$$
⟦Chapter3/Torus · pointP⟧ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · pointQ⟧ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · pointR⟧ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · pointS⟧, at heights $0, 2r, 2R, 2R + 2r$ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · height_pointP⟧ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · height_pointQ⟧ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · height_pointR⟧ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · height_pointS⟧. (The letter $r$ is both the tube radius, as in the formal text, and Milnor's name for the third point; the context tells which.) For these four values of $a$ one has $a \ne 0$ and $a^2 - R^2 - r^2 = \pm 2Rr \ne 0$ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · coef_ne_zero⟧. On $T$, $|x_1| \le \rho \le R + r$, so $0 \le f \le 2R + 2r$ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · height_mem_Icc⟧. Hence $T^a = \emptyset$ for $a < 0$ and $T^a = T$ for $a \ge 2R + 2r$ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · sublevel_height_eq_empty⟧ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · sublevel_height_eq_univ⟧: these are Milnor's cases (1) and (5). The scan prints the hypothesis of (1) as $a < 0 < f(p)$; since $V$ touches $T$ at $p$, $f(p) = 0$ and it must read $a < 0 = f(p)$. Case (5) holds for $a = f(s)$ as well.

**Proposition (V3.23, the critical points).** A point of $T$ is a critical point of $f|_T$ if and only if it is one of $p, q, r, s$ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · isCriticalPointM_height_iff⟧ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · isCriticalPointM_height_iff'⟧.

*Proof as formalized.* By §III.5, $x$ is critical iff $dx_1$ vanishes on $T_xT = \ker dg_x$ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · isCriticalPointM_height_iff_forall⟧. Suppose it does. Two explicit vectors lie in $\ker dg_x$: the rotation vector $(-x_2, x_1, 0)$, on which $dg_x$ gives $4(Q - 2R^2)(-x_1x_2 + x_2x_1) = 0$, and the vector $(Q x_3, 0, -(Q - 2R^2)x_1)$, on which it gives $4(Q - 2R^2)x_1 Q x_3 - 4Q x_3 (Q - 2R^2) x_1 = 0$. Their first coordinates must vanish: $x_2 = 0$, and $Q x_3 = 0$, so $x_3 = 0$. The equation of $T$ then reads $(|x_1| - R)^2 = r^2$, so $|x_1| = R \pm r$, and $x$ is one of the four points. Conversely, at $(a, 0, 0)$ with $a = \pm(R \pm r)$, $dg = \kappa\, dx_1$ with $\kappa = 4a(a^2 - R^2 - r^2) \ne 0$, so $\ker dg = \{w_1 = 0\}$, on which $dx_1$ vanishes.

*Lagrange's rule, as a lemma about Hessians.* Two general facts about a slice submanifold $N$ of a normed space are proved first. A critical point of an ambient function $G$ at a point of $N$ is a critical point of $G|_N$ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · isCriticalPointM_comp_val_of_fderiv_eq_zero⟧. And if $dG_q = 0$ and $x$ is the parametrization of $N$ given by a slice chart at $q$ ($x(u) = \sigma^{-1}(u, 0)$; §0.3) ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · sliceParam⟧, then
$$\operatorname{Hess}(G \circ x)(v, w) = d^2G_q(dx\, v, dx\, w):$$
the second-order chain rule has a further term $dG_q(d^2x(v, w))$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · hessian_comp_apply⟧, and it vanishes because $dG_q = 0$ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · hessian_comp⟧ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · hessian_comp_sliceParam_of_fderiv_eq_zero⟧. For $G = f - \mu g$ with $N = g^{-1}(0)$ and $df_q = \mu\, dg_q$ this computes the Hessian of $f|_N$: it is Lagrange's rule.

*Diagonal forms on a plane.* On a $2$-dimensional space, a symmetric form $B$ with $B(u, u) < 0$ and $B(w, w) > 0$ for some $u, w$ has index $1$ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · index_eq_one_of_finrank_eq_two⟧. Recall that the index is the largest dimension of a subspace on which the form is negative definite (§0.2) ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · index_isGreatest⟧. A negative definite subspace of dimension $2$ would be the whole plane and contain $w$; and the line through $u$ is negative definite, since $B(cu, cu) = c^2 B(u, u) < 0$ for $c \ne 0$. Next, if $B(v, w) = \alpha\, \ell_1(v)\ell_1(w) + \beta\, \ell_2(v)\ell_2(w)$ with $\alpha, \beta \ne 0$, functionals $\ell_1, \ell_2$ that vanish together only at $0$, and vectors $v_1, v_2$ with $\ell_i(v_j) = \delta_{ij}$, then $B$ is non-degenerate and its index is the number of negative coefficients among $\alpha, \beta$ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · isNondegenerate_and_index_of_diag⟧. If $B(v, \cdot) = 0$, then $0 = B(v, v_1) = \alpha \ell_1(v)$ and $0 = B(v, v_2) = \beta \ell_2(v)$, so $v = 0$. For the index, $B(v, v) = \alpha \ell_1(v)^2 + \beta \ell_2(v)^2$. With both coefficients negative the form is negative definite and its index is the dimension $2$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharAlgebra · index_eq_finrank_of_negDef⟧; with one negative it is $1$ by the previous lemma, tested on $v_1$ and $v_2$; with both positive it is positive semi-definite and its index is $0$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharAlgebra · index_eq_zero_of_nonneg⟧.

**Proposition (the Hessian on the axis).** Let $(a, 0, 0) \in T$ with $a \ne 0$ and $a^2 \ne R^2 + r^2$. Then it is a non-degenerate critical point of $f|_T$, of index $[a > 0] + [a(a^2 - R^2 - r^2) > 0]$, where $[\,\cdot\,]$ is $1$ if the condition holds and $0$ otherwise ⟦Chapter3/Torus · onAxis_spec⟧.

*Proof as formalized.*

1. Put $\kappa = 4a(a^2 - R^2 - r^2) \ne 0$ and $G = f - \kappa^{-1} g$. It agrees with $f$ on $T$, where $g = 0$. Its derivative is $dx_1 - \kappa^{-1} dg$, which vanishes at $P = (a, 0, 0)$ because $dg_P = \kappa\, dx_1$. As $f$ is affine, $d^2G_P = -\kappa^{-1} d^2g_P$. So $P$ is a critical point of $G|_T$.
2. Take a slice chart $\sigma$ at $P$ and let $L$ be the derivative of its parametrization at the point over $P$. Its range is $T_PT$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · tangentSubspace_eq_range_fderiv_sliceParam⟧, which is $\ker dg_P = \{w_1 = 0\}$. It is injective, its range having dimension $2 = \dim \mathbb R^2$. Choose $v_1, v_2$ with $L v_1 = (0, 1, 0)$ and $L v_2 = (0, 0, 1)$.
3. By Lagrange's rule and the formula for $d^2g$ on the axis, for all $v, w$,
$$\operatorname{Hess}(G \circ x)(v, w) = -\frac1a\,(Lv)_2(Lw)_2 \;-\; \frac{a^2 + R^2 - r^2}{a(a^2 - R^2 - r^2)}\,(Lv)_3(Lw)_3 .$$
This is a diagonal form with $\ell_1 = (L\,\cdot)_2$ and $\ell_2 = (L\,\cdot)_3$, which vanish together only at $0$ because $(Lv)_1 = 0$ and $L$ is injective, and which are dual to $v_1, v_2$. The coefficients are nonzero, since $a^2 + R^2 - r^2 > 0$. So the form is non-degenerate, and its index counts the negative coefficients: $-1/a < 0$ iff $a > 0$, and the second is negative iff $a(a^2 - R^2 - r^2) > 0$.
4. Non-degeneracy and index of the Hessian of $G \circ x$ at the point over $P$ are those of the Hessian of $G|_T$ at $P$ (§0.2: the Hessian at a critical point may be read in any parametrization) ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · isNondegenerate_hessian_comp_sliceParam_iff⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · index_hessian_comp_sliceParam⟧, and $G|_T = f|_T$.

At the four points the two diagonal entries are $(1/(R+r), 1/r)$ at $p$, $(1/(R-r), -1/r)$ at $q$, $(-1/(R-r), 1/r)$ at $r$ and $(-1/(R+r), -1/r)$ at $s$: the principal curvatures of the torus there, with signs. Hence $p$, $q$, $r$, $s$ are non-degenerate critical points of indices $0, 1, 1, 2$ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · pointP_spec⟧ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · pointQ_spec⟧ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · pointR_spec⟧ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · pointS_spec⟧, read off from the signs of $a$ and of $a^2 - R^2 - r^2 = \pm 2Rr$. So $f|_T$ is a Morse function ⟦Chapter3/Torus · isMorseFunction_height⟧, with critical values $0, 2r, 2R, 2R + 2r$ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · criticalValues_height⟧ and Morse numbers $C_0 = 1$, $C_1 = 2$, $C_2 = 1$, $C_\lambda = 0$ for $\lambda \ge 3$ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · critCount_height⟧. The count $C_1 = 2$ uses $q \ne r$, whose first coordinates $-(R - r)$ and $R - r$ differ. Milnor's normal forms are the lemma of Morse at these indices; they are not restated.

*Theorem 3.1 on the torus (V3.24).* If $[a, b]$ contains none of the four critical values, a diffeomorphism of $T$ carries $T^a$ onto $T^b$, $T^a$ is a strong deformation retract of $T^b$, and the inclusion is a homotopy equivalence ⟦Chapter3/Torus · theorem_3_1_torus⟧. This is Theorem 3.1 for a compact submanifold (Part II) ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · theorem_3_1_submanifold_of_isCompact⟧, the absence of critical points in $f^{-1}[a, b]$ following from the list of critical points. So the sets $T^a$ with $a$ in one of $(0, 2r)$, $(2r, 2R)$, $(2R, 2R + 2r)$ are diffeomorphic to one another. That they are a disk, a cylinder and a punctured torus, Milnor's (2)–(4), and the attachments of cells of dimensions $0, 1, 1, 2$ are not proved; Milnor's phrase that the homotopy type changes when $a$ crosses a critical value also needs $[a, b]$ to contain no *other* critical value.

### III.7 The sign of the determinant: $\operatorname{sgn}\det A = (-1)^{\operatorname{index}}$

Milnor's §5 reads $\chi(M) = C_0 - C_1 + \dots \pm C_n$ off the homology of the slabs between critical levels; that route is Part IV. In Application 2 of §6 he goes the other way: for a function $f$ the vector field $\operatorname{grad} f$ has index $(-1)^\lambda$ at a critical point of index $\lambda$, and the index sum of a vector field is a topological invariant. The files `Chapter3/EulerChar*` run this argument with the Poincaré–Hopf theorem of the companion project, and so obtain the invariance of $\sum_\lambda (-1)^\lambda C_\lambda$ without homology. The first ingredient, which Milnor uses without comment, is linear algebra.

**Proposition (V3.18).** Let $H$ be a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form on a finite-dimensional real inner product space $V$, and $A$ the operator with $H(v, w) = \langle Av, w\rangle$. Then $\operatorname{sgn}\det A = (-1)^{\operatorname{index} H}$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharAlgebra · sgn_det_eq_neg_one_pow_index⟧ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharAlgebra · sgn_det_eq_neg_one_pow_index_of_symm⟧.

*Proof as formalized.* An operator representing a symmetric form is self-adjoint ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharAlgebra · isSymmetric_of_symm⟧. By the spectral theorem in Mathlib, $A$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors $b_1, \dots, b_n$ with eigenvalues $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_n$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Spectrum · LinearMap.IsSymmetric.eigenvectorBasis⟧.

1. In the coordinates $c_i = \langle b_i, v\rangle$, $\langle Av, v\rangle = \sum_i \mu_i c_i^2$: expand $\langle Av, v\rangle = \sum_i \langle Av, b_i\rangle\langle b_i, v\rangle$ in the orthonormal basis and use $\langle Av, b_i\rangle = \langle v, A b_i\rangle = \mu_i \langle b_i, v\rangle$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharAlgebra · inner_apply_self_eq_sum_eigenvalues⟧. So the coordinate isomorphism $v \mapsto (c_i)$ makes the quadratic form $v \mapsto H(v, v)$ equivalent to the weighted sum of squares $\sum_i \mu_i c_i^2$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharAlgebra · quadForm_equivalent_weightedSumSquares⟧.
2. The index of $H$ is Mathlib's negative index of inertia of that quadratic form (§0.2) ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · index⟧. By the uniqueness half of Sylvester's law of inertia ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/QuadraticForm/Signature · QuadraticForm.sigNeg_of_equiv_weightedSumSquares⟧ it is the number of negative weights: $\operatorname{index} H = \#\{i : \mu_i < 0\}$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharAlgebra · index_eq_ncard_eigenvalues_neg⟧.
3. No eigenvalue vanishes: if $\mu_i = 0$, then $A b_i = 0$, so $H(b_i, w) = \langle A b_i, w\rangle = 0$ for all $w$, and non-degeneracy gives $b_i = 0$, which is impossible for a unit vector ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharAlgebra · eigenvalues_ne_zero⟧.
4. $\det A = \prod_i \mu_i$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Spectrum · det_eq_prod_eigenvalues⟧. The sign of a product is the product of the signs ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharAlgebra · sgn_prod⟧, and a product of signs of nonzero numbers is $(-1)$ raised to the number of negative factors ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharAlgebra · prod_sgn_eq_neg_one_pow⟧.

Two remarks. First, the right-hand side $(-1)^{\operatorname{index} H}$ is defined from $H$ alone; so the sign of $\det A$ is the same for the operators representing $H$ with respect to any two inner products. This is what makes the argument work on a submanifold, where the operator lives on a tangent space with the induced inner product, while Milnor's Hessian is read in a chart, where that inner product is the first fundamental form. Second, $H$ is non-degenerate exactly when $A$ is bijective ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharAlgebra · isNondegenerate_iff_bijective⟧: the kernel of $A$ lies in the null space of $H$, and an injective endomorphism of a finite-dimensional space is onto; conversely, if $H(v, \cdot) = 0$, then $\|Av\|^2 = H(v, Av) = 0$. Two facts about definite forms are recorded for later use: a negative definite form has index the dimension, and a positive semi-definite form has index $0$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharAlgebra · index_eq_finrank_of_negDef⟧ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharAlgebra · index_eq_zero_of_nonneg⟧.

### III.8 The tangential gradient and its index

From here to §III.10, $N \subseteq \mathbb R^{K+1}$ is a compact nonempty slice submanifold modelled on $\mathbb R^{d+1}$, and $F : \mathbb R^{K+1} \to \mathbb R$ is smooth with $f = F|_N$. Milnor's sentence that the index of $\operatorname{grad} f$ at a critical point of index $\lambda$ is $(-1)^\lambda$ hides four points: which metric defines the gradient; that the Hessian of $F|_N$ is not the restriction of the Hessian of $F$; that the derivative of a vector field at a zero is an endomorphism of the tangent space; and the linear algebra of §III.7.

*The metric and the field.* The metric is the one induced from $\mathbb R^{K+1}$. The **tangential gradient** is $v(q) = P_q \nabla F(q)$, with $P_q$ the orthogonal projection onto $T_qN$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · tangentialGradient⟧. It is tangent ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · tangentialGradient_mem⟧, and it is the gradient of $f$ for the induced metric: $\langle v(q), t\rangle = dF_q(t)$ for $t \in T_qN$, because $P_q$ is self-adjoint and the identity on $T_qN$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · inner_tangentialGradient⟧. Its zeros are exactly the critical points of $f$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · tangentialGradient_eq_zero_iff⟧: $q$ is critical iff $dF_q$ vanishes on $T_qN$, iff $\nabla F(q) \perp T_qN$, iff $P_q \nabla F(q) = 0$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · isCriticalPointM_comp_val_iff_gradient_mem_orthogonal⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · isCriticalPointM_comp_val_iff_starProjection_gradient_eq_zero⟧.

*The device: $F \circ r$.* Let $r$ be the nearest-point retraction of the tubular neighbourhood $N_\varepsilon$ of $N$, which the companion project constructs (its $\varepsilon$-neighbourhood theorem) ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/Tubular3 · tubular⟧ ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/Tubular4 · tubr⟧ ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/Tubular4 · Nε⟧, read as a map of $\mathbb R^{K+1}$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · tubRet⟧. It is the identity on $N$ and smooth on the open set $N_\varepsilon \supseteq N$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · tubRet_of_mem⟧ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · contDiffAt_tubRet⟧. Its derivative $dr_q$ at $q \in N$ is the orthogonal projection $P_q$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · dret_eq_starProjection⟧: $dr_q$ takes values in $T_qN$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/ChartIndex · dret_mem_tanN⟧, it is idempotent ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/ChartIndex · dret_dret⟧, and its kernel is $(T_qN)^\perp$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/ChartIndex · ker_dret⟧. So $w - dr_q w$ lies in the kernel, hence is orthogonal to $T_qN$, and that characterizes the orthogonal projection. Now $F \circ r$ agrees with $F$ on $N$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · comp_tubRet_comp_val⟧, its derivative at $q \in N$ is $dF_q \circ P_q$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · fderiv_comp_tubRet⟧, and therefore:

- $v(q) = \nabla(F \circ r)(q)$ for $q \in N$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · tangentialGradient_eq_gradient⟧: for every $w$, $\langle \nabla(F \circ r)(q), w\rangle = dF_q(P_q w) = \langle \nabla F(q), P_q w\rangle = \langle P_q \nabla F(q), w\rangle$;
- at a critical point $q$ of $f$, $d(F \circ r)_q = dF_q \circ P_q = 0$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · fderiv_comp_tubRet_eq_zero⟧: $q$ is a critical point of the *ambient* function $F \circ r$, while $\nabla F(q)$ itself is normal to $N$ and need not vanish.

The first item makes the field smooth: $v$ is the restriction to $N$ of $\nabla(F \circ r)$, which is smooth on $N_\varepsilon$, the gradient of a smooth function being smooth ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · contDiffAt_gradient⟧. This is the smoothness required by the Poincaré–Hopf theorem ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · contMDiff_tangentialGradient⟧ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · isTangent_tangentialGradient⟧ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · tangentialGradient_mem_range⟧. The second makes the Hessian computable.

*The derivative of the field.* The companion project reads the derivative of a tangent field $v$ at $q$ as the derivative $dv_q$ of $x \mapsto v(r(x))$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/ChartIndex · dvr⟧. For the tangential gradient, $v \circ r = \nabla(F \circ r) \circ r$, so $dv_q = d(\nabla(F \circ r))_q \circ dr_q$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · dvr_tangentialGradient⟧. The derivative of a gradient is the Hessian: $\langle d(\nabla h)_x t, s\rangle = d^2h_x(t, s)$, since $\nabla h$ is the inverse Riesz map applied to $dh$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · inner_fderiv_gradient⟧. Let $\mathrm{hess}_q$ be the second derivative of $F \circ r$ at $q$ restricted to $T_qN \times T_qN$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · hessT⟧; it is symmetric ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · hessT_symm⟧. As $dr_q$ is the identity on $T_qN$,
$$\mathrm{hess}_q(t, s) = \langle dv_q\, t, s\rangle \qquad (t, s \in T_qN)$$
⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · hessT_apply_eq_inner_dvr⟧. At a zero of $v$, Milnor's Lemma 5 of the companion book makes $dv_q$ map $T_qN$ into itself ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/ChartIndex · dvr_mem_tanN⟧, so it restricts to an endomorphism $dv_q^T$ of $T_qN$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/ChartIndex · dvT⟧, and $dv_q^T$ is the self-adjoint operator of $\mathrm{hess}_q$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · inner_dvT_tangentialGradient⟧.

*The Hessian of $f$.* Let $\phi$ be the parametrization of $N$ by the slice chart that the companion project chooses at $q$, and $z_0$ the point over $q$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/Tubular1 · φ⟧ ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/Tubular3 · z₀⟧. Its derivative is an isomorphism $\mathbb R^{d+1} \cong T_qN$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · tanN_eq_range⟧ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · tanEquiv⟧. At a critical point of $f$, the Hessian of $F \circ \phi$ at $z_0$ is $\mathrm{hess}_q$ pulled back by this isomorphism ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · hessian_comp_φ_eq⟧. Indeed $F \circ \phi = (F \circ r) \circ \phi$ near $z_0$, because $\phi$ takes values in $N$, where $r$ is the identity; so the two Hessians coincide ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · hessian_congr⟧. And $q$ is a critical point of $F \circ r$, so the second-order term of the chain rule vanishes ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · hessian_comp⟧. Milnor's Hessian $f_{**}$ at $q$ may be computed in this parametrization ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · indexM_comp_val_eq_tub⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · isNondegenerate_hessian_comp_sliceParam_iff⟧, and index and non-degeneracy are invariant under a linear change of variables ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · index_bilinearComp⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · isNondegenerate_bilinearComp_iff⟧. Hence the index of $\mathrm{hess}_q$ is the index of $f$ at $q$, and $\mathrm{hess}_q$ is non-degenerate iff $q$ is ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · index_hessT⟧ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · isNondegenerate_hessT_iff⟧.

**Theorem (V3.19, the index of the gradient field).** Let $K \ge 1$ and let $q$ be a non-degenerate critical point of $f = F|_N$ of index $\lambda$. Then $dv_q^T$ is invertible with $\operatorname{sgn}\det dv_q^T = (-1)^\lambda$, and the tangential gradient has index $(-1)^\lambda$ at $q$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · bijective_dvT_tangentialGradient⟧ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharGradient · indexN_tangentialGradient⟧.

*Proof as formalized.* $\mathrm{hess}_q(t, s) = \langle dv_q^T t, s\rangle$ with $\mathrm{hess}_q$ symmetric and non-degenerate, so $dv_q^T$ is bijective and $\operatorname{sgn}\det dv_q^T = (-1)^{\operatorname{index} \mathrm{hess}_q} = (-1)^\lambda$ (§III.7). The index of a tangent field at a zero is defined in the companion project through the extension $x \mapsto v(r(x)) + (x - r(x))$ to the tubular neighbourhood ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/PoincareHopf · indexN⟧. At a non-degenerate zero it equals the sign of the determinant of $dv_q^T$ on the tangent space ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/ChartIndex · indexN_eq_sgn_det⟧ (Milnor's Lemma 4 of the companion book, together with the fact that the extension has derivative $dv_q^T$ on $T_qN$ and the identity on the normal space ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/ChartIndex · det_fderiv_w₀⟧). This is where $K \ge 1$ enters: the companion project computes indices of non-degenerate zeros only in ambient dimension at least $2$.

### III.9 $\sum_\lambda (-1)^\lambda C_\lambda$ is the Poincaré–Hopf integer

*Morse numbers (V3.16).* For a function $f$ on a manifold modelled on $E$, $C_\lambda(f)$ is the number of critical points of index $\lambda$, set to $0$ if there are infinitely many ⟦Chapter3/EulerChar · critCount⟧. If $f$ has finitely many critical points, grouping them by index gives
$$\sum_{p \text{ critical}} (-1)^{\operatorname{index}_p f} = \sum_{\lambda = 0}^{\dim E} (-1)^\lambda C_\lambda(f),$$
the index never exceeding $\dim E$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerChar · finsum_neg_one_pow_indexM_eq_sum_critCount⟧. A Morse function on a compact manifold has finitely many critical points (Corollary 2.3 with compactness; Part I) ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · IsMorseFunction.finite_setOf_isCriticalPointM⟧, so Milnor's word *isolated* in the hypothesis on p. 29 is redundant.

*Euler numbers (V3.17).* An integer $\chi$ is an **Euler number** of $N$ if for every smooth tangent field $v$ on $N$ whose zeros are finitely many distinct points $z_1, \dots, z_j$, the indices add up to $\chi$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerChar · IsEulerNumber⟧. For $K \ge 1$ one exists: this is the Poincaré–Hopf theorem of the companion project, where $\chi$ is the degree of the Gauss map of the boundary of a tubular neighbourhood ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/PoincareHopf · poincare_hopf⟧ ⟦Chapter3/EulerChar · exists_isEulerNumber⟧. It plays the part of Milnor's appeal to the topological invariance of the index sum, which he quotes from Steenrod.

**Theorem (V3.20, formula (3) without homology).** Let $K \ge 1$ and $\chi$ be an Euler number of $N$. For every Morse function $f$ on $N$,
$$\sum_{q \text{ critical}} (-1)^{\operatorname{index}_q f} = \sum_{\lambda=0}^{d+1} (-1)^\lambda C_\lambda(f) = \chi$$
⟦Chapter3/EulerChar · IsEulerNumber.finsum_eq⟧ ⟦Chapter3/EulerChar · IsEulerNumber.sum_critCount_eq⟧.

*Proof as formalized.* First for $f = F|_N$ with $F$ smooth on $\mathbb R^{K+1}$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerChar · IsEulerNumber.finsum_eq_of_ambient⟧. Then $f$ is a Morse function, and its critical set is finite; enumerate it injectively as $z_1, \dots, z_j$. The tangential gradient of $F$ is a smooth tangent field whose zeros are exactly the critical points (§III.8), that is, exactly the $z_i$. By the definition of an Euler number its index sum is $\chi$; by the theorem of §III.8 its index at $z_i$ is $(-1)^{\operatorname{index}_{z_i} f}$. Rewriting the sum over the critical set as the sum over the enumeration gives the first equality. A general smooth $f$ on $N$ is the restriction of a smooth function on $\mathbb R^{K+1}$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerChar · exists_contDiff_comp_val_eq⟧: the function $f \circ r$ is smooth on the tubular neighbourhood $N_\varepsilon$, the retraction being smooth there as a map into the manifold $N$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Corestrict · contMDiff_codRestrict⟧, and a cut-off provides a smooth function on all of $\mathbb R^{K+1}$ that agrees with $f \circ r$ near a closed tube around $N$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/Extend · exists_extension⟧; on $N$, $r$ is the identity. The second equality is the regrouping by index, with $\dim N = d + 1$.

Consequently there is an integer which is simultaneously the index sum of every tangent field and the alternating count of every Morse function ⟦Chapter3/EulerChar · exists_eulerNumber_eq_sum_critCount⟧; the alternating count is the same for all Morse functions on $N$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerChar · sum_critCount_eq_of_isMorseFunction⟧; and once a Morse function exists (Corollary 6.7, §III.16) every Euler number is determined by it ⟦Chapter3/EulerChar · IsEulerNumber.eq_sum_critCount⟧, so the Euler number is unique. Below it is written $\chi(N)$. Its identification with $\sum_i (-1)^i \dim H_i(N; \Bbbk)$ over any field $\Bbbk$ is in Part IV ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyManifold · IsEulerNumber.eq_sum_bettiNumber⟧.

Three readings of Milnor are fixed here. His function in Application 2, said to be any differentiable function, must be a Morse function, since otherwise neither $C_\lambda$ nor the index $(-1)^\lambda$ makes sense; the critical points of the vector field are its zeros; and the last term $\pm C_n$ of the alternating sum takes $n = \dim M$. The vector-field argument does not order the critical points, so distinct critical values, which Milnor's slab argument uses, are not needed. Dimension $0$ is excluded here, because the companion project computes indices of non-degenerate zeros only in positive dimension; there every point is critical of index $0$ and the alternating count is the number of points (§III.21).

### III.10 Two checks: the sphere and the torus

*The sphere (V3.21).* Let $S^n \subseteq \mathbb R^{n+1}$, $n = d + 1 \ge 1$, be the unit sphere, as the slice submanifold of the companion project ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/EulerSphere · sphSub⟧. The companion project computes its index sum to be $1 + (-1)^n$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/EulerSphere · indexSum_sph⟧, so $1 + (-1)^n$ is an Euler number of $S^n$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharSphere · isEulerNumber_sph⟧, and every Morse function on $S^n$ has $\sum_\lambda (-1)^\lambda C_\lambda = 1 + (-1)^n$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharSphere · sum_critCount_sph⟧.

The height function $h(x) = \langle e, x\rangle$, $e$ a unit vector ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharSphere · heightFun⟧, checks this directly. Its gradient is $e$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharSphere · gradient_heightFun⟧, and its tangential gradient is the field $e - \langle e, x\rangle x$ that the companion book uses for the sphere: $e - (e - \langle e, q\rangle q) = \langle e, q\rangle q$ is orthogonal to $T_qS^n = q^\perp$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharSphere · tangentialGradient_heightFun⟧. Its zeros, hence the critical points of $h$, are $\pm e$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharSphere · isCriticalPointM_heightFun_iff⟧. The derivative of that field on the tangent space is $t \mapsto -(\langle e, q\rangle t + \langle e, t\rangle q)$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/EulerSphere · dvr_gradSph_apply⟧. Taking the inner product with $s \perp q$ gives, by §III.8, $\mathrm{hess}_q(t, s) = -\langle e, q\rangle\langle t, s\rangle$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharSphere · hessT_heightFun⟧, non-degenerate wherever $\langle e, q\rangle \ne 0$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharSphere · isNondegenerate_hessT_heightFun⟧. So $h$ is a Morse function ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharSphere · isMorseFunction_heightFun⟧. At $e$ the form is $-\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$, negative definite on the $n$-dimensional tangent space, so the index is $n$; at $-e$ it is $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$, and the index is $0$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharSphere · indexM_heightFun_pos⟧ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharSphere · indexM_heightFun_neg⟧. Hence $C_0 = C_n = 1$ and all other $C_\lambda = 0$, the points $e \ne -e$ being distinct ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharSphere · critCount_heightFun⟧, so $\sum_\lambda (-1)^\lambda C_\lambda = 1 + (-1)^n$ directly ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharSphere · sum_critCount_heightFun⟧. Conversely, every Euler number of $S^n$ equals this count (§III.9), which recomputes the companion project's value by Morse theory ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharSphere · IsEulerNumber.eq_sph⟧. The height function also satisfies the hypotheses of Reeb's theorem, and its indices $0$ and $n$ illustrate §III.1.

*The torus (V3.25).* By §III.6 and §III.9, $C_0 - C_1 + C_2 = 1 - 2 + 1 = 0$ is an Euler number of $T$, every Euler number of $T$ is $0$, and every Morse function on $T$ has $C_0 - C_1 + C_2 = 0$ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · isEulerNumber_torus⟧ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · IsEulerNumber.eq_torus⟧ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · sum_critCount_torus⟧. An independent check avoids Morse theory: the rotation field $(-x_2, x_1, 0)$ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · Torus.rotField⟧ is smooth and tangent to $T$, since $dg$ vanishes on it, and it has no zeros on $T$, since $x_1 = x_2 = 0$ would give $R^2 + x_3^2 = r^2 < R^2$. Its index sum is the empty sum, so every Euler number of $T$ is $0$ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · IsEulerNumber.eq_torus_of_rotation⟧.

### III.11 Sard's theorem on a manifold (Theorem 6.1)

Milnor quotes Theorem 6.1 for $C^1$ maps between manifolds of the same dimension with countable bases, refers to de Rham, and uses it once, for a smooth map into $\mathbb R^n$ (Corollary 6.2). The formal theorem treats smooth maps into a vector space, of any dimensions. Write $\operatorname{crit}(f)$ for the set of points where the manifold derivative of $f$ is not surjective (§0.1) ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Brown · critPoints⟧.

**Theorem 6.1 (V4.3).** Let $M$ be a second countable smooth manifold without boundary, modelled on a finite-dimensional real space $E$; $F$ a finite-dimensional real normed space with a Haar measure $\mu$; $U \subseteq M$ open; and $f : M \to F$ smooth on $U$. Then $\mu(f(U \cap \operatorname{crit}(f))) = 0$. In particular, for $f$ smooth on $M$ the set of critical values is $\mu$-null ⟦Chapter4/Sard · measure_image_inter_critPoints_eq_zero⟧ ⟦Chapter4/Sard · measure_image_critPoints_eq_zero⟧.

($E$ and $F$ are required to lie in the same universe of the type theory, as in the companion project; this has no mathematical content.)

*Proof as formalized.*

1. *Countably many charts.* By second countability, countably many chart domains, those of the charts at points $x_0$ of a countable set, cover $U$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Bases · TopologicalSpace.countable_cover_nhdsWithin⟧.
2. *Sard in a chart.* For a chart $\varphi$ at $x_0$, the set $W$ of points of its target whose image under $\varphi^{-1}$ lies in $U$ is open, and $f \circ \varphi^{-1}$ is smooth on it. The Euclidean Sard theorem of the companion project ⟦Milnor/Chapter3/Sard · sard⟧ makes the image of its critical set in $W$ null.
3. *Critical points go to critical points.* If $x \in U$ lies in the domain of $\varphi$ and $df_x$ is not onto, then $\varphi(x)$ is a critical point of $f \circ \varphi^{-1}$ in $W$ ⟦Chapter4/Sard · mem_critSet_of_mem_critPoints_of_mdifferentiableAt⟧: by the chain rule, $d(f \circ \varphi^{-1})$ at $\varphi(x)$ is $df_x \circ d\varphi^{-1}$, and if this composite were onto, $df_x$ would be onto.
4. So $f(U \cap \operatorname{crit}(f))$ lies in a countable union of null sets, which is null.

The proof is that of A. B. Brown's theorem in the companion project, which records only the density of regular values. Only $\mathbb R^n$ is used as a target in §6, and there all Haar measures, Lebesgue measure among them, have the same null sets.

### III.12 The normal bundle, the endpoint map and Milnor's coordinates

From here to §III.18, $M \subseteq \mathbb R^n$ is a slice submanifold modelled on $\mathbb R^k$ with complement $\mathbb R^w$. It need not be closed or compact, and $k = n$ is allowed. If $M$ is nonempty, then $k + w = n$, because the derivative of a slice chart at a point of $M$ is an isomorphism $\mathbb R^n \cong \mathbb R^k \times \mathbb R^w$ ⟦Chapter4/Focal · slice_dim_add_eq_of_nonempty⟧; for empty $M$ nothing constrains $k$ and $w$, so the formal statements carry $k + w = n$ as a hypothesis where it is used.

*The normal bundle and the endpoint map (V4.1).* The normal bundle is the set
$$N = \{(q, v) \in M \times \mathbb R^n : v \perp T_qM\}$$
⟦Milnor/Chapter8/NormalBundle · normalBundleSet⟧ ⟦Chapter4/NormalBundle · mem_normalBundleSet_iff⟧. When $k + w = n$ it is a slice submanifold of $\mathbb R^n \times \mathbb R^n$ of dimension $n$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter8/NormalBundle · isSliceSubmanifold_normalBundle⟧; Milnor calls this not difficult, and it is Problem 11 of the companion book, formalized there. Its proof is the source of everything below. Near $p \in M$ let $\sigma$ be the slice chart chosen at $p$, $c(a) = (\sigma a)_1$ the first slice coordinate, $x$ the parametrization $u \mapsto \sigma^{-1}(u, 0)$, and $B(a) = (dx_{c(a)})^\dagger$ the adjoint of its derivative at $c(a)$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter8/NormalBundle · NormalAux.B⟧. On the chart domain, $N$ is the zero set of
$$\Theta(a, v) = \big((\sigma a)_2,\; B(a)\, v\big)$$
⟦Milnor/Chapter8/NormalBundle · NormalAux.g⟧ ⟦Milnor/Chapter8/NormalBundle · NormalAux.mem_normalBundleSet_iff⟧: the first component cuts out $M$, and as $T_aM = \operatorname{range} dx_{c(a)}$, $v \perp T_aM$ iff $B(a) v = 0$. The derivative $d\Theta_{(a,v)}(u, h) = ((d\sigma\, u)_2,\ B(a) h + (dB_a u) v)$ is onto ⟦Milnor/Chapter8/NormalBundle · NormalAux.fderiv_g_apply⟧ ⟦Milnor/Chapter8/NormalBundle · NormalAux.surjective_fderiv_g⟧, and a local form of Lemma 1 gives the slice charts ⟦Milnor/Chapter8/LocalSlice · IsSliceSubmanifold.of_locally_preimage⟧. Milnor's endpoint map is $E(q, v) = q + v$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter8/NormalBundle · normalSum⟧. The formal text also uses the generalized endpoint map $E_\Gamma(q, v) = \Gamma(q) + v$ for $\Gamma : \mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R^n$ ⟦Chapter4/NormalBundle · normalEnd⟧, with $E_{\mathrm{id}} = E$ ⟦Chapter4/NormalBundle · normalEnd_id⟧; $\Gamma = -\nabla F$ serves Corollary 6.8 (§III.17). $E_\Gamma$ is smooth over the open set where $\Gamma$ is smooth ⟦Chapter4/NormalBundle · contMDiffOn_normalEnd⟧.

*Milnor's coordinates and the fundamental forms (V4.5).* For $q \in M$, Milnor's coordinates $u \mapsto x(u)$ are the inverse of the slice chart chosen at $q$, with $x(u_q) = q$ ⟦Chapter4/NormalBundle · xParam⟧ ⟦Chapter4/NormalBundle · uParam⟧ ⟦Chapter4/NormalBundle · xParam_uParam⟧. Write $dx$ and $d^2x$ for its first and second derivatives at $u_q$, Milnor's vectors $\partial x/\partial u^i$ and $\partial^2 x/\partial u^i\partial u^j$ ⟦Chapter4/NormalBundle · dParam⟧ ⟦Chapter4/NormalBundle · d2Param⟧. The map $x$ is smooth near $u_q$ ⟦Chapter4/NormalBundle · contDiffAt_xParam⟧, and $dx$ is injective ⟦Chapter4/NormalBundle · injective_dParam⟧. Since $\sigma \circ x = (u \mapsto (u, 0))$ near $u_q$, $d\sigma_q \circ dx = (\xi \mapsto (\xi, 0))$ ⟦Chapter4/NormalBundle · fderiv_σφ_comp_dParam⟧, and $d\sigma_q$ is injective ⟦Chapter4/NormalBundle · injective_fderiv_σφ⟧. The **first fundamental form** is $g(\xi, \eta) = \langle dx\,\xi, dx\,\eta\rangle$ on $\mathbb R^k$ ⟦Chapter4/NormalBundle · firstFF⟧; the **second fundamental form in the direction $v$** is $\ell_v(\xi, \eta) = \langle v, d^2x(\xi, \eta)\rangle$ ⟦Chapter4/NormalBundle · secondFF⟧, linear in $v$ ⟦Chapter4/NormalBundle · secondFF_smul⟧. For $v$ normal at $q$ this is Milnor's $v \cdot \ell_{ij}$: the tangential part of $d^2x$ is orthogonal to $v$. For a linear map $T$ of $\mathbb R^n$, put
$$B_{T,v}(\xi, \eta) = \langle T\, dx\,\xi, dx\,\eta\rangle - \ell_v(\xi, \eta)$$
⟦Chapter4/NormalBundle · normalForm⟧; for $T = \mathrm{id}$ it is $g - \ell_v$, Milnor's matrix $(g_{ij} - v \cdot \ell_{ij})$ ⟦Chapter4/NormalBundle · normalForm_id⟧.

**Proposition (V4.8, the tangent space of $N$).** Assume $k + w = n$ and $(q, v) \in N$. A pair $(u, h) \in \mathbb R^n \times \mathbb R^n$ is tangent to $N$ at $(q, v)$ if and only if $u = dx\,\xi$ for some $\xi \in \mathbb R^k$ and
$$\langle h, dx\,\eta\rangle + \langle v, d^2x(\xi, \eta)\rangle = 0 \quad \text{for every } \eta \in \mathbb R^k$$
⟦Chapter4/NormalBundle · mem_range_mfd_val_normalBundle_iff⟧.

*Proof as formalized.* By Lemma 2 for a local defining function, the tangent space of $N$ at $(q, v)$ is the kernel of $d\Theta$ there ⟦Milnor/Chapter8/LocalSlice · IsSliceSubmanifold.range_mfderiv_val_eq_ker⟧, the dimensions matching since $n + (w + k) = 2n$ ⟦Chapter4/NormalBundle · finrank_normalBundle_add⟧. So $(u, h)$ is tangent iff $(d\sigma\, u)_2 = 0$ and $B(q) h + (dB_q u) v = 0$. The first condition says $d\sigma\, u = (\xi, 0)$ for $\xi = (d\sigma\, u)_1$, that is, $u = dx\,\xi$, by $d\sigma \circ dx = (\cdot, 0)$ and the injectivity of $d\sigma$. For the second, $\langle B(q) h, \eta\rangle = \langle h, dx\,\eta\rangle$ by the definition of the adjoint ⟦Chapter4/NormalBundle · B_self_inner⟧. The derivative of $a \mapsto B(a)$ at $q$ is $u \mapsto (d^2x((d\sigma\, u)_1, \cdot))^\dagger$ ⟦Chapter4/NormalBundle · hasFDerivAt_B⟧, so $\langle (dB_q u) v, \eta\rangle = \langle v, d^2x(\xi, \eta)\rangle$. A vector of $\mathbb R^k$ vanishes iff its inner products with all $\eta$ do. The second condition is the derivative of Milnor's identity $w_\alpha \cdot \partial x/\partial u^j = 0$, here without the normal frame $w_\alpha$.

**Proposition (V4.8, the kernel of $dE_\Gamma$).** Assume $k + w = n$, $(q, v) \in N$, and $\Gamma$ differentiable at $q$. The derivative of $E_\Gamma$ at $(q, v)$ is $(u, h) \mapsto d\Gamma_q(u) + h$ on the tangent space ⟦Chapter4/NormalBundle · hasMFDerivAt_normalEnd⟧, and the dimension of its kernel is the nullity of $B_{d\Gamma_q, v}$ ⟦Chapter4/NormalBundle · finrank_ker_mfd_normalEnd⟧.

*Proof as formalized.* $E_\Gamma$ is the map $(y, z) \mapsto \Gamma(y) + z$ of $\mathbb R^n \times \mathbb R^n$ composed with the inclusion of $N$, so by the chain rule its derivative is $S \circ L$, with $S(u, h) = d\Gamma_q(u) + h$ and $L$ the injective derivative of the inclusion ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/LevelSet · injective_mfderiv_val⟧. The kernel of $S \circ L$ is carried by $L$ isomorphically onto $\operatorname{range} L \cap \ker S$. By the previous proposition this is the image of the null space of $B_{d\Gamma_q, v}$ under $\iota(\xi) = (dx\,\xi, -d\Gamma_q\, dx\,\xi)$. Indeed a tangent pair with $d\Gamma_q(u) + h = 0$ has $u = dx\,\xi$ and $h = -d\Gamma_q\, dx\,\xi$, and the tangency condition then reads $-\langle d\Gamma_q\, dx\,\xi, dx\,\eta\rangle + \langle v, d^2x(\xi, \eta)\rangle = 0$ for all $\eta$, that is, $\xi$ is in the null space; and conversely. As $\iota$ is injective, its first component $dx$ being injective, the dimensions agree.

For $\Gamma = \mathrm{id}$ this is Milnor's computation of pp. 34–35. He chooses orthonormal normal fields $w_\alpha(u)$, coordinates $(u, t) \mapsto (x(u), \sum_\alpha t^\alpha w_\alpha(u))$ on $N$, and a block triangular $n \times n$ matrix whose upper left block is $(g_{ij} - \sum_\alpha t^\alpha w_\alpha \cdot \ell_{ij})$. Two things in that argument need correction. The fields $w_\alpha$ exist only on a coordinate patch (Gram–Schmidt applied to a completion of the $\partial x/\partial u^i$), and Milnor does not say why they exist. And his sentence that the rank of the matrix equals the rank of the upper left block does not hold as written: the ranks differ by $n - k$; it is the *nullities* that agree, and that is what is used.

### III.13 Focal points and Corollary 6.2

Assume $k + w = n$ (V4.2). The **nullity of $E$** at $x \in N$ is the dimension of the kernel of the derivative of $E$ at $x$, the tangent space of $N$ being identified with $\mathbb R^n$ by the chart of $N$ at $x$ ⟦Chapter4/Focal · endpointNullity⟧. Another chart changes the derivative by an isomorphism of $\mathbb R^n$, which preserves the dimension of the kernel. Following Milnor, $e \in \mathbb R^n$ is a **focal point of $(M, q)$ with multiplicity $\mu$** if $(q, e - q) \in N$ and the nullity of $E$ at $(q, e - q)$ is $\mu > 0$ ⟦Chapter4/Focal · IsFocalPointMult⟧; a focal point of $(M, q)$ if this holds for some $\mu$ ⟦Chapter4/Focal · IsFocalPoint⟧; and a focal point of $M$ if it is a focal point of $(M, q)$ for some $q \in M$ ⟦Chapter4/Focal · focalSet⟧. Given $q$ and $e$, the normal vector $e - q$ is determined.

*Focal points are critical values of $E$.* The nullity of $E$ at $x$ is positive iff $x$ is a critical point of $E$ ⟦Chapter4/Focal · endpointNullity_pos_iff⟧: $N$ and $\mathbb R^n$ have the same dimension, so for the derivative not injective, not surjective and singular are the same. Hence $e$ is a focal point of $(M, q)$ iff $(q, e - q) \in N$ is a critical point of $E$ ⟦Chapter4/Focal · isFocalPoint_iff⟧, and the set of focal points of $M$ is exactly the set of critical values of $E$ ⟦Chapter4/Focal · focalSet_eq_image_critPoints⟧. For the inclusion of the critical values, a critical point $x = (q, v)$ has foot point $q$ and $E(x) - q = v$.

**Corollary 6.2 (V4.4).** If $k + w = n$, the set of focal points of $M$ is null for every Haar measure on $\mathbb R^n$ ⟦Chapter4/Focal · measure_focalSet_eq_zero⟧.

*Proof as formalized.* It is the set of critical values of the smooth map $E : N \to \mathbb R^n$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter8/NormalBundle · contMDiff_normalSum⟧, and $N$, a subset of $\mathbb R^{2n}$, is second countable. Theorem 6.1 applies.

### III.14 Assertion 6.4, the shape operator and Lemma 6.3

**Assertion 6.4 (V4.9).** Assume $k + w = n$. The nullity of $E$ at $(q, v) \in N$ is the nullity of $g - \ell_v$ ⟦Chapter4/Focal · endpointNullity_eq_nullity⟧. Hence, for $q \in M$, $v \perp T_qM$, $t \in \mathbb R$ and $\mu \in \mathbb N$: $q + tv$ is a focal point of $(M, q)$ with multiplicity $\mu$ iff $g - t\ell_v$ has nullity $\mu > 0$ ⟦Chapter4/Focal · isFocalPointMult_add_smul_iff⟧.

*Proof as formalized.* The first statement is §III.12 with $\Gamma = \mathrm{id}$, whose derivative is the identity. For the second, $(q, tv) \in N$, the normal vectors forming a subspace, and $\ell_{tv} = t\ell_v$. Milnor takes $v$ a unit vector; that is not needed.

In particular **$q$ is never a focal point of $(M, q)$** ⟦Chapter4/Focal · not_isFocalPoint_self⟧: for $e = q$ the normal vector is $0$ and the form is $g$, positive definite because $g(\xi, \xi) = \|dx\,\xi\|^2$ and $dx$ is injective, so its nullity is $0$. Milnor leaves this case, $t = 0$, implicit; his passage from the singularity of $(g_{ij} - t\, v \cdot \ell_{ij})$ to the eigenvalue $1/t$ divides by $t$.

*The shape operator (V4.6).* Milnor defines the principal curvatures in coordinates with $g_{ij} = \delta_{ij}$ at $q$, without saying why such coordinates exist or why the eigenvalues do not depend on them. The formal text works on the tangent space instead. Let $T_qM = \operatorname{range}(dx) \subseteq \mathbb R^n$ ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · tanSpace⟧, an inner product space with the inner product of $\mathbb R^n$, of dimension $k$ ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · finrank_tanSpace⟧. The map $dx$ is a linear isomorphism $\mathbb R^k \cong T_qM$ ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · dParamEquiv⟧, and an isometry from $(\mathbb R^k, g)$ by the definition of $g$ ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · inner_dParamEquiv⟧. Transport $\ell_v$ to $T_qM$ by $dx^{-1}$ ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · secondFFTan⟧ and let the **shape operator** $S_v$ be the endomorphism of $T_qM$ that represents it, $\langle S_v a, b\rangle = \ell_v(dx^{-1} a, dx^{-1} b)$ ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · shapeOp⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Dual · InnerProductSpace.continuousLinearMapOfBilin⟧ ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · inner_shapeOp_dParamEquiv⟧. It is self-adjoint ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · isSymmetric_shapeOp⟧, because $d^2x$ is symmetric, by the symmetry of second derivatives of a $C^2$ map ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · d2Param_symm⟧. The **principal curvatures** of $M$ at $q$ in the direction $v$ are its $k$ eigenvalues, with multiplicity, in decreasing order ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · principalCurvatures⟧, and the multiplicity of $K$, the number of indices $i$ with $\kappa_i = K$, is the dimension of the eigenspace of $K$ ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · finrank_eigenspace_shapeOp⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Spectrum · LinearMap.IsSymmetric.card_filter_eigenvalues_eq⟧. In coordinates with $g = I$ at $q$ these are Milnor's $K_i$; in general they are the eigenvalues of $g^{-1}(v \cdot \ell)$. That $S_v$ does not depend on the parametrization is not proved, and nothing uses it. The scan lists the curvatures as $K_1, \dots, K_n$; there are $k$ of them.

*Null spaces are eigenspaces.* For every $t$, $\xi$ lies in the null space of $g - t\ell_v$ iff $(1 - tS_v)(dx\,\xi) = 0$ ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · mem_nullSpace_firstFF_sub_iff⟧: for every $\eta$, $\langle dx\,\xi - tS_v\,dx\,\xi,\ dx\,\eta\rangle = (g - t\ell_v)(\xi, \eta)$, and $dx$ is onto $T_qM$. So for $t \ne 0$, $dx$ maps the null space of $g - t\ell_v$ onto the eigenspace of $S_v$ for the eigenvalue $1/t$ ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · map_nullSpace_firstFF_sub_eq_eigenspace⟧, and the nullity is the dimension of that eigenspace ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · nullity_firstFF_sub_smul_secondFF⟧; for $t = 0$ the nullity is $0$ ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · nullity_firstFF_sub_zero_smul_secondFF⟧. This replaces Milnor's normalized coordinates. It also settles a point of language: the multiplicity of an eigenvalue of a symmetric matrix as a root of the characteristic polynomial equals the dimension of its eigenspace, and the latter is what the argument produces.

*Multiplicity as a number.* Let $\operatorname{mult}_q(e)$ be the nullity of $E$ at $(q, e - q)$ if this point lies in $N$, and $0$ otherwise ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · focalMult⟧. Then $e$ is a focal point of $(M, q)$ with multiplicity $\mu$ iff $\operatorname{mult}_q(e) = \mu > 0$, and a focal point iff $\operatorname{mult}_q(e) > 0$ ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · isFocalPointMult_iff_focalMult⟧ ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · isFocalPoint_iff_focalMult_pos⟧.

**Lemma 6.3 (V4.7).** Assume $k + w = n$, $q \in M$ and $v \perp T_qM$. Then $\operatorname{mult}_q(q + tv)$ is $0$ for $t = 0$ and the dimension of the eigenspace of $S_v$ for $1/t$ otherwise ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · focalMult_add_smul⟧. Hence $q + tv$ is a focal point of $(M, q)$ iff $t = K^{-1}$ for a nonzero principal curvature $K$ in the direction $v$ ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · isFocalPoint_add_smul_iff⟧, the multiplicity of $q + K^{-1}v$ is the multiplicity of $K$ ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · focalMult_add_inv_smul⟧, and for any finitely many values of $t$ the multiplicities of the points $q + tv$ add up to at most $k$ ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · sum_focalMult_add_smul_le⟧.

*Proof as formalized.* $(q, tv) \in N$, and its nullity is that of $g - \ell_{tv} = g - t\ell_v$; the two cases are the computation just made. A nonzero eigenspace for $1/t$ is an eigenvalue $K = 1/t \ne 0$. For the bound, the multiplicity at $q + tv$ is the number of indices $i$ with $t \ne 0$ and $\kappa_i = t^{-1}$ ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · focalMult_add_smul_eq_card⟧. For distinct values of $t$ these index sets are disjoint, $t \mapsto t^{-1}$ being injective, so their sizes add up to at most the number $k$ of indices. Milnor takes $v$ to be a unit vector; for any normal $v$ the principal curvatures in the direction $sv$ are $s$ times those in the direction $v$, and nothing changes.

### III.15 The squared distance: Lemma 6.5 and Theorem 6.6

For $p \in \mathbb R^n$ let $L_p(q) = \|q - p\|^2$ on $M$. It is smooth, as the restriction of a smooth function ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · contDiff_normSq_sub⟧. On $\mathbb R^n$ its derivative at $y$ is $u \mapsto 2\langle y - p, u\rangle$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · hasFDerivAt_normSq_sub⟧ and its Hessian is $2\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · hessian_normSq_sub⟧.

*Critical points* (p. 35; V4.10). $q$ is a critical point of $L_p$ iff $q - p \perp T_qM$ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · isCriticalPointM_normSq_sub_iff⟧, that is, iff $(q, p - q) \in N$ ⟦Chapter4/Focal · isCriticalPointM_normSq_sub_iff_mem_normalBundleSet⟧: the derivative $2\langle q - p, \cdot\rangle$ must vanish on $T_qM$.

*The Hessian* (p. 36). In any parametrization $x$,
$$\operatorname{Hess}(L_p \circ x)(\xi, \eta) = 2\big(\langle dx\,\xi, dx\,\eta\rangle + \langle d^2x(\xi, \eta), x - p\rangle\big)$$
at *every* point ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · hessian_normSq_sub_comp⟧, by the second-order chain rule, whose extra term $d(L_p)(d^2x)$ does not vanish here. In Milnor's coordinates at $q$ this is
$$\operatorname{Hess}(L_p \circ x)(u_q) = 2\,(g - \ell_{p - q})$$
⟦Chapter4/Focal · hessian_normSq_sub_xParam⟧. Milnor writes $p = q + tv$ with $v$ a unit normal, which presupposes that $q$ is critical and $p \ne q$. Using $\ell_{p-q}$ directly, which is linear in $p - q$, removes both conditions. The index and the nullity of $L_p$ at a critical point are those of this form ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · indexM_normSq_sub_eq⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldSubmanifold · nullityM_normSq_sub_eq⟧, the Hessian at a critical point being readable in any slice parametrization (§0.2).

**Lemma 6.5 (V4.11).** Assume $k + w = n$, $p \in \mathbb R^n$, $q \in M$. If $(q, p - q) \in N$, the nullity of $L_p$ at $q$ equals the nullity of $E$ at $(q, p - q)$ ⟦Chapter4/Focal · nullityM_normSq_sub_eq_endpointNullity⟧. Hence $p$ is a focal point of $(M, q)$ with multiplicity $\mu$ iff $q$ is a critical point of $L_p$ with nullity $\mu > 0$ ⟦Chapter4/Focal · isFocalPointMult_iff⟧, and $q$ is a degenerate critical point of $L_p$ iff $p$ is a focal point of $(M, q)$ ⟦Chapter4/Focal · isDegenerate_normSq_sub_iff_isFocalPoint⟧.

*Proof as formalized.* The nullity of $L_p$ at $q$ is that of $2(g - \ell_{p-q})$, and a nonzero factor does not change a null space ⟦Chapter4/Focal · nullity_smul_of_ne_zero⟧; by Assertion 6.4 the nullity of $g - \ell_{p-q}$ is that of $E$ at $(q, p - q)$. The last statement uses that non-degeneracy is nullity $0$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · isNondegenerate_hessianM_iff_nullityM_eq_zero⟧. If $q$ is not critical, $p - q$ is not normal at $q$ and $p$ is not a focal point of $(M, q)$, by the definition; Milnor leaves this case implicit.

**Theorem 6.6 (V4.12).** For every slice submanifold $M \subseteq \mathbb R^n$ and every Haar measure $\mu$ on $\mathbb R^n$ (Lebesgue measure in particular), $L_p$ is a Morse function on $M$ for $\mu$-almost every $p$ ⟦Chapter4/Focal · ae_isMorseFunction_normSq_sub⟧ ⟦Chapter4/Focal · ae_volume_isMorseFunction_normSq_sub⟧.

*Proof as formalized.* If $M$ is empty, every $L_p$ is a Morse function vacuously. Otherwise $k + w = n$. If $L_p$ is not a Morse function, then, $L_p$ being smooth, some critical point $q$ is degenerate, and by Lemma 6.5 $p$ is a focal point of $(M, q)$; so $p$ is a focal point of $M$ ⟦Chapter4/Focal · mem_focalSet_of_not_isMorseFunction⟧. The exceptional set lies in the focal set, which is null by Corollary 6.2. Neither closedness nor compactness of $M$ is used, and $k = n$ is allowed.

*Density.* A nonempty open subset of $\mathbb R^n$ has positive Lebesgue measure, so it contains a point of any set of full measure ⟦Mathlib/MeasureTheory/Measure/Restrict · Measure.exists_mem_of_measure_ne_zero_of_ae⟧. Hence every nonempty open set contains a $p$ with $L_p$ a Morse function, and in particular such a $p$ exists ⟦Chapter4/Focal · exists_mem_isMorseFunction_normSq_sub⟧ ⟦Chapter4/Focal · exists_isMorseFunction_normSq_sub⟧. Milnor uses the density in the proof of Corollary 6.8.

### III.16 Corollary 6.7: Morse functions with compact sublevel sets

Milnor deduces Corollary 6.7 from Theorem 6.6 and Whitney's theorem that an $n$-manifold embeds as a closed subset of $\mathbb R^{2n+1}$. What the argument uses about the embedding is that its image is *closed*. The formal statement is for a closed slice submanifold $M \subseteq \mathbb R^n$; abstract compact manifolds are §III.21.

*Compact sublevel sets.* The set $\{y \in \mathbb R^n : \|y - p\|^2 \le a\}$ is closed and bounded, lying in the closed ball of radius $\max(1, a)$ about $p$ (if $\|y - p\| > 1$, then $\|y - p\| \le \|y - p\|^2 \le a$), hence compact ⟦Chapter4/Existence · isCompact_setOf_normSq_sub_le⟧. If $M$ is closed, its inclusion is a closed embedding, and the preimage of a compact set under a closed embedding is compact. So every sublevel set $\{q \in M : L_p(q) \le a\}$ is compact ⟦Chapter4/Existence · isCompact_sublevel_normSq_sub⟧.

**Corollary 6.7 (V4.13).** If $M$ is closed in $\mathbb R^n$, then for almost every $p$, $L_p$ is a Morse function on $M$ all of whose sublevel sets are compact ⟦Chapter4/Existence · ae_isMorseFunction_normSq_sub_and_isCompact_sublevel⟧; so $M$ carries a Morse function $f$ with every $M^a = f^{-1}(-\infty, a]$ compact ⟦Chapter4/Existence · exists_isMorseFunction_isCompact_sublevel⟧, and each $M^a$ then contains only finitely many critical points ⟦Chapter4/Existence · exists_isMorseFunction_finite_critPoints_sublevel⟧.

*Proof as formalized.* Theorem 6.6 and the compactness just proved. For the finiteness: non-degenerate critical points are isolated (Corollary 2.3) ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · IsNondegenerateCriticalPointM.exists_nhds_isCriticalPointM_eq⟧, the critical set of a $C^1$ function is closed ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · isClosed_setOf_isCriticalPointM⟧, so the critical points in the compact set $M^a$ form a compact discrete set, hence a finite one ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · finite_inter_setOf_isCriticalPointM⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · IsMorseFunction.finite_inter_setOf_isCriticalPointM⟧. This is the form in which §§3 and 5 use the corollary. For compact $M$: almost every $L_p$ is a Morse function with finitely many critical points ⟦Chapter4/Compact · ae_isMorseFunction_normSq_sub_finite_critPoints⟧, and $M$ carries one ⟦Chapter4/Compact · exists_isMorseFunction_finite_critPoints⟧.

Two hypotheses Milnor does not state are visible here. The closedness of the embedding is essential: for the open interval $(-1, 1) \subseteq \mathbb R$ every $L_p$ has a non-compact sublevel set (all of the interval, once $a$ is large). And a manifold in Milnor's sense must be Hausdorff with a countable basis: on the long line every continuous real function is eventually constant, so some sublevel set is not compact. For non-compact abstract manifolds the corollary is not formalized, because Mathlib has Whitney's theorem only for compact manifolds.

### III.17 Corollary 6.8: approximation, without boundedness

Milnor's Corollary 6.8: a bounded smooth $f$ on $M$ is a uniform limit of functions without degenerate critical points, and the approximating $g$ can be chosen so that on a given compact set its derivatives up to a given order approximate those of $f$. His proof embeds $M$ as a bounded set by an embedding $h$ with first coordinate $f$, and takes $g = (L_p \circ h - c^2)/2c$ with $p$ near $(-c, 0, \dots, 0)$ and $c$ large. The formal proof is different: it perturbs $f$ by a small linear function, $g = f + \langle a, \cdot\rangle$, and repeats the proof of Theorem 6.6 with $E$ replaced by the generalized endpoint map $E_\Gamma$, $\Gamma = -\nabla F$. For $f = \tfrac12\|x\|^2$ this is Theorem 6.6 itself, since $\tfrac12\|x\|^2 + \langle a, x\rangle = \tfrac12 L_{-a}(x) - \tfrac12\|a\|^2$.

*Gradients.* The Riesz isomorphism $u \mapsto \langle u, \cdot\rangle$ of $\mathbb R^n$ onto its dual ⟦Chapter4/Approximation · rieszEquiv⟧ defines the negative gradient $-\nabla F$ ⟦Chapter4/Approximation · negGrad⟧, with $\langle -\nabla F(y), u\rangle = -dF_y(u)$ ⟦Chapter4/Approximation · inner_negGrad⟧. It is smooth where $F$ is ⟦Chapter4/Approximation · contDiffOn_negGrad⟧, and its derivative is minus the Hessian, $\langle d(-\nabla F)_y u, w\rangle = -d^2F_y(u, w)$ ⟦Chapter4/Approximation · hasFDerivAt_negGrad⟧ ⟦Chapter4/Approximation · inner_fderiv_negGrad⟧. Adding a linear function changes the derivative, $d(F + \langle a, \cdot\rangle)_y(u) = \langle a - (-\nabla F(y)), u\rangle$, but not the Hessian ⟦Chapter4/Approximation · fderiv_add_inner⟧ ⟦Chapter4/Approximation · hessian_add_inner⟧. In Milnor's coordinates the Hessian of $G \circ x$ is $d^2G_q(dx\,\xi, dx\,\eta) + dG_q(d^2x(\xi, \eta))$ ⟦Chapter4/Approximation · hessian_comp_xParam⟧.

*Local extensions.* A smooth $f : M \to \mathbb R$ extends near each $p \in M$ to a smooth $F$ on an open set $U \subseteq \mathbb R^n$: in the slice chart $\sigma$ at $p$, put $F(y) = f(\sigma^{-1}((\sigma y)_1, 0))$, which is constant along the slices $\{(\sigma y)_1 = \mathrm{const}\}$ and agrees with $f$ on $M \cap U$ ⟦Chapter4/Approximation · exists_local_extension_of_contMDiff⟧.

**The local argument.** Assume $k + w = n$. Let $F$ be smooth on an open $U \subseteq \mathbb R^n$ and agree with the smooth $f$ on $M \cap U$, and let $q \in M \cap U$ be a degenerate critical point of $f + \langle a, \cdot\rangle$. Then $(q, a + \nabla F(q))$ is a critical point of $E_{-\nabla F}$, lying over $U$, with value $a$ ⟦Chapter4/Approximation · mem_image_critPoints_of_not_isNondegenerate⟧.

*Proof as formalized.*

1. Put $g = f + \langle a, \cdot\rangle$ on $M$ and $G = F + \langle a, \cdot\rangle$ on $U$; $g = G$ on $M$ near $q$, so $q$ is a critical point of $G|_M$, and $dG_q$ vanishes on $T_qM$. As $dG_q = \langle a - (-\nabla F(q)), \cdot\rangle$, the vector $v = a - (-\nabla F(q)) = a + \nabla F(q)$ is normal at $q$, so $(q, v) \in N$; and $E_{-\nabla F}(q, v) = -\nabla F(q) + v = a$.
2. In Milnor's coordinates at $q$, $\operatorname{Hess}(G \circ x)(\xi, \eta) = d^2F_q(dx\,\xi, dx\,\eta) + \langle v, d^2x(\xi, \eta)\rangle = -\big(\langle d(-\nabla F)_q\, dx\,\xi, dx\,\eta\rangle - \ell_v(\xi, \eta)\big)$. So the Hessian is $-B_{d(-\nabla F)_q, v}$, and the nullity of $g$ at $q$, read in the slice chart ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.nullity_eq⟧, is the nullity of $B_{d(-\nabla F)_q, v}$.
3. By §III.12 this is the dimension of the kernel of $dE_{-\nabla F}$ at $(q, v)$. It is positive because $q$ is degenerate, and an endomorphism of $\mathbb R^n$ with nonzero kernel is not onto ⟦Chapter4/Approximation · finrank_ker_pos_iff_not_surjective⟧.

**Theorem (V4.20).** For every slice submanifold $M \subseteq \mathbb R^n$, every smooth $f : M \to \mathbb R$ and every Haar measure on $\mathbb R^n$, $f + \langle a, \cdot\rangle$ is a Morse function for almost every $a$; such $a$ exist in every nonempty open set ⟦Chapter4/Approximation · ae_isMorseFunction_add_inner⟧ ⟦Chapter4/Approximation · exists_mem_isMorseFunction_add_inner⟧.

*Proof as formalized.* If $M$ is empty there is nothing to prove; otherwise $k + w = n$. Choose a local extension $(U_p, F_p)$ at each $p \in M$. As $M$ is second countable, countably many of the open sets $M \cap U_p$ cover $M$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Bases · TopologicalSpace.countable_cover_nhds⟧. For each of them, $E_{-\nabla F_p}$ is smooth on the open part of $N$ over $U_p$, and Theorem 6.1 on that open set makes the image of its critical points there null. If $f + \langle a, \cdot\rangle$ is not a Morse function, it has a degenerate critical point $q$, which lies in some $U_p$ of the countable family, and the local argument puts $a$ in the corresponding null set. A countable union of null sets is null. No boundedness, compactness or closedness of anything is used.

**Corollary 6.8 for compact $M$ (V4.21).** Let $M \subseteq \mathbb R^n$ be compact, $f$ smooth, $\varepsilon > 0$, $r \in \mathbb N$, and $y_j$ finitely many smooth coordinate systems of $M$ with compact sets $K_j$ in their ranges. There is a Morse function $g = f + \langle a, \cdot\rangle$ with $|g - f| < \varepsilon$ on $M$ and $\|D^i((g - f) \circ y_j^{-1})(u)\| < \varepsilon$ for all $j$, all $i \le r$ and all $u \in K_j$ ⟦Chapter4/Approximation · exists_isMorseFunction_approx⟧.

*Proof as formalized.* Bound $\|x\| \le R$ on $M$, and the iterated derivatives of order $i \le r$ of the inclusion read in $y_j$ by $C_{j,i}$ on $K_j$; these exist because those derivatives are continuous on the open target of $y_j$ ⟦Chapter4/Approximation · exists_bound_iteratedFDeriv_val_comp_symm⟧. Let $D \ge 0$ dominate all these bounds and $\delta = \varepsilon/(D + 1)$, so that $\delta D < \varepsilon$. Choose $a$ with $\|a\| < \delta$ and $f + \langle a, \cdot\rangle$ a Morse function. Then $|g - f| = |\langle a, x\rangle| \le \|a\|\|x\| \le \delta D < \varepsilon$. And $(g - f) \circ y_j^{-1}$ is the linear map $\langle a, \cdot\rangle$ composed with the inclusion read in $y_j$, so its $i$-th derivative is $\langle a, \cdot\rangle$ applied to the $i$-th derivative of the inclusion, of norm at most $\|a\|\, C_{j,i} < \varepsilon$.

Derivatives on a manifold exist only in coordinates, so Milnor's approximation of derivatives on a compact $K$ has to be read in fixed coordinate systems covering $K$, finitely many by compactness: that is how it is stated.

*Why boundedness is unnecessary.* On a non-compact $M$ the perturbation $\langle a, x\rangle$ is unbounded, so the theorem above gives uniform approximation only for compact $M$. Milnor's boundedness hypothesis serves exactly to keep his embedding $h = (f, \dots)$ bounded, so that the error terms $\sum h_i^2/2c$ are uniformly small. The formal proof makes the *perturbation* bounded instead. Let
$$\beta(y) = \frac{y}{\sqrt{1 + \|y\|^2}},$$
Mathlib's diffeomorphism of $\mathbb R^n$ onto its open unit ball ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Normed/Module/Ball/Homeomorph · OpenPartialHomeomorph.univUnitBall⟧, here as a partial diffeomorphism ⟦Chapter4/Uniform · ballDiffeo⟧; $\|\beta(y)\| < 1$ ⟦Chapter4/Uniform · norm_ballDiffeo_lt_one⟧. Then $g = f + \langle a, \beta(\cdot)\rangle$ satisfies $|g - f| \le \|a\|$, whatever $f$ is.

1. *$\beta(M)$ is a submanifold.* For a slice chart $\sigma$ of $M$, $\sigma \circ \beta^{-1}$, on the part of the unit ball over its domain, is a slice chart of $\beta(M)$: $z \in \beta(M)$ iff $\beta^{-1}(z) \in M$ iff $(\sigma\beta^{-1} z)_2 = 0$ ⟦Chapter4/Uniform · ballSliceChart⟧ ⟦Chapter4/Uniform · isSliceSubmanifold_ballImage⟧. The maps $\beta : M \to \beta(M)$ and $\beta^{-1} : \beta(M) \to M$ are mutually inverse ⟦Chapter4/Uniform · toBall⟧ ⟦Chapter4/Uniform · fromBall⟧, and $\beta^{-1}$ is smooth ⟦Chapter4/Uniform · contMDiff_fromBall⟧.
2. *The Morse property comes back along $\beta$.* If $g'$ is a Morse function on $\beta(M)$ and $g = g' \circ \beta$ is smooth, then $g$ is a Morse function on $M$ ⟦Chapter4/Uniform · isMorseFunction_of_ballImage⟧. Read $g$ in the chart $\sigma$ of $M$ at $x$ and $g'$ in the chart $\sigma \circ \beta^{-1}$ of $\beta(M)$ at $\beta(x)$. The two charts send $x$ and $\beta(x)$ to the same point ⟦Chapter4/Uniform · ballSliceChart_toChart_toBall⟧, and since $(\sigma\beta^{-1})^{-1}(u) = \beta(\sigma^{-1}(u))$ the two local representatives agree near it ⟦Chapter4/Uniform · fromBall_ballSliceChart_toChart_symm⟧. Critical points and non-degenerate critical points may be read in any smooth coordinate system ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.isCriticalPointM_iff⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.isNondegenerateCriticalPointM_iff⟧, and functions agreeing near a point have the same critical and non-degenerate critical points there ⟦Chapter4/Uniform · isCriticalPoint_iff_of_eventuallyEq⟧ ⟦Chapter4/Uniform · isNondegenerateCriticalPoint_iff_of_eventuallyEq⟧. So a critical point $x$ of $g$ gives a critical point $\beta(x)$ of $g'$, which is non-degenerate, and hence $x$ is non-degenerate.
3. *Genericity.* Apply the theorem above to the submanifold $\beta(M)$ and the smooth function $f \circ \beta^{-1}$: for almost every $a$, $f \circ \beta^{-1} + \langle a, \cdot\rangle$ is a Morse function on $\beta(M)$, and composed with $\beta$ it is $f + \langle a, \beta(\cdot)\rangle$, which is smooth, $y \mapsto \langle a, \beta(y)\rangle$ being smooth ⟦Chapter4/Uniform · contDiff_inner_ballDiffeo⟧; by step 2 it is a Morse function on $M$ ⟦Chapter4/Uniform · ae_isMorseFunction_add_inner_ballDiffeo⟧.

**Corollary 6.8 (V4.21).** Let $M \subseteq \mathbb R^n$ be any slice submanifold and $f : M \to \mathbb R$ any smooth function, bounded or not. For every $\varepsilon > 0$, $r \in \mathbb N$ and finitely many smooth coordinate systems $y_j$ with compact sets $K_j$ in their ranges, there is a Morse function $g$ with $|g - f| < \varepsilon$ on all of $M$ and $\|D^i((g - f) \circ y_j^{-1})(u)\| < \varepsilon$ for $i \le r$ and $u \in K_j$; one can take $g = f + \langle a, \beta(\cdot)\rangle$ with $a$ small ⟦Chapter4/Uniform · exists_isMorseFunction_approx_uniform⟧.

*Proof as formalized.* As for compact $M$, with $\beta \circ \iota \circ y_j^{-1}$ in place of $\iota \circ y_j^{-1}$ ($\iota$ the inclusion), smooth on the target of $y_j$, and with $D \ge 1$ dominating the derivative bounds on the $K_j$. Then $|g - f| = |\langle a, \beta(x)\rangle| \le \|a\| < \delta \le \delta D < \varepsilon$, and the derivative estimate is as before. Milnor's own route, $L_p$ on an embedding with first coordinate $f$, is not formalized, although its two ingredients, that the image of an embedding is a submanifold and that the Morse notions transport along it, are proved (§III.19, §III.20).

### III.18 Lemma 6.9: the index of $L_p$

**Lemma (the index of $g - \ell_v$).** For $q \in M$ and $v \in \mathbb R^n$, the index of $g - \ell_v$ is the number of principal curvatures in the direction $v$, with multiplicity, that are greater than $1$ ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · index_firstFF_sub_secondFF⟧.

*Proof as formalized.* Let $e_1, \dots, e_k$ be an orthonormal eigenbasis of $S_v$ in $T_qM$, with eigenvalues $\kappa_1, \dots, \kappa_k$, and let $\Phi : \mathbb R^k \to \mathbb R^k$ send $\xi$ to the coordinates $c_i$ of $dx\,\xi$ in this basis, a linear isomorphism. Then $g(\xi, \xi) = \|dx\,\xi\|^2 = \sum_i c_i^2$, the basis being orthonormal, and $\ell_v(\xi, \xi) = \langle S_v\, dx\,\xi, dx\,\xi\rangle = \sum_i \kappa_i c_i^2$, $S_v$ acting diagonally ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Spectrum · LinearMap.IsSymmetric.eigenvectorBasis_apply_self_apply⟧. So the quadratic form of $g - \ell_v$ is equivalent, through $\Phi$, to $\sum_i (1 - \kappa_i)c_i^2$, and by Sylvester's law of inertia ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/QuadraticForm/Signature · QuadraticForm.sigNeg_of_equiv_weightedSumSquares⟧ its index is $\#\{i : 1 - \kappa_i < 0\} = \#\{i : \kappa_i > 1\}$.

At a critical point $q$ of $L_p$, the index of $L_p$ is the index of $2(g - \ell_{p-q})$ (§III.15), a positive multiple ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · index_smul⟧, hence the number of principal curvatures of $M$ at $q$ in the direction $p - q$ that are greater than $1$ ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · indexM_normSq_sub_eq_card⟧. If $p - q = tv$ with $\|v\| = 1$, the curvatures in the direction $p - q$ are $t$ times those in the direction $v$, so the condition $\kappa > 1$ in the direction $p - q$ is Milnor's condition $K > 1/t$ in the direction $v$.

**Lemma 6.9 (V4.23).** Assume $k + w = n$, and let $q$ be a non-degenerate critical point of $L_p$. Then the index of $L_p$ at $q$ is
$$\sum_{t \in [0, 1]} \operatorname{mult}_q\big(q + t(p - q)\big),$$
a sum with finitely many nonzero terms: the number of focal points of $(M, q)$ on the segment from $q$ to $p$, each counted with its multiplicity ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · indexM_normSq_sub_eq_finsum_focalMult⟧.

*Proof as formalized.* Put $v = p - q$, normal at $q$, and let $\kappa_1, \dots, \kappa_k$ be the principal curvatures in the direction $v$.

1. *No $\kappa_i$ equals $1$.* Otherwise the multiplicity of $q + 1 \cdot v = p$ would count the index $i$ and be positive. But it is the nullity of $E$ at $(q, p - q)$, which by Lemma 6.5 is the nullity of $L_p$ at $q$, which is $0$ by non-degeneracy. This is where non-degeneracy enters, and it is why Milnor's count of the eigenvalues that are at least $1/t$ must be a count of those that are greater than $1/t$: equality would make $p$ a focal point of $(M, q)$.
2. By the previous lemma the index is $\#\{i : \kappa_i > 1\}$. Group these indices by the value $t = \kappa_i^{-1}$: the index is $\sum_t \#\{i : t \ne 0,\ \kappa_i = t^{-1}\}$, summed over the finite set of values $\kappa_i^{-1}$ with $\kappa_i > 1$, and each summand is $\operatorname{mult}_q(q + tv)$ by Lemma 6.3 ⟦Chapter4/Curvature · focalMult_add_smul_eq_card⟧.
3. *The support.* If $t \in [0, 1]$ and $\operatorname{mult}_q(q + tv) > 0$, then $t \ne 0$ and $\kappa_i = t^{-1} \ge 1$ for some $i$, hence $\kappa_i > 1$ by step 1, and $t$ is one of the values of step 2. Conversely those values lie in $(0, 1)$. So the sum over $t \in [0, 1]$ has its support in the finite set of step 2, and the two sums agree.

The case $p = q$ is included: the index is $0$, the segment is $\{q\}$, and $q$ is not a focal point of $(M, q)$. Milnor's proof sets it aside implicitly by writing $p = q + tv$ with $t > 0$.

### III.19 The Whitney bridge: the image of an embedding is a submanifold

Milnor's manifolds are abstract; in the proof of Corollary 6.7 he quotes Whitney's embedding theorem and from then on identifies a manifold with its image in Euclidean space. Two facts are taken for granted there: that the image of a differentiable embedding is a manifold in Euclidean space, and that the embedding identifies the two, so that critical points, indices and sublevel sets correspond. This section proves the first, §III.20 the second.

Let $M$ be a smooth manifold without boundary modelled on a finite-dimensional real space $E$, $F$ a finite-dimensional real normed space (Milnor's $\mathbb R^K$), and $e : M \to F$ smooth, a topological embedding (a homeomorphism onto its image), with injective derivative everywhere. Let $Z, W$ be finite-dimensional with $\dim Z = \dim E$ and $\dim Z + \dim W = \dim F$.

*Linear algebra.* If $A : E \to F$ is injective and $T : Z \cong E$, there is $L : W \to F$ such that $(z, w) \mapsto A(Tz) + Lw$ is an isomorphism $Z \times W \cong F$ ⟦Chapter4/WhitneySlice · exists_continuousLinearEquiv_coprod⟧. Choose a complement $C$ of $\operatorname{range} A$. It has the dimension of $W$, since $\operatorname{range} A$ has that of $Z$; take $L$ to be an isomorphism $W \cong C$ followed by the inclusion. The map $(z, w) \mapsto A(Tz) + Lw$ is injective: if $A(Tz) = -Lw$, this vector lies in $\operatorname{range} A \cap C = 0$, so $z = 0$ and $w = 0$. Equal dimensions make it an isomorphism.

**The local immersion theorem, as a slice chart (V4.14).** For every $x \in M$ there is a slice chart $\sigma$ of $e(M) \subseteq F$ whose domain contains $e(x)$, such that for each $p$ in its domain $T(\sigma p)_1$ lies in the target of the chart $\varphi$ of $M$ at $x$, and $e(\varphi^{-1}(T(\sigma p)_1)) = p$ whenever $p \in e(M)$ ⟦Chapter4/WhitneySlice · exists_sliceChart_range⟧.

*Proof as formalized.*

1. Let $L$ complete $de_x$ as above, and $\Psi(z, w) = e(\varphi^{-1}(Tz)) + Lw$, smooth on the open set $S_0 = \{(z, w) : Tz \in \text{target of } \varphi\}$. At $x_0 = (T^{-1}\varphi(x), 0)$ its derivative is $(z, w) \mapsto de_x(Tz) + Lw$, an isomorphism. Here the derivative of $e \circ \varphi^{-1}$ at $\varphi(x)$ is $de_x$, the derivative of a map read in charts ⟦Milnor/Chapter1/InverseFunction · hasFDerivAt_writtenInExtChartAt⟧.
2. By the inverse function theorem on manifolds of the companion project ⟦Milnor/Chapter1/InverseFunction · isLocalDiffeomorphAt_of_isInvertible_mfderiv⟧, $\Psi$ agrees near $x_0$ with a partial diffeomorphism $\Phi$; restrict it to $S_0$.
3. Let $U$ be the set of $y$ in the domain of $\varphi$ with $(T^{-1}\varphi(y), 0)$ in the domain of $\Phi$; it is open and contains $x$. **Here the embedding hypothesis enters:** $e$ is a homeomorphism onto its image, so $U = e^{-1}(O)$ for some open $O \subseteq F$.
4. The slice chart is $\Phi^{-1}$ restricted to $O$, with base point $e(x)$. If $p = e(y)$ is in its domain, then $y \in U$ and $\Phi(T^{-1}\varphi(y), 0) = \Psi(T^{-1}\varphi(y), 0) = e(y) + L0 = p$, so $\Phi^{-1}(p)$ has second component $0$. Conversely, if $\Phi^{-1}(p) = (z, 0)$, then $p = \Psi(z, 0) = e(\varphi^{-1}(Tz))$ lies in $e(M)$. The same computation gives the last assertion.

So $e(M)$ is a slice submanifold of $F$ modelled on $Z$ with complement $W$ ⟦Chapter4/WhitneySlice · isSliceSubmanifold_range⟧, and **$e : M \to e(M)$ is a diffeomorphism** ⟦Chapter4/WhitneySlice · diffeomorphRange⟧ ⟦Chapter4/WhitneySlice · exists_diffeomorph_range⟧. The forward map is smooth because $e$ is smooth with values in $e(M)$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Corestrict · contMDiff_codRestrict⟧. The inverse is smooth because near $e(x)$ it is $p \mapsto \varphi^{-1}(T(\sigma p)_1)$, a composite of the inclusion of $e(M)$, the chart $\sigma$, a linear map and $\varphi^{-1}$ ⟦Chapter4/WhitneySlice · contMDiff_ofInjective_symm⟧.

Three remarks. An injective immersion need not have a submanifold as image (the figure eight); the hypothesis that $e$ is a homeomorphism onto its image is used once, in step 3, and for compact $M$ it is automatic. The inverse of $e$ is not smooth merely because $e$ is a smooth bijection onto $e(M)$ ($x \mapsto x^3$ on $\mathbb R$); it is read off the slice chart. And the model of $e(M)$ may be any $Z$ of the right dimension, which is how an abstract manifold modelled on $E$ is read on $\mathbb R^{d+1}$, the model of §III.9.

### III.20 Invariance of the Morse notions under diffeomorphisms

Milnor defines critical points, non-degeneracy and the index intrinsically, and in §6 treats a function on a manifold and the corresponding function on its image as the same; the invariance this needs is never stated. Let $g : M \to M'$ be a smooth diffeomorphism between smooth manifolds without boundary modelled on real normed spaces $E$, $E'$, and $f : M \to \mathbb R$. The function $f$ on $M$ corresponds to $f \circ g^{-1}$ on $M'$, and all the statements below are V4.15.

*Critical points* ⟦Chapter4/WhitneyInvariance · isCriticalPointM_comp_symm_iff⟧. $q$ is a critical point of $f \circ g^{-1}$ iff $g^{-1}(q)$ is a critical point of $f$, for *every* $f$. If $f$ is differentiable at $g^{-1}(q)$, the chain rule gives $d(f \circ g^{-1})_q = df_{g^{-1}(q)} \circ d(g^{-1})_q$, and $d(g^{-1})_q$ is onto, so one side vanishes iff the other does. If $f$ is not differentiable there, neither is $f \circ g^{-1}$ at $q$, since composing back with $g$ would make $f$ differentiable; and both derivatives are $0$ by convention. Hence $g$ maps the critical set of $f$ onto that of $f \circ g^{-1}$ ⟦Chapter4/WhitneyInvariance · setOf_isCriticalPointM_comp_symm⟧, and the two critical sets have the same number of elements ⟦Chapter4/WhitneyInvariance · ncard_setOf_isCriticalPointM_comp_symm⟧.

*The Hessian.* The Hessian of $f \circ g^{-1}$ at $q$ is read in the chart of $M'$ at $q$ (§0.2) ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · hessianM_eq_chartAt⟧. That is the same as reading $f$ in the coordinate system $\mathrm{chart}_q \circ g$ on $M$ around $g^{-1}(q)$ ⟦Chapter4/WhitneyInvariance · diffeoCoord⟧ ⟦Chapter4/WhitneyInvariance · hessianM_comp_symm_eq⟧, which is a smooth coordinate system ⟦Chapter4/WhitneyInvariance · isSmoothCoord_diffeoCoord⟧. By the chart independence of Part I ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.hessian_comp_symm⟧, at a critical point where $f$ is $C^2$ that Hessian is the Hessian of $f$ pulled back by the derivative of the inverse coordinates. That derivative is $d(g^{-1})_q$: the derivative of the inverse chart at the centre of the chart is the identity ⟦Chapter4/WhitneyInvariance · mfderiv_chartAt_symm_self_apply⟧. So
$$(f \circ g^{-1})_{**}(v, w) = f_{**}\big(d(g^{-1})_q v,\ d(g^{-1})_q w\big)$$
⟦Chapter4/WhitneyInvariance · hessianM_comp_symm_apply⟧. Non-degeneracy, non-degenerate critical points, index and nullity correspond, through the same coordinate system ⟦Chapter4/WhitneyInvariance · isNondegenerate_hessianM_comp_symm_iff⟧ ⟦Chapter4/WhitneyInvariance · isNondegenerateCriticalPointM_comp_symm_iff⟧ ⟦Chapter4/WhitneyInvariance · indexM_comp_symm⟧ ⟦Chapter4/WhitneyInvariance · nullityM_comp_symm⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsSmoothCoord.index_eq⟧.

*Morse functions and Morse numbers.* A Morse function composed with $g^{-1}$ is a Morse function ⟦Chapter4/WhitneyInvariance · IsMorseFunction.comp_diffeomorph_symm⟧, and applying this to $g^{-1}$ gives the converse ⟦Chapter4/WhitneyInvariance · isMorseFunction_comp_symm_iff⟧. For $f$ of class $C^2$, $g$ maps the critical points of $f$ of index $\lambda$ bijectively onto those of $f \circ g^{-1}$, so $C_\lambda(f \circ g^{-1}) = C_\lambda(f)$ ⟦Chapter4/WhitneyInvariance · critCount_comp_symm⟧. Only $C^2$ at the point is used for the Hessian statements, criticality needs no regularity at all, and the model spaces may differ.

### III.21 Part I on an abstract compact manifold

In this section $M$ is a compact Hausdorff smooth manifold without boundary modelled on a finite-dimensional real space $E$: one of Milnor's manifolds, read as Hausdorff, the countable basis being automatic by compactness.

*Embeddings (V4.16).* Mathlib's Whitney theorem for compact manifolds, `exists_embedding_euclidean_of_compact`, gives a smooth closed embedding $e : M \to \mathbb R^n$ with injective derivative, for some $n$; by §III.19 its image is a slice submanifold modelled on $E$ ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · exists_isSliceSubmanifold_whitney⟧, the dimension count $\dim E \le n$ coming from the injectivity of a derivative ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · finrank_le_of_injective_clm⟧. Following $e$ by $v \mapsto (v, 0)$ into a larger Euclidean space gives an embedding into $\mathbb R^{K+1}$ with $K \ge 1$, and with $\dim M + m = K + 1$ for some $m$ ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · exists_embedding_euclidean⟧. The two extra coordinates are there for the Poincaré–Hopf theorem of the companion project, which needs $K \ge 1$; Milnor's $\mathbb R^{2n+1}$ plays no role. Together with §III.19: **$M$ is diffeomorphic to a compact, closed slice submanifold $N \subseteq \mathbb R^{K+1}$, $K \ge 1$, modelled on $\mathbb R^{\dim M}$** ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · exists_diffeomorph_sliceSubmanifold⟧. Below, $\Phi : M \to N$ is such a diffeomorphism.

*Theorem 6.6 and Corollary 6.7 (V4.17).* $M$ carries a Morse function, with finitely many critical points ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · exists_isMorseFunction_of_compactSpace⟧: take a Morse function $f_0$ on $N$ (§III.16) and pull it back to $f_0 \circ \Phi$, a Morse function by §III.20; its critical set is finite by compactness. So the function is $L_p \circ \Phi$, for a $p$ taken from a set of full measure.

*Corollary 6.8 (V4.22).* For any embedding $e : M \to \mathbb R^n$ as in §III.19 and any smooth $f$, $f + \langle a, e(\cdot)\rangle$ is a Morse function for almost every $a$ ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · ae_isMorseFunction_add_inner_comp⟧; this statement needs neither compactness nor the Hausdorff property, nor a closed embedding. If $M$ is empty it is vacuous. Otherwise $e(M)$ is a slice submanifold diffeomorphic to $M$ by $\Phi = e$ (§III.19), $f \circ \Phi^{-1}$ is smooth on it, the theorem of §III.17 applies to it, and §III.20 carries the Morse property back, since $f + \langle a, e(\cdot)\rangle = (f \circ \Phi^{-1} + \langle a, \cdot\rangle) \circ \Phi$. With Mathlib's embedding, the estimates of §III.17 then give Corollary 6.8 on $M$: every smooth $f$ is a uniform limit of Morse functions $f + \langle a, e(\cdot)\rangle$, with derivatives up to any order close on compact sets of finitely many coordinate systems, which may now take values in any real normed space ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · exists_isMorseFunction_approx_of_compactSpace⟧. Milnor's boundedness hypothesis is automatic for compact $M$.

*The Euler number (V4.18).* In dimension $0$ every point is critical for every function ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · isCriticalPointM_of_finrank_eq_zero⟧, of index $0$, so $\sum_\lambda (-1)^\lambda C_\lambda(f) = C_0(f) = \#M$ for every $f$ ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · sum_critCount_of_finrank_eq_zero⟧. Define
$$\chi(M) = \sum_{\lambda=0}^{\dim M} (-1)^\lambda C_\lambda(f_M)$$
for one Morse function $f_M$ chosen once and for all, and $\chi(M) = 0$ if $M$ has none ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · eulerNumberM⟧. This is Milnor's formula $\chi(M) = C_0 - C_1 + \dots \pm C_n$ taken as a definition; the next result shows the choice does not matter.

**Theorem (V4.19, formula (3) on a compact manifold).** For any two Morse functions $f, g$ on $M$, $\sum_\lambda (-1)^\lambda C_\lambda(f) = \sum_\lambda (-1)^\lambda C_\lambda(g)$ ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · sum_critCount_eq_of_isMorseFunction_of_compactSpace⟧. Hence every Morse function $f$ on $M$ has $\sum_\lambda (-1)^\lambda C_\lambda(f) = \chi(M)$ ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · sum_critCount_eq_eulerNumberM⟧ ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · exists_eulerNumber_of_compactSpace⟧.

*Proof as formalized.* In dimension $0$ both sides are $\#M$. If $M$ is empty, every $C_\lambda$ is $0$. Otherwise $\dim M = d + 1$; take the embedded copy $N$, nonempty, with $K \ge 1$. The functions $f \circ \Phi^{-1}$ and $g \circ \Phi^{-1}$ are Morse functions on $N$ with the same Morse numbers as $f$ and $g$ (§III.20), and on $N$ the alternating count does not depend on the Morse function (§III.9).

*Application 2, on an embedded copy.* Let $\dim M = d + 1 \ge 1$, and let $N \subseteq \mathbb R^{K+1}$, $K \ge 1$, be any compact nonempty slice submanifold modelled on $\mathbb R^{d+1}$ and diffeomorphic to $M$, for instance the image of any embedding. Then every Euler number of $N$ equals $\chi(M)$ ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · IsEulerNumber.eq_eulerNumberM⟧. Indeed, for a Morse function $f$ on $M$, the Euler number is $\sum_\lambda (-1)^\lambda C_\lambda(f \circ \Phi^{-1})$ by §III.9, which is $\sum_\lambda (-1)^\lambda C_\lambda(f) = \chi(M)$ by §III.20. So the Poincaré–Hopf integer does not depend on the embedding. And $M$ of positive dimension is diffeomorphic to a compact $N$ on which every smooth tangent field with finitely many zeros has index sum $\chi(M)$ ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · exists_isEulerNumber_eulerNumberM⟧: the Euler number of the copy exists by Poincaré–Hopf, and it equals $\chi(M)$. Vector fields and their indices are defined in the companion project only for submanifolds of Euclidean space, which is why Application 2 is stated on a copy. Finally $\chi$ is invariant under diffeomorphisms of compact Hausdorff manifolds ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · eulerNumberM_eq_of_diffeomorph⟧: compute both sides with $f$ and $f \circ g^{-1}$, whose Morse numbers agree; the dimensions agree when $M$ is nonempty, a derivative of $g$ being an isomorphism, and both counts vanish when it is empty. That $\chi(M) = \sum_i (-1)^i \dim H_i(M; \Bbbk)$ for every field $\Bbbk$ is in Part IV ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyManifold · eulerNumberM_eq_sum_bettiNumber⟧.

**Theorem 4.1 (Reeb) on a compact manifold (V4.24).** If a smooth $f$ on $M$, $\dim M = n$, has exactly two critical points, both non-degenerate, then $M$ is homeomorphic to $S^n$ ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · reeb_of_compactSpace⟧.

*Proof as formalized.* On the copy $N \subseteq \mathbb R^{K+1}$, modelled on $\mathbb R^n$, the function $f \circ \Phi^{-1}$ is smooth, has exactly two critical points (§III.20, the counts of critical points agree), and they are non-degenerate (§III.20). For $n = 0$, §III.4 in dimension $0$ applies; for $n \ge 1$, Reeb's theorem for intrinsic functions on a compact submanifold of $\mathbb R^{K+1}$ (§III.4). The homeomorphism is $\Phi$ followed by $N \cong S^n$.

**Theorem 3.1 on a compact manifold (V2.4, V4.25).** If $f$ is smooth on $M$, $a \le b$, and $f^{-1}[a, b]$ contains no critical point, then a diffeomorphism $\Psi$ of $M$ has $\Psi(M^a) = M^b$, $M^a$ is a strong deformation retract of $M^b$, and the inclusion $M^a \to M^b$ is a homotopy equivalence ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · theorem_3_1_of_compactSpace⟧.

*Proof as formalized.*

1. The homotopy equivalence follows from the strong deformation retraction (Part II) ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsStrongDeformationRetract.exists_homotopyEquiv⟧, so two statements remain.
2. *The empty slab.* If $f$ takes no value in $[a, b]$, then $M^a = M^b$: a point with $f \le b$ has $f < a$. The identity diffeomorphism works, and $M^a$ is trivially a strong deformation retract of itself ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsStrongDeformationRetract⟧.
3. Otherwise some $p_0$ has $f(p_0) \in [a, b]$. It is not critical, so $\dim M \ge 1$, since in dimension $0$ every point is critical. Take the copy $N$, nonempty, and extend $f \circ \Phi^{-1}$ to a smooth $F$ on $\mathbb R^{K+1}$ (§III.9). The critical points of $F|_N$ correspond to those of $f$ (§III.20), so $F|_N$ has none in $f^{-1}[a, b]$. Theorem 3.1 on the compact submanifold $N$ (Part II) gives a diffeomorphism $\Phi_N$ with $\Phi_N(N^a) = N^b$ and the strong deformation retraction of $N^b$ onto $N^a$.
4. *Transport.* $\Phi$ maps $M^c$ onto $N^c$ for every $c$, since $F \circ \Phi = f$. So $\Psi = \Phi^{-1} \circ \Phi_N \circ \Phi$ maps $M^a$ onto $M^b$. And $\Phi$ restricts to a homeomorphism $M^b \cong N^b$ under which $M^a$ corresponds to $N^a$. A strong deformation retract is carried back by a homeomorphism $h$, with retraction $h^{-1} \circ r \circ h$ and homotopy $h^{-1} \circ H \circ (\mathrm{id} \times h)$ ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · IsStrongDeformationRetract.preimage_homeomorph⟧.

Theorems 3.2 and 3.5 are not transferred. Nor is anything for non-compact abstract manifolds: Mathlib has no closed embedding of a non-compact manifold into Euclidean space.

### III.22 What the formalization taught us

- **Reeb's eight lines.** The indices $0$ and $n$ at the extrema are used without comment (§III.1); minimum and maximum are distinct only for $n \ge 1$ and by non-degeneracy (§III.4); a small $\varepsilon$ must put the whole sublevel set in the Morse chart, which needs compactness and a unique minimum (§III.2); the level $f^{-1}(\varepsilon)$ goes onto $f^{-1}(1 - \varepsilon)$ by the proof of Theorem 3.1, not by its statement (§III.4); the easy gluing is Alexander's trick, the pasting lemma and compact-to-Hausdorff, and the cone is where smoothness is lost (§III.3).
- **The torus** must stand upright with $r < R$; its four critical points, indices and normal forms are asserted by Milnor and verified by Lagrange's rule (§III.6).
- **Application 2** needs a metric, $\operatorname{sgn}\det = (-1)^{\operatorname{index}}$, and the replacement of $F$ by $F \circ r$, because the Hessian of $F|_N$ is not the restriction of that of $F$ (§III.7, §III.8); its function must be a Morse function, and *isolated* in §5 is redundant (§III.9).
- **§6, possible misprints and gaps:** $K_1, \dots, K_k$ for $K_1, \dots, K_n$; nullity for rank; normal frames only local, and unnecessary (§III.12); $t = 0$ and $p = q$ set aside (§III.14, §III.18); $> 1/t$ for $\ge 1/t$, equality being excluded exactly by non-degeneracy (§III.18). The shape operator removes the normalized coordinates (§III.14).
- **Corollaries 6.7 and 6.8** need Hausdorff, second countable manifolds and a closed embedding, and fixed charts for derivatives; the boundedness of $f$ in 6.8 is unnecessary (§III.16, §III.17).
- **Abstract manifolds** need two facts Milnor never states: the image of an embedding (not merely of an injective immersion) is a submanifold diffeomorphic to the manifold, and the Morse notions are invariant under diffeomorphisms (§III.19, §III.20).

Not formalized: Remarks 1 and 2 on Reeb's theorem; the sublevel sets (2)–(4) of the torus and its cell attachments; Milnor's own constructions in Corollary 6.8; Theorem 6.1 for $C^1$ maps between manifolds; Corollary 6.7 for non-compact abstract manifolds and Application 1; and the independence of the shape operator from the parametrization, which nothing uses.
