## Part IX. The full path space and the fundamental theorem (Milnor §17)

Milnor's §17 draws the consequences of the finite-dimensional approximation of §16. Theorem 17.1 compares the space $\Omega$ of piecewise smooth paths with the space $\Omega^*$ of all continuous paths. Theorem 17.3, the fundamental theorem of Morse theory, assembles the sublevel sets $\Omega^a$ into one countable cell complex with a $\lambda$-cell for each geodesic of index $\lambda$. The example of the round sphere gives Corollary 17.4 and the homology of $\Omega S^n$ for $n > 2$, and Corollary 17.5 draws a consequence for manifolds with the homotopy type of a sphere. This part retells the formal proofs of everything in §17 that is formalized. The setting is the embedded one of Parts VII and VIII: $N \subseteq V$ is a slice submanifold of a finite-dimensional real inner product space (§0.1, §0.3), with the induced metric and its distance $\rho$; $\Omega = \Omega(N; p, q)$ carries Milnor's metric $d$, and $\Omega^*$ the topology of uniform convergence (§0.8). Every Riemannian manifold is such an $N$ by Nash's embedding theorem, which is quoted, not proved.

The formal route departs from Milnor's in four places.

- **Theorem 17.1 and the book's one genuine error.** Milnor proves 17.1 with a map $h : \Omega^* \to \Omega$ that replaces a continuous path by the broken geodesic through its values at the multiples of a number $A(\omega)$, and asserts without proof that $h$ is continuous (p. 94). For the metric $d$ of $\Omega$, the topology in which the theorem is stated, this is false. §IX.12 retells the formal proof that it is false, for Milnor's own construction on $M = \mathbb R$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · milnor_h_not_continuous⟧. The theorem is true, and §§IX.13–9.18 retell its formal proof by another route: dyadic subdivisions kept fixed on open sets $Y_k \subseteq \Omega^*$, convex *good balls* each carrying one smooth minimal-geodesic map, and Theorem A of the Appendix applied through telescopes ⟦Chapter10/FullPathTheorem · theorem_17_1⟧.
- **Theorem 17.3 by Milnor's second route.** Milnor's main proof of 17.3 uses Whitehead's theorem and Corollary 17.2, which rests on 17.1. The formal proof takes the alternative through the Appendix, to which Milnor refers and which the Appendix carries out (pp. 149–150). It builds a tower of relative cell attachments that commutes only up to homotopy, analyses each step with its own manifold of broken geodesics, and needs neither Whitehead's theorem nor 17.2 (§§IX.2–9.6).
- **Homology without cellular chains.** The homology of $\Omega(S^n; p, q)$ is computed from the filtration of $\Omega$ by the sets $\Omega^a$, the compactness of singular chains, and the lacunary principle (§§IX.8–9.10). The cell complexes of 17.3 have arbitrary attaching maps, not cellular ones, so cellular homology is not available.
- **The sphere in full, for paths rather than loops.** The geodesics of $S^n$ from $p$ to $q \ne \pm p$, their conjugate points with multiplicities, and their indices are all derived (§IX.7). Corollary 17.4 is proved for the path space $\Omega(S^n; p, q)$, which is what Milnor's argument gives; the passage to the loop space needs a lemma moving an end point, which is not formalized.

Not formalized: Corollary 17.2, Theorem 17.3 for $\Omega^*$, the loop-space form of 17.4, the homology of $\Omega S^2$, integral homology, Corollary 17.5 itself, and Serre's remark. What is formalized about 17.5 is that its printed hypotheses are not enough: completeness, which Milnor omits there, cannot be dropped (§IX.11).

**Map.** Paths are relative to `Morse/`.

- `Chapter10/PathHomology`: the homology of a slab of $\Omega$; one geodesic per window; Betti numbers.

### IX.1 The setting

*The submanifold.* $N \subseteq V$ is a slice submanifold modelled on $Z$, with complementary space $W$ ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · IsSliceSubmanifold⟧; $V$, $Z$ and $W$ are finite-dimensional. The distance $\rho(x, y)$ is the infimum of the lengths of piecewise smooth paths in $N$ from $x$ to $y$, and $\infty$ if there is none ⟦Chapter7/Distance · riemDist⟧. It dominates the norm of $V$, $\|x - y\| \le \rho(x, y)$ ⟦Chapter7/Metric · ofReal_norm_sub_le_riemDist⟧, and its balls form a basis of the neighbourhoods of a point in $N$ ⟦Chapter7/Distance · hasBasis_nhdsWithin_riemDist⟧. Completeness, where it is needed, is assumed as closedness of $N$ in $V$, which makes the closed $\rho$-balls about $p$ compact ⟦Chapter10/Broken · isCompact_closedRiemBall_of_isClosed⟧.

*Piecewise smooth paths.* A path of $\Omega(N; p, q)$ is a function $c : \mathbb R \to V$ that is piecewise smooth on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter7/Path · IsPiecewiseSmooth⟧, takes values in $N$ there, and has $c(0) = p$, $c(1) = q$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · pathSpace⟧. Its values off $[0, 1]$ play no role. Its energy is $E(c) = \int_0^1 \|c'\|^2\,dt$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · energy⟧. The type of these paths carries Milnor's metric
$$d(\alpha, \beta) = \max_{t \in [0,1]} \rho(\alpha(t), \beta(t)) + \Big(\int_0^1 \big(\|\alpha'(t)\| - \|\beta'(t)\|\big)^2\,dt\Big)^{1/2}$$
⟦Chapter10/PathMetric · PathSp⟧ ⟦Chapter10/PathMetric · supDist⟧ ⟦Chapter10/PathMetric · l2Dist⟧ ⟦Chapter10/PathMetric · instPseudoEMetricSpace⟧, where the second term compares the speeds. It is an extended *pseudo*metric: two functions that agree on $[0, 1]$ are at distance $0$ ⟦Chapter10/Omega · edist_eq_zero_of_eqOn⟧, so $\Omega$ is not Hausdorff. This matters twice in this part, in the gluing of §IX.18 and in a homotopy of §IX.17, and two small facts handle it. A function that agrees on $[0, 1]$ with a path of $\Omega$ is itself in $\Omega$ ⟦Chapter10/Deformation · pathSpace_congr⟧. A map into $\Omega$ at distance $0$ from a continuous map is continuous ⟦Chapter10/Deformation · continuous_of_edist_eq_zero⟧.

*The energy.* By Lemma 16.A of the exposition, $\sqrt{E_a^b}$ is $1$-Lipschitz for $d$ whenever $0 \le a \le b \le 1$ ⟦Chapter10/PathMetric · lipschitzWith_sqrt_energy⟧, so the energy is continuous on $\Omega$ ⟦Chapter10/PathMetric · continuous_energy⟧ (Part VIII). As a bundled continuous map it is written $E : \Omega \to \mathbb R$ ⟦Chapter10/FundamentalTheorem · energyC⟧, and its sublevel sets are $\Omega^a = \{E \le a\}$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · sublevel⟧ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · sublevel_mono⟧ (§0.4).

*Geodesics.* A geodesic of $\Omega$ is an unbroken geodesic: smooth on $[0, 1]$, with zero tangential acceleration on $(0, 1)$ ⟦Chapter8/Critical · IsUnbrokenGeodesic⟧. At a geodesic $\gamma$ the Hessian $E_{**}$ has an index ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · energyHessian⟧ ⟦Chapter9/FormIndex · formIndex⟧ and a nullity ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · hessNullity⟧ (§0.9, Part VII). The point $q$ lies in the *conjugate locus* of $p$ if some geodesic from $p$ to $q$ has $0$ and $1$ conjugate along it ⟦Chapter11/NonConjugate · conjLocus⟧. Outside the conjugate locus every geodesic from $p$ to $q$ has nullity $0$, by Theorem 14.1 ⟦Chapter11/NonConjugate · hessNullity_eq_zero_of_notMem_conjLocus⟧. This is Milnor's hypothesis in 17.3.

*The space of continuous paths.* $\Omega^* = \Omega^*(N; p, q)$ is the set of continuous maps $\omega : [0, 1] \to V$ with values in $N$, $\omega(0) = p$, $\omega(1) = q$, as a subspace of the space of continuous maps $[0, 1] \to V$ with the uniform distance $d^*$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathSpace · pathCSet⟧ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathSpace · PathC⟧. That is the compact-open topology. Milnor measures $d^*$ with $\rho$ and the formalization with the norm of $V$; on $N$ the two distances define the same topology, and the inequality $\|x - y\| \le \rho(x, y)$ is what is used. The inclusion $i : \Omega \to \Omega^*$ restricts a path to $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathSpace · toPathC⟧. It satisfies $d^*(i\gamma, i\gamma') \le d(\gamma, \gamma')$, because $\|\gamma(t) - \gamma'(t)\| \le \rho(\gamma(t), \gamma'(t))$ is bounded by the first term of $d$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathSpace · edist_toPathC_le⟧. So $i$ is Lipschitz, hence continuous ⟦Chapter10/FullPathSpace · continuous_toPathC⟧; as a continuous map it is written $i$ throughout ⟦Chapter10/FullPathTheorem · pathIncl⟧.

*Homotopy equivalences.* A continuous $f$ is a homotopy equivalence if it has a homotopy inverse ⟦Appendix/Telescope · IsHomotopyEquiv⟧. Mathlib's bundled homotopy equivalences carry the inverse and both homotopies as data ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Homotopy/Equiv · ContinuousMap.HomotopyEquiv⟧. The inclusion of a strong deformation retract (§0.4) is a homotopy equivalence ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsStrongDeformationRetract.exists_homotopyEquiv⟧.

### IX.2 Relative cell complexes

Milnor proves 17.3 by referring to the proof of Theorem 3.5 (p. 95). Theorem 3.5 is about one sublevel set at a time: $M^a$ has the homotopy type of a cell complex. The tower of 17.3 needs a statement about *pairs* $\Omega^{a_n} \subseteq \Omega^{a_{n+1}}$, with control of the inclusion. The formalization introduces the notion that carries this control and proves the three lemmas it needs.

*Adjunction spaces.* For a space $X$ and a continuous $\varphi : S^{n-1} \to X$, the space $X \cup_\varphi e^n$ is the pushout in the category of topological spaces of $\varphi$ and the inclusion $S^{n-1} \to D^n$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · AttachCell⟧, with its inclusion $X \to X \cup_\varphi e^n$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · cellInl⟧ (§0.5, Part II). An *iterated adjunction space* with cells $(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k)$ is obtained from the empty space by attaching a $\lambda_1$-cell, then a $\lambda_2$-cell, and so on, each along an arbitrary continuous map ⟦Chapter2/CWType · IsCellAttachment⟧; a space has the homotopy type of a finite cell complex with these cells if it is homotopy equivalent to one ⟦Chapter2/CWType · IsFiniteCellComplexType⟧. The relative version starts from a space $L$ instead of the empty space: $K$ is obtained from $L$ by attaching cells of the listed dimensions one at a time, and $j : L \to K$ is the composite of the inclusions ⟦Appendix/MonotoneUnion · IsRelCellAttachment⟧.

**Definition.** A continuous map $\iota : A \to X$ is, *up to homotopy, the inclusion of $A$ with cells $(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k)$ attached*, if there are a relative cell attachment $j : A \to K$ with these cells and a homotopy equivalence $e : X \to K$ with $e \circ \iota \simeq j$ ⟦Chapter10/RelativeCells · IsRelCellComplexType⟧. For subsets $A \subseteq B$ the map $\iota$ is usually the inclusion of subspaces ⟦Chapter10/RelativeCells · inclC⟧.

Four bookkeeping facts follow at once. A homotopy equivalence $e : A \to X$ is such a map with no cells: take $K = A$, $j = \mathrm{id}$ and $e^{-1}$ ⟦Chapter10/RelativeCells · IsRelCellComplexType.of_homotopyEquiv⟧. The property depends only on the homotopy class of $\iota$ ⟦Chapter10/RelativeCells · IsRelCellComplexType.congr⟧, and in particular only on $\iota$ as a function ⟦Chapter10/RelativeCells · IsRelCellComplexType.of_eq⟧. If $\iota$ has it and $e' : X \to Y$ is a homotopy equivalence, then $e' \circ \iota$ has it, with the homotopy equivalence $e \circ e'^{-1} : Y \to K$, since $e e'^{-1} e' \iota \simeq e \iota \simeq j$ ⟦Chapter10/RelativeCells · IsRelCellComplexType.comp_homotopyEquiv⟧. For subsets $A \subseteq B \subseteq C$, if $A \to B$ has the property and the inclusion $B \to C$ is a homotopy equivalence, then $A \to C$ has it with the same cells ⟦Chapter10/RelativeCells · IsRelCellComplexType.mono⟧. This last form is how Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 enter: they enlarge a sublevel set by an inclusion that is a homotopy equivalence.

**Lemma 3.7, relative and iterated.** Let $j : L \to K$ attach cells $(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k)$ to $L$, and let $e : L \to L'$ be a homotopy equivalence. Then the same cells can be attached to $L'$, giving $j' : L' \to K'$, and there is a homotopy equivalence $E : K \to K'$ with $E \circ j = j' \circ e$ exactly ⟦Chapter10/RelativeCells · IsRelCellAttachment.transport⟧.

*The proof as formalized* is an induction on the attachment. With no cells, $K' = L'$, $j' = \mathrm{id}$ and $E = e$. For the inductive step, suppose $K = K_1 \cup_\varphi e^n$ and that $E_1 : K_1 \to K'_1$ has been built with $E_1 j_1 = j'_1 e$. Lemma 3.7 (Part II) extends the homotopy equivalence $E_1$ over the new cell: it gives a homotopy equivalence $F : K_1 \cup_\varphi e^n \to K'_1 \cup_{E_1\varphi} e^n$ that is $E_1$ on $K_1$ ⟦Chapter2/Attaching · lemma_3_7⟧. Attach the cell to $K'_1$ along $E_1 \circ \varphi$. Then $F(j x) = F(j_1 x) = E_1(j_1 x) = j'_1(e x)$, which is the required identity. Milnor uses Lemma 3.7 one cell at a time in the proof of Theorem 3.5; the lemma packages that use, relative to a base space.

**Invariance under homotopy-commutative squares.** Let $\iota' : A' \to X'$ be, up to homotopy, the inclusion of $A'$ with cells attached, and let $\alpha : A \to A'$ and $\beta : X \to X'$ be homotopy equivalences with $\beta \circ \iota \simeq \iota' \circ \alpha$. Then $\iota$ is, up to homotopy, the inclusion of $A$ with the same cells attached ⟦Chapter10/RelativeCells · IsRelCellComplexType.of_square⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Let $j : A' \to K$ and $e : X' \to K$ be the data for $\iota'$. Transport the attachment along the homotopy inverse $\alpha^{-1} : A' \to A$ by the previous lemma: this gives $j'' : A \to K''$ with the same cells and a homotopy equivalence $E : K \to K''$ with $E j = j'' \alpha^{-1}$. The homotopy equivalence for $\iota$ is $E \circ e \circ \beta : X \to K''$, and
$$E e \beta \iota \simeq E e \iota' \alpha \simeq E j \alpha = j'' \alpha^{-1}\alpha \simeq j''.$$
Only homotopy commutativity of the square is used. That is the point of the notion: the homotopy equivalences between $\Omega^a$ and models of it need not commute with the inclusions on the nose.

**Attaching a cell.** If $\iota : A \to X$ has the property with cells $c$ and $\varphi : S^{n-1} \to X$ is continuous, then $A \to X \cup_\varphi e^n$ has it with cells $c$ followed by $n$ ⟦Chapter10/RelativeCells · IsRelCellComplexType.attach⟧. Lemma 3.7 extends $e : X \to K$ to a homotopy equivalence $F : X \cup_\varphi e^n \to K \cup_{e\varphi} e^n$ that agrees with $e$ on $X$, so $F \circ (\text{inclusion}) \circ \iota = (\text{inclusion}) \circ e \circ \iota \simeq (\text{inclusion}) \circ j$, and the new relative attachment is $j$ followed by the new cell.

**Lemma D, relative.** Let $A_0 \subseteq A$ be subsets of a Hausdorff space with $A$ closed. Let $\chi_i : \mathbb R^{k_i} \to M$, for $i$ in a list $l$, be continuous and injective on the closed unit ball $D^{k_i}$, with pairwise disjoint images $\chi_i(D^{k_i})$, each meeting $A$ exactly in the image of the unit sphere: $\chi_i(z) \in A$ iff $\|z\| = 1$, for $z \in D^{k_i}$. If $A_0 \to A$ is, up to homotopy, the inclusion with cells $c$, then $A_0 \to A \cup \bigcup_{i \in l} \chi_i(D^{k_i})$ is, up to homotopy, the inclusion with cells $c$ followed by $k_i$, $i \in l$ ⟦Chapter10/RelativeCells · isRelCellComplexType_union_cells⟧.

*The proof as formalized* is an induction on the list. For the empty list, $A \cup \emptyset = A$ and nothing is attached. For a list $i :: l$, the case of one cell of Lemma D (Part II) identifies $A \cup \chi_i(D^{k_i})$ with the adjunction space $A \cup_\varphi e^{k_i}$, $\varphi = \chi_i|S^{k_i-1}$: they form a pushout square in which $A \to A \cup \chi_i(D^{k_i})$ is the inclusion ⟦Chapter2/CellPatch · exists_isPushout_union_cell_of_t2⟧. So the lemma on attaching a cell applies, followed by the homeomorphism from the pushout to the union, which composed with the inclusion of $A$ is the inclusion of $A$. The new set $A' = A \cup \chi_i(D^{k_i})$ is closed, since a compact image is closed in a Hausdorff space. The remaining cells still meet $A'$ exactly in their boundary spheres, because they are disjoint from $\chi_i(D^{k_i})$. The induction hypothesis for $l$ and $A'$ finishes, after rewriting $A' \cup \bigcup_{j \in l} = A \cup \bigcup_{j \in i :: l}$.

### IX.3 The relative Theorem 3.5

Milnor's Theorem 3.5 and Remark 3.3 describe one sublevel set. The tower of 17.3 needs the inclusion of one sublevel set into another, and on the manifold of broken geodesics, which is not compact: only its sublevel sets below the energy bound are. The formalization states the relative Theorem 3.5 under exactly that hypothesis, for $f = F|N$ with $F : V \to \mathbb R$ smooth, $N \subseteq V$ a slice submanifold, and a level $a_0$ with $N^{a_0}$ compact.

**One critical level, relative to a lower level.** Let $S$ be a finite set of non-degenerate critical points of $f$, all with value $c$. Let $b \le a' < c < a \le a_0$, assume that every critical point outside $S$ has value $< a'$ or $> a$, and that $N^{a_0}$ is compact. If $N^b \to N^{a'}$ is, up to homotopy, the inclusion with cells $c$, then there is a list $l$ of the points of $S$, without repetition, such that $N^b \to N^a$ is, up to homotopy, the inclusion with cells $c$ followed by the indices of the points of $l$ ⟦Chapter10/RelativeCells · IsRelCellComplexType.sublevel_step_of_isCompact⟧.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. Every slab $f^{-1}[\beta, \beta']$ with $\beta' \le a_0$ is compact, as a closed subset of $N^{a_0}$. Put $\varepsilon_0 = \min(c - a', a - c) > 0$. The slab $[c - \varepsilon_0, c + \varepsilon_0]$ is compact, and its only critical points are those of $S$.
2. Remark 3.3 for a submanifold (Part II) gives $\varepsilon_1 > 0$ such that, for $0 < \varepsilon \le \varepsilon_1$, there are cells $\chi_p(D^{\lambda_p})$, $p \in S$, $\lambda_p$ the index of $p$. Each is continuous and injective on the ball, meets $N^{c-\varepsilon}$ exactly in the image of the sphere, and lies in $N^{c+\varepsilon}$; they are pairwise disjoint; and $N^{c-\varepsilon} \cup \bigcup_p \chi_p(D^{\lambda_p})$ is a strong deformation retract of $N^{c+\varepsilon}$ ⟦Chapter2/SeveralCritical · remark_3_3_submanifold⟧. Take $\varepsilon = \min(\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_0/2)$, so that $a' < c - \varepsilon$ and $c + \varepsilon < a$.
3. Theorem 3.1 on the compact slabs $[a', c - \varepsilon]$ and $[c + \varepsilon, a]$, which contain no critical points, makes the inclusions $N^{a'} \to N^{c-\varepsilon}$ and $N^{c+\varepsilon} \to N^a$ homotopy equivalences ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · theorem_3_1_submanifold⟧.
4. By the first inclusion and the enlargement fact of §IX.2, $N^b \to N^{c-\varepsilon}$ has cells $c$. List the points of $S$ without repetition as $l$. Lemma D, relative, attaches the cells $\chi_p$ one at a time to the closed set $N^{c-\varepsilon}$ (closed since $f$ is continuous), so $N^b \to N^{c-\varepsilon} \cup \bigcup_{p} \chi_p(D^{\lambda_p})$ has cells $c$ followed by the $\lambda_p$, $p \in l$.
5. The strong deformation retraction of step 2 makes the inclusion of that union into $N^{c+\varepsilon}$ a homotopy equivalence, and step 3 makes $N^{c+\varepsilon} \to N^a$ one. Two more uses of the enlargement fact give the claim.

**The relative Theorem 3.5.** Let $N^{a_0}$ be compact, every critical point of $f$ in $N^{a_0}$ non-degenerate, and $b \le a_0$ with neither $b$ nor $a_0$ a critical value. Then the critical points of $f$ in $f^{-1}(b, a_0)$ can be listed without repetition as $p_1, \dots, p_j$ with $f(p_1) \le \dots \le f(p_j)$, so that the inclusion $N^b \to N^{a_0}$ is, up to homotopy, the inclusion of $N^b$ with cells of dimensions $\operatorname{index}(p_1), \dots, \operatorname{index}(p_j)$ attached in this order ⟦Chapter10/RelativeCells · isRelCellComplexType_sublevel_of_isCompact⟧.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. *Finitely many critical points.* The critical points of $f$ in $N^{a_0}$ form a compact set, as a closed subset of a compact one ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · isClosed_setOf_isCriticalPointM⟧, and a discrete one, since non-degenerate critical points are isolated ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · isDiscrete_inter_setOf_isCriticalPointM⟧. So they form a finite set $C$.
2. *Induction.* The statement is proved for every level $a \le a_0$ that is not a critical value, by strong induction on the number $m$ of points of $C$ with value in $(b, a)$.
3. *No critical point in $(b, a)$.* Theorem 3.1 on the compact slab $[b, a]$ makes the inclusion $N^b \to N^a$ a homotopy equivalence, which is a relative cell complex with no cells.
4. *Otherwise* let $p$ be a point of $C$ with the largest value $c \in (b, a)$, and $S$ the points of $C$ at level $c$. Choose a level $a' \in [b, c)$ that is not a critical value and lies above every other critical value in $(b, c)$. If there are such values, let $q_0$ have the largest of them and put $a' = (f(q_0) + c)/2$; a critical point at that level would lie strictly between $f(q_0)$ and $c$. If there are none, put $a' = b$.
5. Every critical point outside $S$ then has value $< a'$ or $> a$, and the points with value in $(b, a')$ are those with value in $(b, a)$ minus $S$, strictly fewer. The induction hypothesis gives the list for $(b, a')$ and the relative cell structure of $N^b \to N^{a'}$. The one-level step adds the points of $S$, all at the level $c$, which is above everything already listed. Concatenating keeps the list sorted and without repetition.

The level $a_0$ plays two roles: its sublevel set is compact, and all the hypotheses concern critical points in it. Nothing is assumed about critical points above $a_0$, of which there may be infinitely many.

### IX.4 Towers and countable cell complexes

Milnor's conclusion of 17.3 is a countable CW complex $K = \bigcup K_i$ with homotopy equivalences $\Omega^{a_i} \to K_i$ whose restrictions to the smaller sublevel sets are the previous maps, a strictly commutative ladder (p. 95). The formalization replaces both the complex and the ladder.

*The countable cell complex* is given by its finite stages, as in the Appendix (Part XI). A *cell tower* with cells $\kappa_0, \kappa_1, \dots$ is a sequence $K_0 \to K_1 \to \cdots$ in which $K_0$ is an iterated adjunction space with cells $\kappa_0$ and each map $K_n \to K_{n+1}$ is a relative cell attachment with cells $\kappa_{n+1}$ ⟦Appendix/MonotoneUnion · CellTower⟧. A tower may be given by its stages and the maps between them ⟦Appendix/MonotoneUnion · CellTower.ofSequence⟧ ⟦Appendix/MonotoneUnion · CellTower.tmap_ofSequence⟧. A space $X$ *has the homotopy type of the countable cell complex* with these cells if it is homotopy equivalent to the mapping telescope of such a tower ⟦Appendix/MonotoneUnion · IsCountableCellComplexType⟧. The telescope stands for the union $\bigcup K_n$ with the weak topology. The two are homotopy equivalent by Example 2 of the Appendix, which rests on Whitehead's theorem and is not formalized; that is why the telescope, and not the union, appears in the formal statement. The attaching maps are arbitrary continuous maps, since cellular approximation is not in Mathlib.

*The comparison.* A space $X$ is the *homotopy direct limit* of subsets $X_0 \subseteq X_1 \subseteq \cdots$ if the projection to $X$ of Milnor's telescope $X_\Sigma = \bigcup_n X_n \times [n, n+1] \subseteq X \times \mathbb R$ is a homotopy equivalence ⟦Appendix/Telescope · IsHomotopyDirectLimit⟧ (Part XI). The Appendix proves that if $X$ is the homotopy direct limit of the $X_n$ and $g_n : X_n \to K_n$ are homotopy equivalences to the stages of a cell tower with $c_n \circ g_n \simeq g_{n+1}$ on $X_n$, where $c_n : K_n \to K_{n+1}$ is the map of the tower, then $X$ has the homotopy type of the countable cell complex ⟦Appendix/MonotoneUnion · isCountableCellComplexType_of_tower⟧ ⟦Appendix/MonotoneUnion · nonempty_homotopyEquiv_mappingTelescope⟧. Only homotopy-commutative squares are needed. This is the formal form of Milnor's alternative proof on p. 150, and it is what makes a strictly commuting ladder unnecessary.

*The inductive construction.* A *stage* $n$ of the construction is a space $K$ with a homotopy equivalence $g : X_n \to K$ ⟦Chapter10/RelativeCells · TowerStage⟧. A *step* from a stage, with cells $c$, is a space $K'$, a relative cell attachment $j : K \to K'$ with cells $c$, and a homotopy equivalence $g' : X_{n+1} \to K'$ with $g' \circ (X_n \subseteq X_{n+1}) \simeq j \circ g$ ⟦Chapter10/RelativeCells · TowerStep⟧ ⟦Appendix/Telescope · up⟧.

**The inductive step** (Milnor p. 95). If the inclusion $X_n \to X_{n+1}$ is, up to homotopy, the inclusion with cells $c$ attached, then every stage $(K, g)$ at $n$ has a step with cells $c$ ⟦Chapter10/RelativeCells · nonempty_towerStep⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Let $j_A : X_n \to K_A$ and $e : X_{n+1} \to K_A$ be the data of the relative cell structure, with $e \circ (X_n \subseteq X_{n+1}) \simeq j_A$. Transport the attachment $j_A$ along the homotopy equivalence $g : X_n \to K$ (Lemma 3.7, relative): this gives $j' : K \to K'$ with the same cells and a homotopy equivalence $E : K_A \to K'$ with $E j_A = j' g$. Put $g' = E \circ e$. Then $g' \circ (X_n \subseteq X_{n+1}) = E \circ e \circ (X_n \subseteq X_{n+1}) \simeq E j_A = j' g$.

The stages are then defined by recursion, choosing a step at each stage ⟦Chapter10/RelativeCells · towerStages⟧ ⟦Chapter10/RelativeCells · towerStepAt⟧.

**The countable cell complex of a filtered space.** Let $X$ be the homotopy direct limit of $X_0 \subseteq X_1 \subseteq \cdots$. Suppose $X_0$ has the homotopy type of a finite cell complex with cells $\kappa_0$, and each inclusion $X_n \to X_{n+1}$ is, up to homotopy, the inclusion with cells $\kappa_{n+1}$ attached. Then $X$ has the homotopy type of the countable cell complex with cells $\kappa_0, \kappa_1, \dots$ ⟦Chapter10/RelativeCells · isCountableCellComplexType_of_rel⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Stage $0$ is the finite cell complex $K_0$ with a homotopy equivalence $X_0 \to K_0$. The recursion produces stages $K_n$, relative cell attachments $K_n \to K_{n+1}$ and homotopy equivalences $g_n : X_n \to K_n$ with homotopy-commutative squares. These stages and maps form a cell tower, and the comparison theorem applies, the map of the tower being the attachment $K_n \to K_{n+1}$ by construction.

**Sublevel sets.** If $E : X \to \mathbb R$ is continuous and $a_0 \le a_1 \le \cdots$ tend to $\infty$, then $X$ is the homotopy direct limit of the sublevel sets $X^{a_n}$, for every space $X$, with no paracompactness ⟦Appendix/Examples · isHomotopyDirectLimit_sublevel⟧. The proof in the Appendix uses the locally finite sum $f = \sum_j \min(1, \max(0, E - a_j + 1))$, which is continuous and is $\ge n + 1$ off $X^{a_n}$, so Lemma 2.1 of the exposition applies ⟦Appendix/Telescope · isHomotopyDirectLimit_of_level⟧. Consequently, for such levels, a finite cell complex at the bottom and relative cell structures on the inclusions $X^{a_n} \to X^{a_{n+1}}$ give $X$ the homotopy type of the countable cell complex ⟦Chapter10/RelativeCells · isCountableCellComplexType_of_relSublevel⟧.

*What Milnor leaves implicit.* His ladder asks for $g_{n+1}$ to extend $g_n$. Lemma 3.7 extends a homotopy equivalence over the cells of $X_n \cup e^{\lambda_1} \cup \cdots$, but $X_{n+1}$ is only homotopy equivalent to this space, by the retractions of Theorems 3.1 and 3.2, which move $X_n$. So the extension holds only up to homotopy. Strict compatibility can be arranged with nested subdivisions (Lemma 17.E of the exposition), and that is neither formalized nor needed: the comparison theorem only uses homotopy-commutative squares, which is exactly what the inductive step produces.

### IX.5 One step of the energy filtration

The input of the tower is the relative cell structure of each inclusion $\Omega^a \to \Omega^b$. It comes from the manifold of broken geodesics of Part VIII.

*Milnor's data* (Part VIII). For end points $p, q \in N$, an energy bound $c > 0$ such that the closed $\rho$-ball of radius $\sqrt c$ about $p$ is compact, and a sufficiently fine subdivision $0 = t_0 < \dots < t_{m+1} = 1$, the data of §16 are recorded in one structure ⟦Chapter10/Broken · BrokenSetup⟧. Such data exist, with a uniform subdivision, as soon as the ball is compact ⟦Chapter10/Broken · exists_brokenSetup_uniform⟧, which holds for every $c$ when $N$ is closed in $V$ ⟦Chapter10/Broken · isCompact_closedRiemBall_of_isClosed⟧. The break points $y = (x_1, \dots, x_m) \in N^m$ of broken geodesics of energy $< c$ form Milnor's open set $U$ ⟦Chapter10/Broken · BrokenSetup.nodes⟧. On it the energy of the broken geodesic with break points $y$ is $E'(y) = \sum_i \rho(x_i, x_{i+1})^2/(t_{i+1} - t_i)$ ⟦Chapter10/Broken · BrokenSetup.energy'⟧. The broken geodesic itself is a path of $\Omega$ ⟦Chapter10/Broken · BrokenSetup.path⟧ ⟦Chapter10/Broken · BrokenSetup.path_mem_pathSpace⟧ with energy $E'(y)$ ⟦Chapter10/Broken · BrokenSetup.energy_path⟧ and break points $y$ ⟦Chapter10/Broken · BrokenSetup.nodeVal_path⟧. Its index, as a natural number, is the index of $E_{**}$ at it ⟦Chapter10/Finite · BrokenSetup.geodIndex⟧.

*Four results of Part VIII are used.* (a) $E'$ agrees on the compact set $B^{c_1} = \{y \in U : E'(y) \le c_1\}$, $c_1 < c$, with a smooth function $F$ on all of $V^m$ ⟦Chapter10/Finite · BrokenSetup.exists_contDiff_eq_energy'⟧, and $\{y \in U : E'(y) < c_1\} = N^m \cap O$ for an open $O$ ⟦Chapter10/Finite · BrokenSetup.exists_isOpen_sublevel_lt⟧, which is a slice submanifold of $V^m$ ⟦Chapter10/Product · isSliceSubmanifold_pi_inter⟧. (b) The sublevel sets $B^b$, $b < c$, are compact ⟦Chapter10/BrokenEnergy · BrokenSetup.isCompact_sublevel⟧. (c) The critical points of $F$ on $S = N^m \cap O$ are exactly the break points of unbroken geodesics ⟦Chapter10/BrokenVariation · BrokenSetup.isCriticalPointM_iff⟧, and at such a point the index and nullity of the Hessian of $F|S$ are those of $E_{**}$ at the geodesic ⟦Chapter10/BrokenIndex · BrokenSetup.theorem_16_2_index⟧ (Theorem 16.2). (d) Every geodesic of energy $< c$ is, on $[0, 1]$, the broken geodesic of its break points, and those lie in $U$ ⟦Chapter10/Finite · BrokenSetup.eqOn_path_nodeVal_of_isUnbrokenGeodesic⟧. Finally, $\Omega^a$ and $B^a$ have the same homotopy type for $a < c$: Milnor's retraction $r_1$ ⟦Chapter10/Deformation · BrokenSetup.homotopyEquivBroken⟧ followed by the identification of broken geodesics with their break points ⟦Chapter10/Omega · BrokenSetup.homotopyEquivNodes⟧.

**The relative Theorem 16.3, in the space of break points.** Let $a \le b < c$ be levels that are not energies of geodesics, and let every geodesic of energy $\le b$ have nullity $0$. Then the break points of the geodesics with energy in $(a, b)$ form a list $\mathcal Y$ without repetition, ordered by non-decreasing energy, and the inclusion $B^a \to B^b$ is, up to homotopy, the inclusion of $B^a$ with one cell of dimension $\operatorname{index}(\gamma)$ attached for each of them, in this order ⟦Chapter10/FundamentalTheorem · BrokenSetup.isRelCellComplexType_nodes⟧.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. Put $c_1 = (b + c)/2$, so $b < c_1 < c$. Take the smooth extension $F$ of $E'$ from $B^{c_1}$ and the slice submanifold $S = N^m \cap O = \{y \in U : E'(y) < c_1\}$ of (a). On $S$, $F = E'$.
2. For $t \le b$, the sublevel set of $F|S$ at $t$ is $B^t$, since a point of $U$ with $E' \le t \le b$ lies in $S$. In particular $(F|S)^b = B^b$ is compact by (b).
3. By (c), the critical points of $F|S$ are the break points of geodesics, and their nullities are those of $E_{**}$, which are $0$ below $b$; so every critical point of $F|S$ in $(F|S)^b$ is non-degenerate ⟦Chapter1/Nondegenerate · isNondegenerate_iff_nullity_eq_zero⟧. Neither $a$ nor $b$ is a critical value, because neither is the energy of a geodesic.
4. The relative Theorem 3.5 (§IX.3), with $a_0 = b$, gives the sorted list of critical points in $(a, b)$ and the relative cell structure of $(F|S)^a \to (F|S)^b$, with cells the indices of the Hessian of $F|S$. By (c) these are the indices $\operatorname{index}(\gamma)$.
5. The identifications $B^t \cong (F|S)^t$, $t = a, b$, are homeomorphisms given by the same formula, $y \mapsto y$, so they commute with the inclusions. Invariance under squares (§IX.2) transports the relative cell structure to $B^a \to B^b$.

**One formula for all levels.** The homotopy equivalence $\Omega^a \to B^a$ is the composite of Milnor's retraction and the identification of a broken geodesic with its break points ⟦Chapter10/FundamentalTheorem · BrokenSetup.omegaEquivNodes⟧. As a map it is $\gamma \mapsto \big((r_1\gamma)(t_1), \dots, (r_1\gamma)(t_m)\big)$ for every $a < c$ ⟦Chapter10/FundamentalTheorem · BrokenSetup.omegaEquivNodes_apply⟧. Because one formula serves all levels, the square formed with the inclusions $\Omega^a \to \Omega^b$ and $B^a \to B^b$ commutes on the nose.

**The relative Theorem 16.3 for $\Omega$** (the step of Milnor's proof of 17.3). Under the same hypotheses, the inclusion $\Omega^a \to \Omega^b$ of sublevel sets of the energy on $\Omega$, with Milnor's metric, is, up to homotopy, the inclusion of $\Omega^a$ with one cell of dimension $\operatorname{index}(\gamma)$ attached for each geodesic with energy in $(a, b)$, in order of non-decreasing energy ⟦Chapter10/FundamentalTheorem · BrokenSetup.isRelCellComplexType_Omega⟧. The proof is invariance under squares, applied to the previous statement and the two homotopy equivalences $\Omega^a \to B^a$ and $\Omega^b \to B^b$.

*Where this departs from Milnor.* Milnor applies 16.2 with 3.3 and 3.7 to each slab and requires one critical value in it. Here an interval may contain any finite number of critical values, whose cells are attached in order of energy by the induction of §IX.3. Each step uses its own manifold of broken geodesics, with an energy bound $c > b$; no relation between the subdivisions of different steps is needed.

**Finitely many geodesics below each level.** If every geodesic of energy $\le b < c$ has nullity $0$, then the break points $y \in U$ of geodesics with $E'(y) \le b$ form a finite set ⟦Chapter10/FundamentalTheorem · BrokenSetup.finite_geodesics⟧. With $S$ and $F$ as above, they are the critical points of $F|S$ in the compact set $(F|S)^b$, which form a closed, discrete set by non-degeneracy, hence a finite one. A broken geodesic is determined by its values on $[0, 1]$, since those determine its break points ⟦Chapter10/FundamentalTheorem · BrokenSetup.eq_of_eqOn_path⟧. For $N$ closed in $V$, $p, q \in N$ and every geodesic from $p$ to $q$ of nullity $0$, it follows that the energies $\le b$ of geodesics from $p$ to $q$ form a finite set, for every $b$: take data with $c = \max(b, 0) + 1$ and map the finite set of break points by $E'$, using (d) ⟦Chapter10/FundamentalTheorem · finite_geodesicEnergies⟧. This is the discreteness of the critical values that Milnor draws from 16.3 at the start of the proof of 17.3.

**The index of a geodesic.** For $\gamma \in \Omega$ the index of $E_{**}$ at $\gamma$ is a priori in $\mathbb N \cup \{\infty\}$; the natural number $\operatorname{ind}(\gamma)$ is defined as its truncation ⟦Chapter10/FundamentalTheorem · geodesicIndex⟧. At a geodesic the index is finite by the index theorem (Theorem 15.1, Part VII) ⟦Chapter9/IndexTheorem · theorem_15_1⟧, so the natural number is the index ⟦Chapter10/FundamentalTheorem · geodesicIndex_eq⟧, and it agrees with the index of the broken geodesic with the same break points ⟦Chapter10/FundamentalTheorem · BrokenSetup.geodIndex_eq_geodesicIndex⟧.

### IX.6 Theorem 17.3

**Theorem 17.3 with given levels.** Let $N$ be closed in $V$, $p, q \in N$, and every geodesic from $p$ to $q$ of nullity $0$. Let $a_0 \le a_1 \le \cdots$ be levels tending to $\infty$, with $a_0 < 0$, none of them the energy of a geodesic. Then there are finite lists $G_0 = \emptyset, G_1, G_2, \dots$ of paths such that ⟦Chapter10/FundamentalTheorem · theorem_17_3_of_levels⟧:

- every entry of $G_{n+1}$ is a geodesic from $p$ to $q$ with energy in $(a_n, a_{n+1})$;
- every geodesic from $p$ to $q$ with energy in $(a_n, a_{n+1})$ agrees on $[0, 1]$ with an entry of $G_{n+1}$;
- no two entries of one list agree on $[0, 1]$, and each list is ordered by non-decreasing energy;
- $\Omega(N; p, q)$, with Milnor's metric, has the homotopy type of the countable cell complex with one cell of dimension $\operatorname{index}(\gamma)$ for each entry $\gamma$ of $G_0$, then $G_1$, and so on.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. For each $n$ choose data of §16 with the energy bound $c_n = \max(a_{n+1}, 0) + 1 > a_{n+1}$. They exist because $N$ is closed, so the $\rho$-balls about $p$ are compact.
2. With the data $B_n$, the relative Theorem 16.3 for $\Omega$ applies to $a_n \le a_{n+1} < c_n$: the levels are not energies of geodesics, and the nullities are $0$. It gives a sorted list $\mathcal Y_n$ of break points and the relative cell structure of $\Omega^{a_n} \to \Omega^{a_{n+1}}$.
3. Put $G_0 = \emptyset$ and let $G_{n+1}$ be the list of broken geodesics with break points in $\mathcal Y_n$. Their energies are the $E'$-values, in $(a_n, a_{n+1})$. A geodesic with energy in $(a_n, a_{n+1}) \subseteq (-\infty, c_n)$ is, on $[0, 1]$, the broken geodesic of its break points by (d) of §IX.5, and these break points are in $\mathcal Y_n$. Two entries that agree on $[0, 1]$ have the same break points, so they are equal entries, and $\mathcal Y_n$ has no repetition.
4. *The tower.* $E$ is continuous on $\Omega$ and the $a_n$ tend to $\infty$. $\Omega^{a_0}$ is empty, since $a_0 < 0 \le E$, so it has the homotopy type of the complex with no cells ⟦Chapter2/CWType · IsFiniteCellComplexType.of_isEmpty⟧. The steps are those of step 2, with the indices read as natural numbers. The countable cell complex of sublevel sets (§IX.4) concludes.

**Theorem 17.3** (Milnor p. 95). Let $N$ be closed in $V$, $p, q \in N$, and every geodesic from $p$ to $q$ of nullity $0$. Then there are finite lists $G_0, G_1, \dots$ of geodesics from $p$ to $q$ such that every geodesic from $p$ to $q$ agrees on $[0, 1]$ with an entry of some list; entries of different lists never agree on $[0, 1]$, so the list is unique; no two entries of one list agree on $[0, 1]$; the energies increase strictly from one list to the next and weakly within a list; and $\Omega(N; p, q)$, with Milnor's metric, has the homotopy type of the countable cell complex with one cell of dimension $\operatorname{index}(\gamma)$ for each entry $\gamma$ ⟦Chapter10/FundamentalTheorem · theorem_17_3⟧. That is, one $\lambda$-cell for each geodesic from $p$ to $q$ of index $\lambda$, attached in order of increasing energy.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. *The levels.* For each $n$ the interval $(n, n+1)$ is infinite and the energies $\le n + 1$ of geodesics form a finite set (§IX.5), so there is $x_n \in (n, n+1)$ that is not such an energy. Put $a_0 = -1$ and $a_{n+1} = x_n$. Then $n - 1 \le a_n < n$; the levels are monotone, tend to $\infty$, have $a_0 < 0$, and none is the energy of a geodesic (for $a_0$ because energies are $\ge 0$).
2. Theorem 17.3 with these levels gives the lists. Every entry lies in a list $G_{k+1}$, since $G_0$ is empty.
3. *Every geodesic appears.* For a geodesic $\gamma$, choose $K$ with $E(\gamma) + 1 < K$. Then $a_0 < E(\gamma) < a_K$, and as $E(\gamma)$ is none of the $a_k$ there is $k$ with $a_k < E(\gamma) < a_{k+1}$ ⟦Chapter10/FundamentalTheorem · exists_mem_Ioo_succ_of_lt⟧ (the least $j$ with $E(\gamma) < a_j$ is positive; take $k = j - 1$). So $\gamma$ agrees on $[0, 1]$ with an entry of $G_{k+1}$.
4. *Exactly one list.* Paths agreeing on $[0, 1]$ have the same energy ⟦Chapter8/Hessian · energy_congr⟧. Entries of $G_{k+1}$ and $G_{k'+1}$ have energies in $(a_k, a_{k+1})$ and $(a_{k'}, a_{k'+1})$, which are disjoint for $k \ne k'$ by monotonicity. The same observation gives the strict increase of energies between lists.

**With Milnor's hypothesis.** If $q$ is not conjugate to $p$ along any geodesic, every geodesic from $p$ to $q$ has nullity $0$ (Theorem 14.1, §IX.1), and the theorem applies ⟦Chapter10/FundamentalTheorem · theorem_17_3_nonconj⟧. By Corollary 18.2 this holds for almost every $q$ (Part X).

*What the proof needs that Milnor does not say, and what it does not need.*

- **The levels must tend to infinity**, or the sets $\Omega^{a_i}$ do not exhaust $\Omega$. Milnor's sequence $a_0 < a_1 < \cdots$ is not required to.
- **Exactly one critical value in each interval**, as Milnor requires (p. 95), is impossible when there are only finitely many geodesics, and at most one is what a cell-by-cell argument needs. The formal proof needs neither: the relative Theorem 3.5 attaches the cells of any finite number of critical values in one interval, in order of energy, so the levels can be taken in $(n, n+1)$ without looking at the critical values. The condition of at most one critical value returns in the homology computation of §IX.9, where the homology of a slab is read off from its cells only when they all sit at one critical level.
- **The tower commutes only up to homotopy** (§IX.4), and each step uses its own manifold of broken geodesics. Milnor's strict ladder would need nested subdivisions.
- **No Whitehead theorem, no Corollary 17.2.** Milnor's main route proves that $\Omega \to K$ induces isomorphisms of homotopy groups and invokes Whitehead's theorem and Corollary 17.2 ($\Omega$ has the homotopy type of a CW complex), which rests on 17.1 and on Milnor's 1959 paper. Neither is used: the Appendix's comparison of telescopes needs only that $\Omega$ is the homotopy direct limit of the $\Omega^{a_n}$, which holds because $E$ is continuous for $d$.
- **Completeness** enters only through the compactness of the $\rho$-balls about $p$, which follows from closedness of $N$ in $V$. Connectedness is not needed.
- **Geodesics are counted by their values on $[0, 1]$.** Paths are functions on $\mathbb R$, so the cells correspond to classes of geodesics that agree on $[0, 1]$.
- **Theorem 17.3 for $\Omega^*$** would follow by composing with Theorem 17.1 (§IX.18); it is not stated.

### IX.7 The geodesics of the round sphere

Milnor's example on pp. 95–96 lists the geodesics from $p$ to $q \ne p, p'$ on $S^n$ ($p'$ the antipode of $p$) as $\gamma_0, \gamma_1, \dots$, says that the interior of $\gamma_k$ meets $\{p, p'\}$ exactly $k$ times, each time at a point conjugate to $p$ with multiplicity $n - 1$, and concludes $\lambda(\gamma_k) = k(n-1)$. The formal proof derives each of these statements, plus the two that the argument needs and does not state: the list is complete, and $p$ and $q$ are not conjugate along any geodesic.

*The sphere.* $S^n \subseteq \mathbb R^{n+1}$ is the unit sphere ⟦Milnor/Chapter5/HairyBall · Sph⟧ with its slice structure ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/EulerSphere · sphSub⟧ and the induced metric. Its tangent projection is $P_x v = v - \langle x, v\rangle x$ ⟦Chapter6/SphereCurvature · tanProj_sph⟧, its second fundamental form $\mathrm{II}_x(v, w) = -\langle v, w\rangle x$ ⟦Chapter6/SphereCurvature · secondFundForm_sph⟧, and its curvature tensor, with Milnor's sign, $R(a, b)c = \langle a, c\rangle b - \langle b, c\rangle a$ on tangent vectors ⟦Chapter6/SphereCurvature · curvOp_sph⟧ (Part V). In what follows $n \ge 1$, which is the hypothesis wherever a unit vector orthogonal to a given one must exist.

**Great circles.** For $p \in S^n$, a unit vector $u \perp p$ and $L \in \mathbb R$, the *great circle* is $c(t) = \cos(Lt)\,p + \sin(Lt)\,u$ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · greatCircle⟧. Its velocity is $c'(t) = L(\cos(Lt)\,u - \sin(Lt)\,p)$ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · hasDerivAt_greatCircle⟧, of norm $|L|$ and orthogonal to $c(t)$, and $c'' = -L^2 c$ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · deriv_deriv_greatCircle⟧. Since $c'' = -\|c'\|^2 c = \mathrm{II}(c', c')$, the acceleration is normal and $c$ is a geodesic on all of $\mathbb R$ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · isGeodesicOn_greatCircle⟧ ⟦Chapter7/Geodesic · isGeodesicOn_of_deriv_deriv_eq⟧. Its energy on $[0, 1]$ is $L^2$ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · energy_greatCircle⟧.

**Every geodesic from $p$ is a great circle.** If $\gamma \in \Omega(S^n; p, q)$ is a geodesic, there are a unit vector $u \perp p$ and $L \ge 0$ with $\gamma = c$ on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · exists_eqOn_greatCircle⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Milnor's assertion that the geodesics are great circles rests on uniqueness of geodesics, but $\gamma$ is only given on $[0, 1]$, where its velocity at $0$ is a one-sided derivative. So the proof first extends the segment past its ends: $\gamma$ agrees on $[0, 1]$ with the radial geodesic $t \mapsto \exp_p(t v)$, $v = \gamma'(0)$, which is a geodesic on some $(-\eta, 1 + \eta)$ ⟦Chapter9/GeodesicVariation · exists_initial_ray⟧. Its derivative at $0$ is $v$, by uniqueness of derivatives within $[0, 1]$, and $v$ is tangent at $p$, so $\langle p, v\rangle = 0$. Let $u = v/\|v\|$, or any unit vector orthogonal to $p$ if $v = 0$ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · exists_unit_orthogonal⟧, and $L = \|v\|$. The extension and the great circle are geodesics on $(-\eta, 1 + \eta)$ with the same position and velocity at $0$, so they agree there ⟦Chapter7/Geodesic · IsGeodesicOn.eqOn⟧.

**Two Jacobi fields.** Let $c$ be a great circle and $e$ a vector orthogonal to $p$ and $u$. Then $J(t) = \sin(Lt)\,e$ is a Jacobi field along $c$ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · isJacobiField_sin_smul⟧. Indeed $e \perp c(t)$ for all $t$, so $J$ is tangent and the covariant derivative is the ordinary one: $DJ/dt = L\cos(Lt)\,e$ and $D^2J/dt^2 = -L^2 J$. As $J \perp c'$ and $\|c'\|^2 = L^2$, the curvature term is $R(c', J)c' = \|c'\|^2 J - \langle J, c'\rangle c' = L^2 J$, and the Jacobi equation $D^2J/dt^2 + R(c', J)c' = 0$ holds ⟦Chapter8/Jacobi · IsJacobiField⟧. Milnor obtains such fields from rotations and Lemma 14.3; the formal proof checks the equation directly. The field $t\,c'(t)$ is a Jacobi field along any geodesic ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · isJacobiField_smul_vel⟧, in particular along $c$ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · isJacobiField_smul_deriv_greatCircle⟧.

**Conjugate points along a great circle.** Let $L > 0$ and $0 < t \le 1$. The multiplicity of $0$ and $t$ as conjugate parameters along $c$ (the dimension of the space of Jacobi fields vanishing at both ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · conjSpace⟧ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · conjMultiplicity⟧) is $n - 1$ if $Lt$ is a positive multiple of $\pi$, and $0$ otherwise ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · conjMultiplicity_greatCircle⟧.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. Let $D$ send a Jacobi field $J$ of the conjugate space to its initial covariant derivative $DJ/dt(0)$. It is linear and injective: a Jacobi field with $J(0) = 0$ and $DJ/dt(0) = 0$ vanishes ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · jacobiSpace_eq_zero⟧ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSystem · IsJacobiField.eqOn_zero⟧. So the multiplicity is the dimension of the range of $D$.
2. Let $J$ be in the conjugate space and $y = DJ/dt(0)$. Then $y \perp p$, being tangent at $p$. Write $a = \langle u, y\rangle$ and $e = y - a u$, so $e \perp p, u$. The field $K = L^{-1}\sin(Ls)\,e + (a/L)\,s\,c'(s)$ is a Jacobi field (the two fields above, made fields on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · IsJacobiField.comp_projIcc_mem⟧) with $K(0) = 0$ and $DK/dt(0) = e + a u = y$. A Jacobi field is determined by its value and covariant derivative at $0$ ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · jacEval_bijective⟧, so $J = K$.
3. From $J(t) = 0$: $L^{-1}\sin(Lt)\,e + a t\,w(t) = 0$, with $w(t) = c'(t)/L$ a unit vector orthogonal to $e$. Pairing with $w(t)$ gives $a t = 0$, so $a = 0$ as $t > 0$. Then $\sin(Lt)\,e = 0$, so if $\sin(Lt) \ne 0$ then $e = 0$ and $y = 0$.
4. Conversely, if $\sin(Lt) = 0$, every $y \perp p, u$ is $DJ/dt(0)$ for $J = L^{-1}\sin(Ls)\,y$, which vanishes at $0$ and at $t$.
5. So the range of $D$ is $\{p, u\}^\perp$, of dimension $n - 1$ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · finrank_orthogonal_span_pair⟧, if $\sin(Lt) = 0$, and $0$ otherwise. As $Lt > 0$, $\sin(Lt) = 0$ means $Lt = k\pi$ with $k \in \mathbb N$.

On p. 80 Milnor obtains $n - 1$ independent Jacobi fields from rotations, which shows multiplicity at least $n - 1$, and only for the antipode at the end of a semicircle. The statement used on p. 96 needs it at $p$ and $p'$ after any number of half-turns, needs equality, and needs that no other point of the circle is conjugate to $p$. Determining the whole $n$-dimensional space of Jacobi fields vanishing at $0$, as above, gives all three.

**Milnor's geodesics $\gamma_k$.** For $p, q \in S^n$ with $q \ne \pm p$, let $\theta = \arccos\langle p, q\rangle \in (0, \pi)$ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · sphAngle⟧ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · sphAngle_pos⟧ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · sphAngle_lt_pi⟧, and $u = (q - \cos\theta\,p)/\sin\theta$ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · sphDir⟧, a unit vector orthogonal to $p$ with $q = \cos\theta\,p + \sin\theta\,u$ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · norm_sphDir⟧ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · inner_sphDir⟧ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · eq_sphDir⟧. Put
$$L_{2j} = \theta + 2\pi j, \qquad L_{2j+1} = 2\pi(j+1) - \theta$$
⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · sphLen⟧, and let $\gamma_k$ be the great circle along $u$ with $L = L_k$ for even $k$, and along $-u$ with $L = L_k$ for odd $k$ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · sphGeod⟧. Then $\gamma_k(1) = q$ by periodicity of $\cos$ and $\sin$ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · sphGeod_one⟧, so $\gamma_k$ is a geodesic in $\Omega(S^n; p, q)$ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · sphGeod_mem_pathSpace⟧ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · isUnbrokenGeodesic_sphGeod⟧ with energy $L_k^2$ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · energy_sphGeod⟧. From $0 < \theta < \pi$,
$$k\pi < L_k < (k+1)\pi$$
⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · mul_pi_lt_sphLen⟧ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · sphLen_lt⟧, so the lengths are positive and strictly increasing ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · sphLen_pos⟧ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · sphLen_strictMono⟧. Milnor's description of $k$ as the number of passages of $\gamma_k$ through $p$ or $p'$ in its interior amounts to this inequality: $\gamma_k(t) = \pm p$ exactly when $L_k t \in \pi\mathbb Z$, that is at $t = j\pi/L_k$, $1 \le j \le k$, in $(0, 1)$.

**The $\gamma_k$ are all the geodesics from $p$ to $q$.** Every geodesic $\gamma \in \Omega(S^n; p, q)$ agrees on $[0, 1]$ with some $\gamma_k$ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · exists_eqOn_sphGeod⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* $\gamma$ is a great circle $\cos(Lt)\,p + \sin(Lt)\,w$ with $L \ge 0$. From $\gamma(1) = q$, pairing with $p$ gives $\cos L = \cos\theta$, and then $\sin L\cdot w = \sin\theta\cdot u$. The equality of cosines gives an integer $m$ with $L = \theta + 2\pi m$ or $L = 2\pi m - \theta$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/SpecialFunctions/Trigonometric/Complex · Real.cos_eq_cos_iff⟧. In the first case $\sin L = \sin\theta > 0$, so $w = u$; and $m \ge 0$, since $m \le -1$ would make $L < 0$. So $\gamma = \gamma_{2m}$. In the second case $\sin L = -\sin\theta$, so $w = -u$; and $m \ge 1$, since $L \ge 0$ and $\theta > 0$. So $\gamma = \gamma_{2(m-1)+1}$. This uses $q \ne \pm p$ twice: for $\sin\theta > 0$, and through $\gamma$ not being constant.

**The index of $\gamma_k$ is $k(n-1)$.** By the index theorem, the index of $E_{**}$ at $\gamma_k$ is $\sum_{0 < t < 1}$ of the multiplicity of $0$ and $t$ ⟦Chapter9/IndexTheorem · theorem_15_1⟧. By the conjugate points along a great circle ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · conjMultiplicity_sphGeod⟧, the summand is $n - 1$ when $L_k t \in \pi\mathbb N$ and $0$ otherwise. For $k\pi < L < (k+1)\pi$ such a sum is $k(n - 1)$: the support is $\{j\pi/L : 1 \le j \le k\}$, which has $k$ elements ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · finsum_mem_Ioo_ite_eq⟧. Hence the index is $k(n-1)$ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · formIndex_sphGeod⟧. Milnor uses the index theorem here without saying so.

**The index only depends on $[0, 1]$.** For any $N$, two geodesics of $\Omega$ that agree on $[0, 1]$ have the same index ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · formIndex_energyHessian_congr⟧: by the index theorem both indices count conjugate points, and the Jacobi fields along two curves agreeing on $[0, 1]$ are the same ⟦Chapter9/JacobiVariation · IsJacobiField.congr_curve⟧. So every geodesic from $p$ to $q$ agreeing with $\gamma_k$ on $[0, 1]$ has index $k(n-1)$ ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · formIndex_eq_of_eqOn_sphGeod⟧. This is needed only because paths are functions on $\mathbb R$.

**$p$ and $q$ are not conjugate along any geodesic** ⟦Chapter10/SphereGeodesics · notMem_conjLocus_sph⟧. Suppose $q$ were in the conjugate locus of $p$, along a geodesic $\gamma$. Then $\gamma = \gamma_k$ on $[0, 1]$ for some $k$, and conjugacy only depends on the curve on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter11/NonConjugate · IsConjugate.congr_curve_iff⟧. So $0$ and $1$ would be conjugate along $\gamma_k$, with positive multiplicity ⟦Chapter9/JacobiSpace · isConjugate_iff⟧. But at $t = 1$ the multiplicity is $0$ unless $L_k \in \pi\mathbb N$, and $k\pi < L_k < (k+1)\pi$ excludes that. Milnor rephrases non-conjugacy as $q \ne p, p'$; the direction proved here is the one that Theorem 17.3 needs.

### IX.8 The homology of an exhaustion, and of a slab

Milnor reads the homology of $\Omega S^n$ off the cells of Corollary 17.4 (p. 96). In the formalization the cell complexes have non-cellular attaching maps, so the homology is computed from the energy filtration, with two general tools.

*Homology* is singular homology with coefficients in a field $\Bbbk$ (§0.6, Part IV). For subsets $A \subseteq B$ of a space, $H_i(B, A; \Bbbk)$ is the relative homology of the pair ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · subsetHomology⟧, $H_i(X; \Bbbk)$ the homology of a space ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · spaceHomology⟧, and $b_i(X) = \dim_\Bbbk H_i(X; \Bbbk)$ its Betti number ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · bettiNumber⟧. A triple $A \subseteq B \subseteq D$ has the long exact sequence $\cdots \to H_i(B, A) \to H_i(D, A) \to H_i(D, B) \to H_{i-1}(B, A) \to \cdots$ ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · subsetTripleLongExactSeq⟧ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · LongExactSeq⟧. Two facts about such sequences are used: the map $H_i(B, A) \to H_i(D, A)$ is bijective when $H_i(D, B)$ and $H_{i+1}(D, B)$ vanish ⟦Chapter10/SublevelHomology · LongExactSeq.bijective_f_of_subsingleton⟧, and $H_i(D, A)$ vanishes when $H_i(B, A)$ and $H_i(D, B)$ do ⟦Chapter10/SublevelHomology · LongExactSeq.subsingleton_B⟧.

**Exactness passes to an exhausted complex.** Let $\varphi_k : Q_k \to Q$ be chain maps of complexes of modules, injective in every degree, such that every chain of $Q$ in degree $j$ comes from some $Q_k$. If every $Q_k$ is exact in degree $j$, so is $Q$ ⟦Chapter10/SublevelHomology · exactAt_of_injective_of_exists⟧. A cycle $x$ of $Q$ is $\varphi_k(y)$; $y$ is a cycle, because $\varphi_k$ commutes with the differential and is injective; $y$ bounds in $Q_k$, so $x$ bounds in $Q$.

**Homology commutes with exhaustions** (Hatcher, Proposition 3.33). Let $A_0 \subseteq A_1 \subseteq \cdots$ be subsets of $B$ such that every compact subset of $B$ lies in some $A_k$. If $H_j(A_k, A_{k_0}; \Bbbk) = 0$ for all $k \ge k_0$, then $H_j(B, A_{k_0}; \Bbbk) = 0$ ⟦Chapter10/SublevelHomology · subsingleton_subsetHomology_of_exhaustion⟧ ⟦Chapter10/SublevelHomology · subsingleton_subsetHomology_univ_of_exhaustion⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* The relative homology $H_j(B, A)$ vanishes iff the quotient complex of the singular chains of $B$ by the chains made of simplices in $A$ is exact in degree $j$ ⟦Chapter10/SublevelHomology · subsingleton_subsetHomology_iff_exactAt⟧ ⟦Homology/Excision · relChainsIsoQuot⟧. Apply the previous lemma to the maps $C(A_k)/C(A_{k_0}) \to C(B)/C(A_{k_0})$, $k \ge k_0$ ⟦Homology/Excision · excisionMap⟧. They are injective: a chain of $A_k$ whose image in $B$ is made of simplices in $A_{k_0}$ is itself made of such simplices ⟦Homology/Excision · push_mem_supported_iff⟧. Every chain of $B$ comes from some $A_k$: it is a finite combination of singular simplices, the union of their images is compact, so it lies in some $A_k$ by hypothesis, and then in $A_{\max(k, k_0)}$ ⟦Homology/Excision · range_push⟧. Each quotient for $A_k$ is exact in degree $j$ by hypothesis.

**From the steps to all pairs.** If $H_j(A_{k+1}, A_k; \Bbbk) = 0$ for all $k \ge k_0$, then $H_j(A_k, A_{k_0}; \Bbbk) = 0$ for all $k \ge k_0$ ⟦Chapter10/SublevelHomology · subsingleton_subsetHomology_of_steps⟧. This is an induction on $k$, starting from $H_j(A_{k_0}, A_{k_0}) = 0$ ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · subsingleton_subsetHomology_self⟧, along the exact sequences of the triples $A_{k_0} \subseteq A_k \subseteq A_{k+1}$.

**The homology of an exhausted space.** If every compact subset of $X$ lies in some $A_k$ and the groups $H_i$ and $H_{i+1}$ of the pairs $(A_k, A_{k_0})$, $k \ge k_0$, vanish, then the inclusion induces $H_i(A_{k_0}, \emptyset; \Bbbk) \cong H_i(X; \Bbbk)$ ⟦Chapter10/SublevelHomology · subsetHomologyEquivOfExhaustion⟧ ⟦Chapter10/SublevelHomology · spaceHomologyEquivOfExhaustion⟧ ⟦Chapter10/SublevelHomology · nonempty_linearEquiv_spaceHomology_of_exhaustion⟧ ⟦Chapter10/SublevelHomology · spaceHomologyEquivOfExhaustion'⟧. The proof is the exact sequence of the triple $\emptyset \subseteq A_{k_0} \subseteq X$, whose third terms $H_i(X, A_{k_0})$ and $H_{i+1}(X, A_{k_0})$ vanish by the previous results, with $H_i(X, \emptyset) \cong H_i(X)$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · subsetHomologyUnivEquiv⟧. The consequences for Betti numbers and finite dimension follow ⟦Chapter10/SublevelHomology · bettiNumber_eq_of_exhaustion⟧ ⟦Chapter10/SublevelHomology · finiteDimensional_spaceHomology_of_exhaustion⟧, as do the versions with hypotheses on the steps $(A_{k+1}, A_k)$ only ⟦Chapter10/SublevelHomology · spaceHomologyEquivOfSteps⟧ ⟦Chapter10/SublevelHomology · bettiNumber_eq_of_steps⟧. The vanishing is needed in degree $i + 1$ for injectivity and in degree $i$ for surjectivity.

**Sublevel sets exhaust the compact sets.** If $f : X \to \mathbb R$ is continuous and the levels $a_k$ are unbounded above, every compact $K \subseteq X$ lies in some $X^{a_k}$, since $f$ is bounded on $K$ ⟦Chapter10/SublevelHomology · exists_subset_sublevel_of_isCompact⟧. For the path space this is where the topology enters: with Milnor's metric $E$ is continuous, so compact subsets of $\Omega$ have bounded energy. For uniform convergence alone, in which $E$ is only lower semicontinuous, this fails: a uniformly convergent sequence of paths whose energies tend to $\infty$, together with its limit, is compact.

**One critical level inside a compact sublevel set** (Milnor p. 29, for a manifold that is not compact). Let $f = F|N$ as in §IX.3, $N^{a_0}$ compact, $S$ a finite set of non-degenerate critical points all at the level $c$, and $a' < c < a \le a_0$ with no other critical value in $[a', a]$. Then $H_i(N^a, N^{a'}; \Bbbk)$, read in any space $T$ homeomorphic to $N$, is finite-dimensional, of dimension the number of points of $S$ of index $i$ ⟦Chapter10/SublevelHomology · Homology.sublevel_step_critical_of_isCompact⟧. The proof is that of §IX.3, with homology in place of homotopy type: Remark 3.3 at the level $c$, Theorem 3.1 on the compact slabs $[a', c - \varepsilon]$ and $[c + \varepsilon, a]$, and the homology of finitely many disjoint cells attached to a closed set ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · finrank_subsetHomology_of_cells⟧ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyCells · cellAttachHomology⟧ (Part IV).

**The homology of a slab inside a compact sublevel set.** Let the critical points of $f$ in $N^{a_0}$ be non-degenerate, let $P$ be a finite set whose points of value $\le a$ are exactly the critical points of value $\le a$, let $a' \le a \le a_0$ be regular values, and suppose **all critical values in $(a', a)$ are equal**. Then $H_i(N^a, N^{a'}; \Bbbk)$ is finite-dimensional, of dimension the number of critical points of index $i$ in $f^{-1}(a', a)$ ⟦Chapter10/SublevelHomology · Homology.sublevel_step_of_isCompact⟧. If some critical value lies in $(a', a)$, all those critical points sit at one level $c$ and the previous result applies. If none does, Theorem 3.1 on the compact slab $[a', a]$ makes $N^{a'}$ a strong deformation retract of $N^a$, so the relative homology vanishes ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · subsingleton_subsetHomology_of_isSDR⟧, and so does the count.

### IX.9 The homology of the path space

**The homology of a slab of $\Omega$** (Milnor p. 29, in the path space). Let $a \le b < c$ be levels that are not energies of geodesics, every geodesic of energy $\le b$ of nullity $0$, and **all geodesics with energy in $(a, b)$ of the same energy**. Then $H_j(\Omega^b, \Omega^a; \Bbbk)$ is finite-dimensional, of dimension the number of break points $y \in U$ of geodesics with energy in $(a, b)$ and index $j$ ⟦Chapter10/PathHomology · BrokenSetup.finrank_subsetHomology_Omega⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Take $c_1$, $F$ and $S$ as in §IX.5, and $P$ the finite set of critical points of $F|S$ in the compact set $(F|S)^b$. The slab homology of the previous section, with $a_0 = b$ and $T = S$, gives $H_j((F|S)^b, (F|S)^a)$; its hypothesis that all critical values in $(a, b)$ are equal is the hypothesis on the energies. Then compare pairs. The homotopy equivalences $\alpha : \Omega^a \to (F|S)^a$ and $\beta : \Omega^b \to (F|S)^b$, built from the one formula of §IX.5, form a map of pairs, and a map of pairs whose two components are homotopy equivalences induces isomorphisms of relative homology, by the five lemma ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · isIso_relHomology_map_of_homotopyEquiv⟧. Finally the critical points of index $j$ are counted as break points, by Theorem 16.2.

This is where Milnor's requirement of one critical value per interval is genuinely used, in the weak form of at most one. With all the critical points of a slab on one level, their cells are attached simultaneously to the same sublevel set, and the homology of the slab is spanned by them, one generator per cell. With two critical levels in one slab, the boundary of a cell at the upper level could meet a cell at the lower level, and the homology of the slab would no longer be read off from the list of cells.

*One geodesic in each window.* Let $g_0, g_1, \dots$ be geodesics from $p$ to $q$ with strictly increasing energies $E_k = E(g_k)$. The *window levels* are $a_0 = -1$ and $a_{k+1} = (E_k + E_{k+1})/2$ ⟦Chapter10/PathHomology · windowLevels⟧. Then $E_k < a_{k+1} < E_{k+1}$ ⟦Chapter10/PathHomology · lt_windowLevels_succ⟧ ⟦Chapter10/PathHomology · windowLevels_succ_lt⟧, so $E_j$ lies in the window $(a_k, a_{k+1})$ iff $j = k$ ⟦Chapter10/PathHomology · mem_window_iff⟧; the levels are monotone ⟦Chapter10/PathHomology · windowLevels_monotone⟧ and tend to $\infty$ when the energies do ⟦Chapter10/PathHomology · tendsto_windowLevels⟧. The cells of such a space are recorded as no cell at stage $0$ and one cell of dimension $d_k$ at stage $k + 1$ ⟦Chapter10/PathHomology · oneCellEach⟧.

**One cell for each geodesic, one geodesic per window.** Let $N$ be closed in $V$, $p, q \in N$, every geodesic from $p$ to $q$ of nullity $0$, and suppose the geodesics from $p$ to $q$ are, up to their values off $[0, 1]$, a sequence $g_0, g_1, \dots$ with strictly increasing energies tending to $\infty$, every geodesic agreeing with $g_k$ on $[0, 1]$ having index $d_k$. Then $\Omega(N; p, q)$ has the homotopy type of the countable cell complex with one cell of dimension $d_k$ at stage $k + 1$ ⟦Chapter10/PathHomology · omega_cells_of_unique⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* No geodesic energy is a window level: a geodesic agrees with some $g_j$ on $[0, 1]$, so has energy $E_j$, which is $\ge 0 > a_0$ and differs from every $a_{k+1}$. Theorem 17.3 with the window levels gives lists $G_{k+1}$. Every entry of $G_{k+1}$ has energy in the $k$-th window, so it agrees with $g_k$ on $[0, 1]$. Some entry agrees with $g_k$, since $g_k$ has energy in the window. No two entries agree on $[0, 1]$, while all agree with $g_k$, so the list has exactly one entry ⟦Chapter10/PathHomology · List.eq_singleton_of_pairwise_not⟧, of index $d_k$ by §IX.5.

**The homology of each window.** Under the same hypotheses, except that the energies need not tend to $\infty$, $H_j(\Omega^{a_{k+1}}, \Omega^{a_k}; \Bbbk)$ is $\Bbbk$ for $j = d_k$ and $0$ otherwise ⟦Chapter10/PathHomology · finrank_window⟧. Take data of §16 with $c = \max(a_{k+1}, 0) + 1$ and apply the slab homology: all geodesics in the window agree with $g_k$ on $[0, 1]$, so they have one energy. The break points in the window with index $j$ are those of $g_k$ if $j = d_k$, and there are none otherwise, because a broken geodesic is determined by its values on $[0, 1]$.

**Lacunary indices** (Milnor p. 96, for $\Omega S^n$, $n \ge 3$). If moreover the energies tend to $\infty$ and $d_k = kr$ with $r \ge 2$, then for every field $\Bbbk$ and every $i$, $b_i(\Omega(N; p, q)) = 1$ if $r$ divides $i$, and $0$ otherwise ⟦Chapter10/PathHomology · bettiNumber_omega_of_unique⟧.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. Let $X_k = \Omega^{a_k}$ for the window levels. Then $X_0 = \emptyset$, as $a_0 = -1 < 0 \le E$, and every compact subset of $\Omega$ lies in some $X_k$, because $E$ is continuous and the levels tend to $\infty$.
2. Fix $i$ and put $K = i + 2$. For $k \ge K$ the steps $(X_{k+1}, X_k)$ have homology only in degree $kr \ge Kr \ge 2i + 4 > i + 1$, so they vanish in degrees $i$ and $i + 1$. Passing to the union, $b_i(\Omega) = \dim H_i(X_K, \emptyset) = \dim H_i(X_K, X_0)$ ⟦Chapter10/SublevelHomology · bettiNumber_eq_of_steps⟧ ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · finrank_subsetHomology_congr⟧. This uses step 1 only through compactness: no cell structure on $\Omega$ is needed.
3. The Morse numbers of the finite filtration $X_0 \subseteq \dots \subseteq X_K$ are $C_j = \sum_{k < K} \dim H_j(X_{k+1}, X_k)$ ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · singularMorseNumber⟧. By the window homology, $C_j = 1$ if $r \mid j$ and $j/r < K$, and $C_j = 0$ otherwise.
4. The strong Morse inequalities for singular homology hold for the filtration ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · singular_morse_inequality⟧ (Part IV). No two consecutive $C_j$ are nonzero: $r \mid j$ and $r \mid j + 1$ would give $r \mid 1$. So the lacunary principle (the remark on $\mathbb CP^n$, p. 31) gives $\dim H_j(X_K, X_0) = C_j$ for every $j$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · eq_of_altSum_le_of_lacunary⟧.
5. For $j = i$: if $r \mid i$ then $i/r \le i < K$, so $C_i = 1$; otherwise $C_i = 0$.

Milnor computes integral homology from the cellular chain complex, whose differentials vanish because no two cells have consecutive dimensions. Here the coefficients form a field, and the same gap is used through the lacunary principle on finite stages. The integral statement is not formalized.

### IX.10 Corollary 17.4 and the homology of $\Omega(S^n; p, q)$

Let $p, q \in S^n$ with $q \ne \pm p$. The energies $E(\gamma_k) = L_k^2$ increase strictly, as the $L_k$ are positive and increase ⟦Chapter10/SpherePaths · strictMono_energy_sphGeod⟧, and tend to $\infty$, as $L_k > k\pi$ ⟦Chapter10/SpherePaths · tendsto_energy_sphGeod⟧.

**Corollary 17.4** (Milnor p. 96), for the path space. For $n \ge 2$ and $p, q \in S^n$ with $q \ne \pm p$, $\Omega(S^n; p, q)$, with Milnor's metric, has the homotopy type of the countable cell complex with one cell in each of the dimensions $0, n-1, 2(n-1), \dots$, attached in this order ⟦Chapter10/SpherePaths · corollary_17_4⟧.

*The proof as formalized* applies the theorem on one geodesic per window (§IX.9) with $N = S^n$, which is closed in $\mathbb R^{n+1}$ as it is compact ⟦Milnor/Chapter6/EulerSphere · isCompact_sph⟧. Every geodesic from $p$ to $q$ has nullity $0$, because $q$ is not in the conjugate locus of $p$ (§IX.7). The sequence is $g_k = \gamma_k$: these are all the geodesics, with strictly increasing energies tending to $\infty$, and every geodesic agreeing with $\gamma_k$ on $[0, 1]$ has index $d_k = k(n-1)$.

**The homology of $\Omega(S^n; p, q)$** (Milnor p. 96). For $n \ge 3$, $q \ne \pm p$ and every field $\Bbbk$, $\dim_\Bbbk H_i(\Omega(S^n; p, q); \Bbbk)$ is $1$ if $n - 1$ divides $i$, and $0$ otherwise ⟦Chapter10/SpherePaths · bettiNumber_sphere_paths⟧. This is the lacunary case of §IX.9 with $r = n - 1 \ge 2$.

*What Milnor leaves implicit.* Milnor states 17.4 for the loop space $\Omega(S^n)$, but his proof applies 17.3, which needs non-conjugate end points, while $p$ is conjugate to itself along every great circle. Passing to loops needs the independence of $\Omega^*(M; p, q)$ from $q$ up to homotopy (Lemma 17.D of the exposition) together with Theorem 17.1; that lemma is not formalized, so the formal statements are for $q \ne \pm p$, which is what the argument gives. The case $n = 1$, where every cell is a point, is not stated. For $n = 2$ there is a cell in every dimension and the lacunary principle does not apply, which is why the homology needs $n \ge 3$, Milnor's $n > 2$.

### IX.11 Completeness cannot be dropped: $\mathbb R^{n+1} \setminus \{0\}$

Corollary 17.5 (p. 96) asserts that two non-conjugate points of a manifold with the homotopy type of $S^n$, $n > 2$, are joined by infinitely many geodesics. Its proof rests on Theorem 17.3, which assumes completeness; the statement does not. The formalization proves that the hypothesis cannot be dropped, with $N = \mathbb R^{n+1} \setminus \{0\}$ and the flat metric.

*Open sets are submanifolds of codimension $0$.* For a zero-dimensional space $W$, the map $x \mapsto (x, 0)$ is a global diffeomorphism $V \to V \times W$ ⟦Chapter10/Incomplete · zeroGraph⟧. Restricted to an open set $O$ it is a slice chart for $O$ whose slice condition holds automatically, as its whole domain lies in $O$ ⟦Chapter10/Incomplete · openSliceChart⟧. So $O$ is a slice submanifold of $V$ modelled on $V$ ⟦Chapter10/Incomplete · isSliceSubmanifold_of_isOpen⟧, and every tangent space of $O$ is all of $V$, computed in that chart ⟦Chapter10/Incomplete · tangentSubspace_of_isOpen⟧.

*Two facts of calculus.* A function with vanishing second derivative on an open interval is affine there ⟦Chapter10/Incomplete · eq_add_smul_of_hasDerivAt⟧, and a function continuous on $[0, 1]$ with vanishing second derivative on $(0, 1)$ is $f(t) = f(0) + t(f(1) - f(0))$ on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter10/Incomplete · eqOn_affine_of_hasDerivAt⟧.

*Codimension $0$ means flat.* Suppose every tangent space of $N$ is $V$. Then:

- the tangent projection is the identity ⟦Chapter10/Incomplete · tanProj_eq_id_of_top⟧ ⟦Chapter10/Incomplete · prj_eq_id_of_top⟧, so the second fundamental form $\mathrm{II} = dP$ vanishes ⟦Chapter10/Incomplete · secondFundForm_eq_zero_of_top⟧, and the curvature $R(a, b)c = \mathrm{II}(b, \mathrm{II}(a, c)) - \mathrm{II}(a, \mathrm{II}(b, c))$ vanishes ⟦Chapter6/Submanifold · curvOp_apply⟧ ⟦Chapter10/Incomplete · curvR_eq_zero_of_top⟧;
- a geodesic has acceleration normal to $V$, hence zero ⟦Chapter10/Incomplete · IsGeodesicOn.deriv_deriv_eq_zero_of_top⟧, so it is a straight line ⟦Chapter10/Incomplete · IsGeodesicOn.eq_add_smul_of_top⟧, and a geodesic from $p$ to $q$ is the segment $t \mapsto p + t(q - p)$ on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter10/Incomplete · IsUnbrokenGeodesic.eqOn_segment_of_top⟧;

For every $n \ge 1$ this also contradicts the conclusion of Theorem 17.3 without completeness, which would make $\Omega(N; p, q)$ a single $0$-cell up to homotopy, whereas it has the homotopy type of the loop space of $S^n$, which is not contractible. That comparison is not formalized; what is proved is that $N$ satisfies every hypothesis of the formal Theorem 17.3 except closedness in the ambient space, which is where completeness enters it.

### IX.12 Milnor's proof of Theorem 17.1, and the step that does not hold as we read it

**Theorem 17.1** (p. 93). The inclusion $i : \Omega \to \Omega^*$ is a homotopy equivalence.

*Milnor's construction* (pp. 93–94), paraphrased. Choose a continuous $f : M \to [0, \infty)$ whose sublevel sets $f^{-1}[0, r]$ are compact. For each $r$ let $\varepsilon_1(r) \le \infty$ be the largest number such that any two points of $f^{-1}[0, r]$ at distance less than $\varepsilon_1(r)$ are joined by a unique minimal geodesic, depending differentiably on its end points; Corollary 10.8 makes $\varepsilon_1$ positive. Choose a continuous $\varepsilon_2$ with $0 < \varepsilon_2 < \varepsilon_1$. For $\omega \in \Omega^*$ put $\varepsilon(\omega) = \varepsilon_2(\max_t f(\omega(t)))$ and
$$F(\omega, \alpha) = (\alpha - 1)\,\varepsilon(\omega) + \max_{|t - t'| \le \alpha} \rho(\omega(t), \omega(t')).$$
$F(\omega, \cdot)$ is strictly increasing, negative at $\alpha = 0$ and non-negative at $\alpha = 1$; its zero is called $2A(\omega)$, and $A$ depends continuously on $\omega$. At the zero, two points of $\omega$ with parameters at most $2A$ apart are at distance at most $(1 - 2A)\varepsilon(\omega) < \varepsilon_1$, so they are joined by a unique minimal geodesic. Let $k$ be the largest integer $\le 1/A(\omega)$. The path $h(\omega)$ agrees with $\omega$ at $t = 0, A, 2A, \dots, kA$ and at $t = 1$, and is a minimal geodesic on each of the intervals between. Milnor then writes "Clearly h is continuous" (p. 94), and says that arguments as in §16 give homotopies $h \circ i \simeq \mathrm{id}$ and $i \circ h \simeq \mathrm{id}$.

*What fails.* As we read it, the claim does not hold for the metric $d$ of $\Omega$. The number of intervals of $h(\omega)$ changes when $1/A(\omega)$ passes through an integer. Suppose $1/A(\omega_0) = k$ exactly. Then $kA(\omega_0) = 1$, the last interval $[kA, 1]$ is empty, and $h(\omega_0)$ has $k$ segments. For $\omega$ near $\omega_0$ with $A(\omega)$ slightly smaller, still $k(\omega) = k$, but now $kA(\omega) = 1 - \delta$ with a small $\delta > 0$. So $h(\omega)$ has one more segment, from $\omega(1 - \delta)$ to $\omega(1) = q$ in time $\delta$. Its length $\ell = \rho(\omega(1 - \delta), q)$ is small, but its energy is $\ell^2/\delta$. For a piecewise smooth $\omega$ this is harmless: by Cauchy–Schwarz $\ell^2/\delta$ is at most the energy of $\omega$ on the last $\delta$ of time, and along a sequence converging in $\Omega$ that energy is uniformly small (Lemma 16.B of the exposition, Part VIII). But $h$ must be defined and continuous on all of $\Omega^*$, and for a path that is merely continuous $\ell$ can be as large as $\delta^{1/3}$, so that $\ell^2/\delta \approx \delta^{-1/3} \to \infty$. Since the energy is continuous on $(\Omega, d)$, $h(\omega)$ then cannot converge to $h(\omega_0)$ in $\Omega$.

The formalization turns this into a theorem, for Milnor's own construction with $M = \mathbb R$. Everything below is in `Chapter10/MilnorH`, most of it in the namespace `MilnorH`.

**Milnor's choices for $M = \mathbb R$.** Take $p = q = 0$. Then $\Omega^*$ is the space of continuous $\omega : [0, 1] \to \mathbb R$ with $\omega(0) = \omega(1) = 0$, with the uniform metric, and $\Omega$ the space of piecewise smooth such paths, with Milnor's metric (§IX.1, with $N = \mathbb R$).

- *The distance* is $\rho(x, y) = |x - y|$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · riemDist_real⟧. The segment $t \mapsto x + t(y - x)$ has length $|x - y|$, which bounds $\rho$ from above ⟦Chapter7/Distance · riemDist_le_pathLength⟧, and $\rho(x, y) \ge |x - y|$ always (§IX.1).
- *The exhaustion* is $f(x) = |x|$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · exhaust⟧, with compact sublevel sets $[-r, r]$.
- *$\varepsilon_2 \equiv 1$* ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · epsTwo⟧. Any two points of $\mathbb R$ are joined by a unique minimal geodesic, the segment, which depends affinely on its end points; so $\varepsilon_1 \equiv \infty$, and the constant $1$ is an admissible continuous $\varepsilon_2$. This admissibility is elementary and is recorded in the file's documentation, not as a separate theorem; the formal definitions simply make these choices. Then $\varepsilon(\omega) = \varepsilon_2(\sup_t |\omega(t)|) = 1$ for every $\omega$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · eps⟧ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · eps_eq⟧.

**The modulus.** Milnor's maximum is the modulus of continuity
$$m_\omega(\alpha) = \sup\{|\omega(t) - \omega(t')| : t, t' \in [0, 1],\ |t - t'| \le \alpha\}$$
⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · modulus⟧. The set is bounded by $2\max|\omega|$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · bddAbove_modulus⟧, so every term is at most the supremum ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · le_modulus⟧, and a common bound for the terms bounds it ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · modulus_le⟧. For $0 \le \alpha \le \beta$: $m_\omega(\alpha) \ge 0$, $m_\omega$ is non-decreasing, and $m_\omega(0) = 0$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · modulus_nonneg⟧ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · modulus_mono⟧ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · modulus_zero⟧. It is subadditive, $m_\omega(\alpha + \beta) \le m_\omega(\alpha) + m_\omega(\beta)$: a step $|t - t'| \le \alpha + \beta$ longer than $\alpha$ is split at the point at distance $\alpha$ from $t$ towards $t'$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · modulus_add_le⟧. Hence $|m_\omega(\alpha) - m_\omega(\beta)| \le m_\omega(|\alpha - \beta|)$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · abs_modulus_sub_le⟧. By uniform continuity of $\omega$ on the compact $[0, 1]$, $m_\omega(\delta) \to 0$ as $\delta \to 0$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · exists_modulus_le⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/UniformSpace/HeineCantor · CompactSpace.uniformContinuous_of_continuous⟧. So $m_\omega$ is continuous on $[0, \infty)$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · continuousOn_modulus⟧.

**Milnor's $F$, $A$ and $k$.** With these choices
$$F(\omega, \alpha) = (\alpha - 1)\,\varepsilon(\omega) + m_\omega(\alpha) = \alpha - 1 + m_\omega(\alpha)$$
⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · milnorF⟧, which is strictly increasing in $\alpha \ge 0$, since $m_\omega$ is non-decreasing ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · milnorF_lt⟧. The formal definition of $2A(\omega)$ makes sense for every $\omega$:
$$2A(\omega) = \inf\{\alpha \in [0, 1] : F(\omega, \alpha) \ge 0\}$$
⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · twoA⟧. Milnor's claims about it are then proved, so this is exactly his $2A$.

- $1$ belongs to the set, because $F(\omega, 1) = m_\omega(1) \ge 0$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · one_mem_twoA_set⟧.
- A zero $\alpha_0 \in [0, 1]$ of $F(\omega, \cdot)$ equals $2A(\omega)$: it lies in the set, and a smaller element $\alpha$ would give $0 \le F(\omega, \alpha) < F(\omega, \alpha_0) = 0$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · twoA_eq⟧.
- $2A(\omega) \in (0, 1]$ and $F(\omega, 2A(\omega)) = 0$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · twoA_spec⟧. $F(\omega, \cdot)$ is continuous on $[0, 1]$ with $F(\omega, 0) = -1 < 0 \le F(\omega, 1)$, so the intermediate value theorem gives a zero $\alpha_0$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Order/IntermediateValue · intermediate_value_Icc⟧. By the previous point $\alpha_0 = 2A(\omega)$, and $\alpha_0 \ne 0$ because $F(\omega, 0) = -1$.

Then $A(\omega) = 2A(\omega)/2$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · milnorA⟧ is positive ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · milnorA_pos⟧ and at most $\tfrac12$, since $2A(\omega) \le 1$. (Milnor writes $A : \Omega^* \to (0, 1]$; the values lie in $(0, \tfrac12]$.) Milnor's $k$ is $\lfloor 1/A(\omega) \rfloor$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · milnorK⟧, and $kA \le 1$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · milnorK_mul_le⟧. The continuity of $A$ in $\omega$, which Milnor also claims, is true (Lemma 17.A of the exposition) but is not formalized; it is not involved in the failure of continuity.

**Milnor's $h$.** For a function $x$, a number $A > 0$ and an integer $k$ with $kA \le 1$, let $\mathcal L_{A,k}(x)$ be the path through the points $(jA, x(jA))$, $0 \le j \le k$, and $(1, x(1))$, affine in between ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · plin⟧. On $[jA, (j+1)A]$, $j < k$, it is the segment ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · seg⟧ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · plin_eq_seg⟧, and on $[kA, 1]$ the last segment ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · lastSeg⟧ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · plin_eq_last⟧, which is a single point when $kA = 1$. It is smooth on every interval free of the break points $0, A, \dots, kA$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · breaks⟧ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · plin_piece⟧, hence piecewise smooth ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · plin_isPiecewiseSmooth⟧. It starts at $x(0)$, ends at $x(1)$, and passes through the nodes ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · plin_zero⟧ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · plin_one⟧ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · plin_node⟧. Its acceleration vanishes on every open interval between nodes ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · deriv_deriv_plin_seg⟧ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · deriv_deriv_plin_last⟧. Its energy is
$$E(\mathcal L_{A,k}(x)) = \sum_{j < k} \frac{(x((j+1)A) - x(jA))^2}{A} + \frac{(x(1) - x(kA))^2}{1 - kA}$$
⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · integral_seg⟧ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · integral_last⟧ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · energy_plin⟧. When $kA = 1$ the last term is $0$: the integral over a degenerate interval vanishes, and in the formal arithmetic the quotient $0/0$ is $0$.

Milnor's $h(\omega)$ is $\mathcal L_{A(\omega),k(\omega)}(\omega)$, with $\omega$ extended by constants off $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · hPath⟧. It satisfies Milnor's two conditions: it agrees with $\omega$ at $t = jA$, $j \le k$, and at $t = 1$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · hPath_node⟧ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · hPath_one⟧, and on each interval between it has zero acceleration, so it is a geodesic of $\mathbb R$ there ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · deriv_deriv_hPath⟧; the minimal geodesics of $\mathbb R$ are exactly these segments. It is a path of $\Omega$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · hPath_mem⟧, which defines Milnor's map $h : \Omega^* \to \Omega$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · hMap⟧.

**The family $\omega_\lambda$.** Let
$$g(t) = (1 - t)^{1/3} - (1 - t), \qquad \omega_\lambda = \lambda g$$
⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · g⟧ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · omegaFam⟧ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · omegaFam_apply⟧. Then $g(0) = g(1) = 0$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · g_zero⟧ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · g_one⟧, so $\omega_\lambda \in \Omega^*(\mathbb R; 0, 0)$, and $g(1 - s) = s^{1/3} - s$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · g_one_sub⟧. Near $t = 1$ the path is only Hölder continuous of exponent $\tfrac13$; that is the whole point. With $u = (1 - t)^{1/3} \in [0, 1]$ one has $g = u - u^3$, so $0 \le g$ on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · g_nonneg⟧, and
$$c_0 - g = \Big(u - \frac{\sqrt3}{3}\Big)^2\Big(u + \frac{2\sqrt3}{3}\Big) \ge 0, \qquad c_0 = \frac{2\sqrt3}{9}$$
⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · c₀⟧ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · g_le⟧. The maximum $c_0$ is attained at $1 - s_0$, $s_0 = \sqrt3/9 = (\sqrt3/3)^3$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · s₀⟧ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · g_one_sub_s₀⟧, and $s_0 < \tfrac12$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · s₀_lt_half⟧. The critical parameter is
$$\lambda_* = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \qquad \lambda_* c_0 = \frac13, \qquad \lambda_*^2 = \frac34$$
⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · lamStar⟧ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · lamStar_mul_c₀⟧ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · lamStar_sq⟧.

**Uniform convergence.** $d^*(\omega_\lambda, \omega_\mu) \le c_0|\lambda - \mu|$, because $|\lambda g - \mu g| = |\lambda - \mu|\,g \le c_0|\lambda - \mu|$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · dist_omegaFam_le⟧. So $\lambda \mapsto \omega_\lambda$ is Lipschitz into $\Omega^*$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · continuous_omegaFam⟧, and $\omega_\lambda \to \omega_{\lambda_*}$ uniformly as $\lambda \to \lambda_*$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · tendsto_omegaFam⟧.

**$A$ and $k$ along the family.** For $\lambda \ge 0$ and $\alpha \ge s_0$, $m_{\omega_\lambda}(\alpha) = \lambda c_0$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · modulus_omegaFam⟧. It is at most $\lambda c_0$ because all values of $\omega_\lambda$ lie in $[0, \lambda c_0]$, and at least $\lambda c_0$ because of the pair $(1 - s_0, 1)$, whose parameters are $s_0 \le \alpha$ apart and whose values are $\lambda c_0$ and $0$. Hence, as long as $\lambda c_0 \le \tfrac12$, the number $\alpha = 1 - \lambda c_0 \ge \tfrac12 > s_0$ is a zero of $F$: $F(\omega_\lambda, 1 - \lambda c_0) = -\lambda c_0 + \lambda c_0 = 0$. So
$$2A(\omega_\lambda) = 1 - \lambda c_0, \qquad A(\omega_\lambda) = \frac{1 - \lambda c_0}{2}$$
⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · twoA_omegaFam⟧ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · milnorA_omegaFam⟧, a continuous function of $\lambda$. At $\lambda_*$: $A = \tfrac13$, $1/A = 3$, $k = 3$, and $kA = 1$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · milnorA_lamStar⟧ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · one_div_milnorA_lamStar⟧ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · milnorK_lamStar⟧. This is the critical configuration: $1/A$ is an integer and the last interval is empty. For $\lambda_* \le \lambda \le \tfrac54\lambda_*$ one has $\tfrac13 \le \lambda c_0 \le \tfrac5{12}$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · lam_mul_c₀_mem⟧, so $1/A = 2/(1 - \lambda c_0) \in [3, \tfrac{24}{7}]$ and $k = 3$ throughout ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · milnorK_omegaFam⟧.

**The energy at $\lambda_*$.** $h(\omega_{\lambda_*})$ has nodes $0, \tfrac13, \tfrac23, 1$ and no last segment, so by the energy formula
$$E(h(\omega_{\lambda_*})) = 3\lambda_*^2\Big(g(\tfrac13)^2 + \big(g(\tfrac23) - g(\tfrac13)\big)^2 + g(\tfrac23)^2\Big)$$
⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · energy_hPath_lamStar⟧, which is at most $1$, because the values $\lambda_* g$ lie in $[0, \tfrac13]$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · energy_hPath_lamStar_le⟧. Numerically it is about $0.44$.

**The energy blow-up.** Let $\delta_\lambda = (3\lambda c_0 - 1)/2 = 1 - 3A(\omega_\lambda)$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · delta⟧, the length of the new last interval. It is positive for $\lambda > \lambda_*$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · delta_pos⟧ and at most $\tfrac18$ for $\lambda \le \tfrac54\lambda_*$.

**Lemma.** For $\lambda_* < \lambda \le \tfrac54\lambda_*$ and $\delta = \delta_\lambda$,
$$E(h(\omega_\lambda)) \ \ge\ \frac{\lambda^2(\delta^{1/3} - \delta)^2}{\delta} \ \ge\ \frac{27}{64}\,\delta^{-1/3}$$
⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · le_energy_hPath⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Here $k = 3$, $A = (1 - \lambda c_0)/2$ and $kA = 1 - \delta < 1$, so $h(\omega_\lambda)$ has a fourth segment, from $(1 - \delta, \omega_\lambda(1 - \delta)) = (1 - \delta, \lambda(\delta^{1/3} - \delta))$ to $(1, 0)$. In the energy formula every term is non-negative, so the energy is at least the last term, $\lambda^2(\delta^{1/3} - \delta)^2/\delta$. Put $v = \delta^{1/3}$, so $v^3 = \delta$ and $0 < v \le \tfrac12$ (as $\delta \le \tfrac18$). Then
$$\frac{\lambda^2(v - v^3)^2}{v^3} = \frac{\lambda^2(1 - v^2)^2}{v} \ \ge\ \frac{\tfrac34 \cdot \tfrac9{16}}{v} = \frac{27}{64}\,\delta^{-1/3},$$
using $\lambda^2 \ge \lambda_*^2 = \tfrac34$ and $1 - v^2 \ge \tfrac34$.

As $\lambda \downarrow \lambda_*$, $\delta_\lambda^{1/3} \downarrow 0$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · tendsto_delta_cbrt⟧, so
$$E(h(\omega_\lambda)) \to \infty \qquad (\lambda \downarrow \lambda_*)$$
⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · tendsto_energy_hPath⟧. (Numerically, $E(h(\omega_\lambda))$ is already about $9.7$ when $\lambda/\lambda_* - 1 = 10^{-3}$.)

**$\sqrt E$ is $1$-Lipschitz for $d$.** This is Lemma 16.A of the exposition, specialized to the paths of $\mathbb R$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · MilnorH.lipschitzWith_sqrt_energy⟧ ⟦Chapter10/PathMetric · lipschitzWith_sqrt_energy⟧. It follows that
$$d\big(h(\omega_\lambda), h(\omega_{\lambda_*})\big) \ \ge\ \sqrt{E(h(\omega_\lambda))} - \sqrt{E(h(\omega_{\lambda_*}))} \ \to\ \infty \qquad (\lambda \downarrow \lambda_*)$$
⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · tendsto_edist_hMap⟧.

**Theorem (Milnor's $h$ is not continuous).** For $M = \mathbb R$, $p = q = 0$, $f = |\cdot|$ and $\varepsilon_2 \equiv 1$, Milnor's map $h : \Omega^* \to \Omega$ is not continuous at $\omega_{\lambda_*}$ ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · milnor_h_not_continuous⟧; in particular it is not continuous ⟦Chapter10/MilnorH · milnor_h_not_continuous'⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* If $h$ were continuous at $\omega_{\lambda_*}$, then, since $\omega_\lambda \to \omega_{\lambda_*}$, $h(\omega_\lambda) \to h(\omega_{\lambda_*})$ in $\Omega$ as $\lambda \downarrow \lambda_*$. The energy is continuous on $\Omega$, so $E(h(\omega_\lambda))$ would converge to the finite number $E(h(\omega_{\lambda_*}))$. But it tends to $\infty$.

*What exactly fails, and what does not.*

- **What fails is the continuity of $h$ for $d$**, and only that. $A$ is continuous, as Milnor says; along the family it is $(1 - \lambda c_0)/2$, and $k = 3$ for all $\lambda_* \le \lambda \le \tfrac54\lambda_*$. What moves is the last break point $kA = 3A$, which leaves $1$, and the segment born there is short in time and in space, so $h$ is continuous for the uniform metric $d^*$ (an observation of the exposition, not formalized). The failure is in the $L^2$ term of $d$: the new segment has speed $\ell/\delta$ on an interval of length $\delta$, so it contributes $\ell^2/\delta$ to the energy, and this is unbounded.
- **It is not an artefact of the choices.** The claim is made for every $M$, every $f$ and every admissible $\varepsilon_2$, so one admissible instance is a counterexample to it. Other constant choices $\varepsilon_2 \equiv c$ give nothing new: $F_c(c\omega, \alpha) = c\,F_1(\omega, \alpha)$, so $A_c(c\omega) = A_1(\omega)$ and $h_c(c\omega) = c\,h_1(\omega)$, and the family $c\,\omega_\lambda$ fails in the same way (a remark of the file's documentation, not formalized).
- **The rest of the proof falls with $h$.** The homotopies $h \circ i \simeq \mathrm{id}$ and $i \circ h \simeq \mathrm{id}$ by the arguments of §16 are homotopies through maps into $\Omega$, so they need $h$ continuous for $d$. A weaker statement, that $i$ is a weak homotopy equivalence, would be enough for Theorem 19.2 (the exposition derives it at once from the sets $Y_k$ below, since every compact subset of $\Omega^*$ lies in one of them; this is not formalized), but Theorem 19.6 and the remark after Corollary 18.2 use the full theorem.
- **The theorem is true.** It is proved below by a different route, whose key decision is to keep the subdivision fixed: on each of the open sets $Y_k$ the break points are the dyadic points $j/2^k$, so no segment is ever born, and the sets are glued with the Appendix.

The finding was confirmed by an independent review against the scan of the book, by numerical checks, and by this formal proof (Findings, §§16–19).

### IX.13 Convergence in Milnor's metric controls the velocities

The modified proof deforms paths by maps given pointwise by smooth formulas: a point $\gamma(s)$ is moved along a minimal geodesic that depends smoothly on $\gamma(s)$ and on a point of a broken geodesic. Continuity of such a map for $d$ needs more than $d$ visibly provides. $d$ compares the speeds $\|\gamma'\|$, but the velocity of $s \mapsto \Phi(\gamma(s), \dots)$ is $D\Phi(\gamma'(s), \dots)$, which involves the velocity vector itself. The lemmas of this section supply the missing control. Milnor's continuity claim passes over them, and they are needed even for maps that are continuous.

**Integration by parts on a smooth piece.** Let $K$ be smooth on $[s, t] \subseteq [0, 1]$ and $\beta \in \Omega$. Then
$$\int_s^t \langle \beta', K\rangle = \langle \beta(t), K(t)\rangle - \langle \beta(s), K(s)\rangle - \int_s^t \langle \beta, K'\rangle$$
⟦Chapter10/FullPathSpace · PathSp.integral_inner_deriv_eq⟧. The function $\langle \beta, K\rangle$ is continuous on $[s, t]$ and differentiable off the finite set of break points of $\beta$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathSpace · PathSp.hasDerivAt_of_notMem⟧, with derivative $\langle \beta', K\rangle + \langle \beta, K'\rangle$. Both terms are integrable, the first because $\beta'$ is continuous on the pieces of $\beta$ ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · intervalIntegrable_of_piecewise⟧. The fundamental theorem of calculus off a countable set applies ⟦Mathlib/MeasureTheory/Integral/DivergenceTheorem · integral_eq_of_hasDerivAt_off_countable_of_le⟧.

**Uniform convergence.** If $\gamma_j \to \gamma$ in $\Omega$, then $\gamma_j \to \gamma$ uniformly on $[0, 1]$ in the norm of $V$, since $\|\cdot\| \le \rho$ and the first term of $d$ is the supremum of $\rho$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathSpace · PathSp.eventually_norm_sub_le_of_tendsto⟧.

**Weak convergence of the velocities.** If $\gamma_j \to \gamma$ in $\Omega$, then $\int_0^1 \langle \gamma_j', \gamma'\rangle \to \int_0^1 \|\gamma'\|^2$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathSpace · PathSp.tendsto_integral_inner_deriv⟧. The proof is an induction over the pieces of $\gamma$ ⟦Chapter8/Piecewise · piecewise_induction⟧: adjacent intervals add, so it suffices to treat one smooth piece $[s, t]$, on which $\gamma'$ agrees with a function $K$ smooth on $[s, t]$ ⟦Chapter8/Energy · IsPiecewiseSmooth.derivWithin_piece⟧. Integration by parts rewrites $\int_s^t \langle \gamma_j', K\rangle$ and $\int_s^t \|\gamma'\|^2 = \int_s^t \langle \gamma', K\rangle$. The boundary terms converge because $\gamma_j \to \gamma$ pointwise, and the integral terms differ by at most $(t - s)\,\sup\|K'\|\,\sup_{[s,t]}\|\gamma_j - \gamma\|$, which tends to $0$ by uniform convergence.

**$L^2$ convergence of the velocities.** If $\gamma_j \to \gamma$ in $\Omega$, then $\int_0^1 \|\gamma_j' - \gamma'\|^2 \to 0$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathSpace · PathSp.tendsto_integral_norm_deriv_sub_sq⟧. Expand
$$\int_0^1 \|\gamma_j' - \gamma'\|^2 = E(\gamma_j) - 2\int_0^1 \langle \gamma_j', \gamma'\rangle + E(\gamma) \ \to\ E(\gamma) - 2E(\gamma) + E(\gamma) = 0,$$
using the continuity of $E$ for $d$ (§IX.1) and the weak convergence.

**A criterion for convergence in $\Omega$.** Conversely, if $\alpha_j(s) \to \alpha(s)$ for every $s \in [0, 1]$ and $\int_0^1 \|\alpha_j' - \alpha'\|^2 \to 0$, then $\alpha_j \to \alpha$ in $\Omega$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathSpace · PathSp.tendsto_of_tendsto_apply_of_integral⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Once $\int_0^1 \|\alpha_j' - \alpha'\|^2 \le 1$, the inequality $\|\alpha_j'\|^2 \le 2\|\alpha'\|^2 + 2\|\alpha_j' - \alpha'\|^2$ gives $E(\alpha_j) \le C = 2E(\alpha) + 2$. Paths of energy at most $C$ are uniformly Hölder of exponent $\tfrac12$ for $\rho$, by Cauchy–Schwarz, so pointwise convergence at the points of a finite net of $[0, 1]$ makes the supremum term of $d$ small ⟦Chapter10/RetractionConv · PathSp.eventually_supDist_le_of_tendsto_apply⟧ (Part VIII). The second term is at most $\big(\int_0^1 \|\alpha_j' - \alpha'\|^2\big)^{1/2}$, because $|\|\alpha_j'\| - \|\alpha'\|| \le \|\alpha_j' - \alpha'\|$.

So convergence in $\Omega$ is pointwise convergence together with $L^2$ convergence of the velocities. The last lemma says that smooth formulas preserve the second.

**Smooth maps preserve $L^2$ convergence of velocities.** Let $\Phi$ be of class $C^1$ on an open set $U$ containing the curve $F_0([a, b])$. Let $F_i \to F_0$ uniformly on $[a, b]$, all curves differentiable off finite sets, with velocities $F_i'$ bounded in $L^2$, $\int_a^b \|F_i'\|^2 \le C$, and converging to $F_0'$ in $L^2$. Then $\int_a^b \|(\Phi \circ F_i)' - (\Phi \circ F_0)'\|^2 \to 0$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · tendsto_integral_deriv_comp_sub_sq⟧.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. The image $F_0([a, b])$ is compact, so a closed $\delta$-thickening $K'$ of it lies in $U$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/MetricSpace/Thickening · IsCompact.exists_cthickening_subset_open⟧. On the compact $K'$, $D\Phi$ is bounded by some $M$ and uniformly continuous.
2. Given $\varepsilon$, choose $\eta$ with $2\eta^2(|C| + 1) = \varepsilon/2$, and $\rho_0$ such that points of $K'$ closer than $\rho_0$ have $\|D\Phi(x) - D\Phi(x')\| < \eta$. Eventually $F_i$ is within $\min(\delta, \rho_0)$ of $F_0$ on $[a, b]$, so $F_i(s) \in K'$ and $\|D\Phi(F_i(s)) - D\Phi(F_0(s))\| < \eta$.
3. Off the finitely many exceptional points, by the chain rule,
$$(\Phi \circ F_i)' - (\Phi \circ F_0)' = \big(D\Phi(F_i) - D\Phi(F_0)\big)F_i' + D\Phi(F_0)\,(F_i' - F_0'),$$
so its squared norm is at most $2\eta^2\|F_i'\|^2 + 2M^2\|F_i' - F_0'\|^2$.
4. Integrating, the first part is at most $2\eta^2 C \le \varepsilon/2$, and the second is eventually $< \varepsilon/2$ by the $L^2$ convergence.

### IX.14 Good balls

Milnor's $\varepsilon_1$ asks for unique minimal geodesics depending differentiably on their end points. The modified proof needs three things more. First, a uniform scale: the same uniqueness for all pairs of a region at once. Second, convexity: the minimal geodesics between points of a region stay in it, so that a deformation along them does not leave the region. Third, one smooth map $(y, z, s) \mapsto \sigma_{yz}(s)$ on each whole region, not only near each pair. Lemma 17.B of the exposition gets these from J. H. C. Whitehead's convex balls. The formalization gets convexity from the ambient distance instead, without normal coordinates.

*Corollary 10.8, recalled* (Part VI). For a scale $\delta$ and points $y, z \in N$, the conclusion of Corollary 10.8 says: there is $v \in T_yN$ with $\|v\| < \delta$, $\exp_y v = z$ and $\rho(y, z) = \|v\|$; it is the only tangent vector at $y$ of norm $< \delta$ with $\exp_y(\cdot) = z$; and it is the value at $(y, z)$ of a map $G$, smooth on an open set of pairs, which gives such vectors for the nearby pairs at distance $< \delta$ ⟦Chapter7/Distance · IsShortJoin⟧. Lemma 10.3, read with the distance, gives around each $x \in N$ a number $s > 0$ and an open set $\Omega_x \ni x$ such that points of $N \cap \Omega_x$ are at distance $< s$ from $x$, and every $y \in N \cap \Omega_x$ and $z$ at distance $< \delta \le s$ from $y$ are joined as in Corollary 10.8 at the scale $\delta$ ⟦Chapter7/Distance · exists_isShortJoin⟧.

**The minimal join vector.** A vector $w$ is *the minimal join vector* from $y$ to $z$ if $y, z \in N$, $w \in T_yN$, $\exp_y w = z$, $\rho(y, z) = \|w\|$, and the conclusion of Corollary 10.8 holds for $y, z$ at some scale $\delta > \|w\|$ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · IsMinJoin⟧. It is unique: two such vectors have the same norm $\rho(y, z)$, smaller than the scale of the first, and the uniqueness in Corollary 10.8 identifies both with its vector ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · IsMinJoin.eq⟧. The function $v(y, z)$ is defined as the minimal join vector when there is one, and $0$ otherwise ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · minJoin⟧. It is one global function, so it does not depend on the region in which it is found ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · minJoin_unique⟧ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · isMinJoin_minJoin⟧. It is always tangent at $y$ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · minJoin_mem_tangentSubspace⟧, it is the vector of Corollary 10.8 when that applies ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · isMinJoin_of_isShortJoin⟧, and $v(y, y) = 0$ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · minJoin_self⟧. The *minimal geodesic* is $\sigma_{yz}(s) = \exp_y(s\,v(y, z))$ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · minGeo⟧. It starts at $y$ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · minGeo_zero⟧, is constant when $y = z$ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · minGeo_self⟧, and has initial velocity $v(y, z)$ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · hasDerivAt_minGeo_zero⟧. When there is a minimal join vector, it lies in the domain of $\exp$ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · IsMinJoin.mem_expDom⟧, and $\sigma_{yz}$ is a geodesic on an open interval containing $[0, 1]$, of constant speed $\|v(y, z)\|$ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · IsMinJoin.isGeodesicOn_minGeo⟧.

**Convexity from the ambient distance.** Three lemmas replace Whitehead's theorem.

- *A bound for the second fundamental form.* For $x \in N$ there is $R > 0$ with $R\,\|\mathrm{II}_y(v, v)\| \le \|v\|^2$ for $y \in N$, $\|y - x\| < R$, $v \in T_yN$ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · exists_secondFundForm_bound⟧. Near $x$, $\mathrm{II}_y(v, v)$ is the derivative of the smooth ambient extension of $y \mapsto P_y$ in a slice chart at $x$, applied to $v$ twice ⟦Chapter6/Projection · projRep⟧ ⟦Chapter6/Projection · dN_tanProj_eq_fderiv⟧. That derivative is continuous at $x$, so its norm is below $C = \|D(\text{extension})(x)\| + 1$ on some ball $B(x, R_1)$; then $R = \min(R_1, 1/C)$ works, as $R\,\|\mathrm{II}_y(v, v)\| \le C^{-1} \cdot C\|v\|^2$.
- *Convexity of the squared distance.* Under that bound, along a geodesic $\gamma$ that stays within $R$ of $x$ for $s \in [a, b]$, the function $s \mapsto \|\gamma(s) - x\|^2$ is convex on $[a, b]$ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · convexOn_norm_sub_sq⟧. Its second derivative is $2\langle \gamma - x, \gamma''\rangle + 2\|\gamma'\|^2$; along a geodesic $\gamma'' = \mathrm{II}(\gamma', \gamma')$ ⟦Chapter7/Geodesic · IsGeodesicOn.deriv_deriv_eq⟧, and $|\langle \gamma - x, \gamma''\rangle| \le R\,\|\mathrm{II}(\gamma', \gamma')\| \le \|\gamma'\|^2$. A non-negative second derivative gives convexity ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Convex/Deriv · convexOn_of_hasDerivWithinAt2_nonneg⟧.
- *Short geodesics stay in small balls.* Under the same bound, a geodesic $\gamma$ on $(-\eta, 1 + \eta)$ with $\gamma(0), \gamma(1) \in B(x, r)$ and $r + \|\gamma'(0)\| \le R$ maps $[0, 1]$ into $B(x, r)$ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · dist_lt_of_isGeodesicOn⟧. The speed is constant ⟦Chapter7/Geodesic · IsGeodesicOn.norm_deriv_eq⟧, so $\|\gamma(s) - \gamma(0)\| \le \|\gamma'(0)\|\,s$ and $\gamma$ stays within $R$ of $x$ on $[0, 1]$. There $\|\gamma - x\|^2$ is convex, so it is at most the larger of its values at $0$ and $1$, which are $< r^2$.

**Good balls.** An open set $O \subseteq V$ is a *good ball* if (shortJoin) for one $\delta > 0$ any two points $y, z$ of $N \cap O$ are at distance $< \delta$ and joined as in Corollary 10.8 at the scale $\delta$, and (convex) $\sigma_{yz}(s) \in N \cap O$ for $y, z \in N \cap O$ and $0 \le s \le 1$ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · IsGoodBall⟧. Good balls are balls of $V$ intersected with $N$, not metric balls of $\rho$. In a good ball $O$, for $y, z \in N \cap O$:

- $v(y, z)$ is the minimal join vector ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · IsGoodBall.isMinJoin⟧ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · IsGoodBall.join⟧; it is the only tangent vector of norm $< \delta$, for one $\delta$ uniform on $O$, with $\exp_y(\cdot) = z$ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · IsGoodBall.exists_uniq⟧; and a tangent vector $w$ at $y$ with $\exp_y w = z$ and $\rho(y, z) = \|w\|$ is $v(y, z)$ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · IsGoodBall.eq_minJoin⟧. So $\sigma_{yz}$ is the unique minimal geodesic from $y$ to $z$; it has length $\|v(y, z)\| = \rho(y, z)$ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · IsGoodBall.norm_minJoin⟧, ends at $z$ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · IsGoodBall.minGeo_one⟧, and is a geodesic of constant speed ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · IsGoodBall.isGeodesicOn_minGeo⟧.
- Near each pair, $v$ agrees with the smooth map $G$ of Corollary 10.8 ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · IsGoodBall.eventually_minJoin_eq⟧: $G$ gives tangent vectors of norm $< \delta$ reaching the second point, and uniqueness at the scale $\delta$ identifies them with $v$. Hence $v$ is continuous on $(N \cap O)^2$ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · IsGoodBall.continuousOn⟧, and so is $(y, z, s) \mapsto \sigma_{yz}(s)$ on $(N \cap O)^2 \times [0, 1]$, as $\exp$ is continuous near the points of its domain ⟦Chapter7/GeodesicFlow · eventually_mem_expDom_and_continuousWithinAt_exp⟧ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · IsGoodBall.continuousOn_minGeo⟧.
- Sub-arcs of minimal geodesics are minimal geodesics: $v(y, \sigma_{yz}(s)) = s\,v(y, z)$ for $0 \le s \le 1$, by uniqueness at the uniform scale, since $\sigma_{yz}(s) \in N \cap O$ by convexity ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · IsGoodBall.minJoin_minGeo⟧. So the minimal geodesic from $y$ to $\sigma_{yz}(s)$ is $\tau \mapsto \sigma_{yz}(\tau s)$, and $\rho(y, \sigma_{yz}(s)) = s\,\rho(y, z)$ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · IsGoodBall.minGeo_minGeo⟧ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · IsGoodBall.riemDist_minGeo⟧. A deformation of Milnor's kind, which moves a break point along the path, needs these facts to fix broken geodesics; the deformation actually used (§IX.16) does not.

**Smooth dependence on the end points.** Near each pair $(y_0, z_0)$ of points of $N \cap O$ there are an open set $U \ni (y_0, z_0)$ of $V \times V$, a number $\varepsilon > 0$, and a map $\Phi$ smooth on $U \times (-\varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon)$, with $\Phi((y, z), s) = \sigma_{yz}(s)$ for all $s$ and all $y, z \in N \cap O$ with $(y, z) \in U$ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · IsGoodBall.exists_smooth_minGeo⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Take the smooth $G$ of Corollary 10.8 near $(y_0, z_0)$, and a smooth retraction $r$ onto $N$ near $y_0$, read off a slice chart ⟦Chapter11/ExpSmooth · exists_retraction⟧. The initial data $c(y, z) = r(y)$ and $\bar w(y, z) = P_{r(y)}G(y, z)$ are smooth and tangent, and on pairs of the good ball they are $y$ and $v(y, z)$. The radial geodesics $(u, t) \mapsto \exp_{c(u)}(t\,\bar w(u))$ ⟦Chapter11/ExpSmooth · raysE⟧ depend smoothly on $(u, t)$ on a neighbourhood of $\{(y_0, z_0)\} \times [0, 1]$, because the base geodesic $\sigma_{y_0z_0}$ exists on an open interval containing $[0, 1]$, and geodesics depend smoothly on their initial data over compact intervals ⟦Chapter11/ExpSmooth · raysGoodE_of_isGeodesicOn⟧ (Part X).

**Lemma 17.B: every point is the centre of good balls.** For $x \in N$ there is $r_0 > 0$ such that the ambient balls $B(x, r)$ are good for all $r \le r_0$ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · exists_isGoodBall_ball⟧.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. Take $R$ from the bound on the second fundamental form at $x$, and $s$, $\Omega_x$ from Lemma 10.3 read with the distance. Put $t = \min(R/3, s/2)$.
2. Choose $r_1$ such that the points of $N \cap B(x, r_1)$ are at distance $< t$ from $x$, which is possible since the $\rho$-balls form a basis of neighbourhoods (§IX.1), and $r_2$ with $B(x, r_2) \subseteq \Omega_x$. Put $r_0 = \min(r_1, r_2, R/3)$, and let $r \le r_0$.
3. *Short joins.* For $y, z \in N \cap B(x, r)$, $\rho(y, z) \le \rho(y, x) + \rho(x, z) < 2t \le s$, and $y \in \Omega_x$, so $y$ and $z$ are joined as in Corollary 10.8 at the scale $s$.
4. *Convexity.* The minimal geodesic $\sigma_{yz}$ is a geodesic with $\sigma(0) = y$, $\sigma(1) = z$ and $\|\sigma'(0)\| = \rho(y, z) < 2t \le 2R/3$. As $r \le R/3$, $r + \|\sigma'(0)\| < R$, and the third convexity lemma keeps $\sigma([0, 1])$ in $B(x, r)$; it lies in $N$, being a geodesic of $N$.

Two variants follow: good balls inside any given ball $B(x, \varepsilon)$ ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · exists_isGoodBall⟧, and a Lebesgue number: for compact $K \subseteq N$ there is $\varepsilon > 0$ such that every ball $B(x, \varepsilon)$, $x \in K$, lies in a good ball ⟦Chapter10/GoodBalls · exists_lebesgue_goodBall⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/MetricSpace/Pseudo/Lemmas · lebesgue_number_lemma_of_metric⟧. The proof of 17.1 uses a refinement of the latter (§IX.15).

### IX.15 Systems of joins

The topological part of the proof only uses a few properties of the minimal geodesics, and the formalization isolates them.

**Definition.** A *system of joins* for $N$ consists of a map $(y, z, u) \mapsto \sigma_{yz}(u)$ from $V \times V \times \mathbb R$ to $V$, the *joins*, and a family of *good sets*, such that ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem⟧:

- every good set $O$ is open;
- (convexity) for $y, z \in N \cap O$ and $u \in [0, 1]$, $\sigma_{yz}(u) \in N \cap O$;
- $\sigma_{yz}(0) = y$ for $y \in N$; $\sigma_{yz}(1) = z$ for $y, z \in N \cap O$, $O$ good; and $\sigma_{yy}(u) = y$;
- (smoothness) for each good set $O$ there are an open $U \supseteq (N \cap O)^2$, a number $\varepsilon > 0$, and a map $\Phi$ smooth on $U \times (-\varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon)$ with $\Phi((y, z), u) = \sigma_{yz}(u)$ for $y, z \in N \cap O$ and $u \in (-\varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon)$;
- (Lebesgue numbers) for every compact $K \subseteq N$ there is $\varepsilon > 0$ such that each ball $B(x, \varepsilon)$, $x \in K$, lies in a good set.

The smoothness asks for **one** smooth map on a whole good set. Milnor's differentiable dependence on the end points, and the local smoothness of §IX.14, give it only near each pair. The good balls are turned into a system of joins by gluing.

**Smooth gluing with a parameter.** Let $K \subseteq S$ with $K$ compact in a finite-dimensional space $E$, and $g : E \times \mathbb R \to F$. Suppose every $x \in K$ has an open neighbourhood $U_x$, a number $\varepsilon_x > 0$ and a map $\Phi_x$ smooth on $U_x \times (-\varepsilon_x, 1 + \varepsilon_x)$ with $\Phi_x(y, u) = g(y, u)$ for $y \in S \cap U_x$ and all $u$. Then there are $\varepsilon > 0$ and one map $\Phi$, smooth on $E \times (-\varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon)$, with $\Phi(y, u) = g(y, u)$ for $y \in K$ and all $u$ ⟦Chapter10/GoodJoin · exists_smooth_glue⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Finitely many $U_{x_i}$ cover $K$. Take a smooth partition of unity $\psi_i$ on $E$ subordinate to them on $K$ ⟦Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/PartitionOfUnity · SmoothPartitionOfUnity.exists_isSubordinate⟧, let $\varepsilon$ be the least $\varepsilon_{x_i}$, and put $\Phi(y, u) = \sum_i \psi_i(y)\,\Phi_{x_i}(y, u)$. Each term is smooth on $E \times (-\varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon)$: near a point with $y \in U_{x_i}$ because both factors are, and near a point with $y \notin U_{x_i}$ because it vanishes on a neighbourhood, $y$ being outside the closed support of $\psi_i$. For $y \in K$, every $i$ with $\psi_i(y) \ne 0$ has $y \in U_{x_i}$, so $\Phi_{x_i}(y, u) = g(y, u)$, and $\sum_i \psi_i(y) = 1$.

**Local compactness.** For $x \in N$ there is $r > 0$ with $N \cap \bar B(x, r)$ compact ⟦Chapter10/GoodJoin · exists_isCompact_inter_closedBall⟧: inside the domain of a slice chart, $N$ is the zero set of the normal component, so $N \cap \bar B(x, r)$ is closed in the compact ball.

**The good sets.** For $x \in N$ and $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $r \in (0, \varepsilon)$ such that the balls $B(x, r')$ are good for all $r' \le 2r$ and $N \cap \bar B(x, r)$ is compact ⟦Chapter10/GoodJoin · exists_goodSet⟧: take $r = \min(r_0/2, r_1, \varepsilon/2)$ with $r_0$ from Lemma 17.B and $r_1$ from local compactness.

**The system of joins of good balls.** The joins are the minimal geodesics $\sigma_{yz}$ of §IX.14, and the good sets are the balls $B(x, r)$, $x \in N$, of the previous statement ⟦Chapter10/GoodJoin · goodJoin⟧ ⟦Chapter10/GoodJoin · goodJoin_geo⟧.

*The verification as formalized.* Openness, convexity, and the end-point conditions hold because $B(x, r)$ itself is a good ball. For smoothness, the compact set $K = (N \cap \bar B(x, r))^2$ lies in $(N \cap B(x, 2r))^2$, and $B(x, 2r)$ is a good ball. Near each pair of $K$ its minimal geodesics have a local smooth extension (§IX.14), valid for all parameters $u$, and gluing gives one map $\Phi$, smooth on $(V \times V) \times (-\varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon)$, which equals $\sigma$ on $K$ and so on $(N \cap B(x, r))^2$. This is why the definition asks for good balls up to radius $2r$: the closed ball $\bar B(x, r)$, needed for compactness, must sit inside an open good ball. For Lebesgue numbers, cover the compact $K \subseteq N$ by good sets $B(y, r_y)$, $y \in K$, and take a Lebesgue number of this cover.

### IX.16 Dyadic pieces, and the deformation inside $\Omega^*$

Fix a system of joins $J$. The modified proof replaces his variable subdivision $0, A(\omega), 2A(\omega), \dots$ by the dyadic subdivisions, which are fixed, and restricts each to the open set of paths for which it is fine enough.

*Dyadic subdivisions.* The dyadic points of level $k$ are the $j/2^k$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathSpace · dy⟧, and $j_k(s) = \min(\lfloor s\,2^k \rfloor, 2^k - 1)$ is the index of a dyadic interval of level $k$ containing $s \in [0, 1]$, the cap putting $s = 1$ in the last one ⟦Chapter10/FullPathSpace · dseg⟧ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathSpace · mem_Icc_dseg⟧ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · dseg_dy⟧ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · dseg_eq_or⟧. Each dyadic interval of level $k + 1$ lies in one of level $k$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathSpace · Icc_dy_succ_subset⟧.

**Definitions.** A function $c$ is *good at level $k$* if each dyadic piece $c([j/2^k, (j+1)/2^k])$, $j < 2^k$, lies in $N \cap O$ for some good set $O$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.IsGood⟧. Its *dyadic broken geodesic* is
$$b_k(c)(s) = \sigma_{c(j/2^k)\,c((j+1)/2^k)}\big(s\,2^k - j\big), \qquad j = j_k(s)$$
⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.broken⟧, and the *deformation* moves each point of $c$ along the join to the corresponding point of $b_k(c)$:
$$H_k(c, u)(s) = \sigma_{c(s)\,b_k(c)(s)}(u)$$
⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.interp⟧.

This deformation is not Milnor's $r_u$ of §16, which the exposition's Lemma 17.C reuses and which moves a break point along the path. Moving every point along a join keeps each piece in its good set by convexity, and is given by one smooth formula on each piece, which is what continuity for $d$ needs.

For $c$ good at level $k$:

- On the $j$-th piece, $b_k(c)(s) = \sigma_{c(j/2^k)\,c((j+1)/2^k)}(s\,2^k - j)$, also at the right end point, where $j_k$ jumps to the next piece; there both formulas give $c((j+1)/2^k)$, by the end-point conditions ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.IsGood.broken_eq⟧ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.param_mem_Icc⟧.
- *Convexity keeps everything in place.* On the $j$-th piece, $b_k(c)(s) \in N \cap O$, since it is a join of two points of $N \cap O$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.broken_mem_of⟧, and then $H_k(c, u)(s) \in N \cap O$ for $u \in [0, 1]$, as a join of $c(s)$ and $b_k(c)(s)$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.interp_mem_of⟧. So $b_k(c)$ and every $H_k(c, u)$ are good at level $k$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.IsGood.broken⟧ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.IsGood.interp⟧.
- $H_k(c, 0) = c$ and $H_k(c, 1) = b_k(c)$ on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.interp_zero⟧ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.interp_one⟧. The broken geodesic passes through the dyadic points of $c$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.broken_dy⟧, in particular through $c(0)$ and $c(1)$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.broken_zero⟧ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.broken_one⟧, so the deformation fixes the end points, by $\sigma_{yy}(u) = y$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.interp_zero_right⟧ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.interp_one_right⟧.
- All of this only sees the values of $c$ on $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.isGood_congr⟧ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.broken_congr⟧ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.interp_congr⟧, and good at level $k$ implies good at level $k + 1$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.IsGood.mono⟧.

**The sets $Y_k$** (Lemma 17.C (i) of the exposition). A continuous path $\gamma \in \Omega^*$, extended by constants off $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.ext⟧, lies in $Y_k$ if it is good at level $k$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.YSet⟧, that is, if each piece $\gamma([j/2^k, (j+1)/2^k])$ maps into a good set ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.mem_YSet_iff⟧.

- *$Y_k$ is open in $\Omega^*$* ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.isOpen_YSet⟧. For a compact piece and an open good set, the paths mapping the piece into the set form a neighbourhood in the compact-open topology ⟦Mathlib/Topology/CompactOpen · ContinuousMap.eventually_mapsTo⟧ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.isCompact_piece⟧; intersect over the $2^k$ pieces.
- *$Y_k \subseteq Y_{k+1}$* ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.YSet_mono⟧, as dyadic pieces refine.
- *Every continuous path lies in some $Y_k$* ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.exists_mem_YSet⟧. The image $\gamma([0, 1])$ is a compact subset of $N$; let $\varepsilon$ be a Lebesgue number for it. By uniform continuity there is $\delta$ with $|t - t'| < \delta \Rightarrow \|\gamma(t) - \gamma(t')\| < \varepsilon$; take $k$ with $2^{-k} < \delta$. Then each piece maps into $B(\gamma(j/2^k), \varepsilon)$, which lies in a good set.

**The deformation is continuous on $Y_k$.** The map $(\gamma, u, s) \mapsto H_k(\gamma, u)(s)$ is continuous on $Y_k \times [0, 1] \times [0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.continuous_interp_star⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* The closed sets $\{s \in [j/2^k, (j+1)/2^k]\}$ form a finite cover, so it suffices to prove continuity on each ⟦Mathlib/Topology/LocallyFinite · LocallyFinite.continuous⟧. Near a point $(\gamma_0, u_0, s_0)$ of the $j$-th one, let $O$ be the good set containing the $j$-th piece of $\gamma_0$, and $\Phi$ its smooth map. Nearby paths still map the $j$-th piece into $O$, by the compact-open argument, and for them
$$H_k(\gamma, u)(s) = \Phi\Big(\big(\gamma(s),\ \Phi((\gamma(j/2^k), \gamma((j+1)/2^k)),\ s\,2^k - j)\big),\ u\Big),$$
a composite of continuous maps, since evaluation $(\gamma, s) \mapsto \gamma(s)$ is continuous on $\Omega^* \times \mathbb R$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.continuous_ext⟧.

Since each $H_k(\gamma, u)$ is good at level $k$ and runs from $p$ to $q$, the deformation is a continuous map $H_k^* : Y_k \times [0, 1] \to Y_k$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.interpStar⟧ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.interpStar_apply⟧, with $H_k^*(\gamma, 0) = \gamma$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathDeform · JoinSystem.interpStar_zero⟧.

### IX.17 The deformation inside $\Omega$: $X_k$ is homotopy equivalent to $Y_k$

This is the analytic heart of the modified proof, the step at which Milnor's $h$ falls short: the maps must be continuous for $d$.

*Smooth formulas on pieces.* An interval $[a, b] \subseteq [0, 1]$ with no dyadic point of level $k$ inside lies in one dyadic piece ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.exists_piece_of_Icc⟧. On the $j$-th piece of a function $c$ good at level $k$, with good set $O$ and smooth map $\Phi$,
$$b_k(c)(s) = \Phi\big((c(j/2^k), c((j+1)/2^k)),\ s\,2^k - j\big), \qquad H_k(c, u)(s) = \Phi\big((c(s),\ b_k(c)(s)),\ u\big)$$
⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.IsGood.exists_formula⟧. So $b_k(c)$ is smooth on every interval free of the finite set of dyadic points ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.dyPts⟧ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.IsGood.contDiffOn_broken⟧, and $H_k(c, u)$ on every such interval on which $c$ is smooth ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.IsGood.contDiffOn_interp⟧. If $c(0) = p$ and $c(1) = q$, then $b_k(c) \in \Omega$, with break points among the dyadic points ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.IsGood.broken_mem_pathSpace⟧; and if $c \in \Omega$, then $H_k(c, u) \in \Omega$, with break points among the dyadic points and those of $c$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.IsGood.interp_mem_pathSpace⟧.

*The sets $X_k$.* $X_k \subseteq \Omega$ is the set of piecewise smooth paths good at level $k$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.XSet⟧, which is $i^{-1}(Y_k)$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.XSet_eq_preimage⟧. The inclusion $i : X_k \to Y_k$ is continuous ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.toY⟧. To produce elements of $\Omega$ the maps below are normalized to be constant off $[0, 1]$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.clampF⟧ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.clampF_eqOn⟧; since $d$ only sees $[0, 1]$, this changes nothing but makes the values definite.

**The broken geodesic $b : Y_k \to X_k$** ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.brokenΩ⟧ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.brokenΩ_eqOn⟧ **is continuous from $d^*$ to $d$** ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.continuous_brokenΩ⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* By the criterion of §IX.13 it suffices to prove pointwise convergence and $L^2$ convergence of the velocities as $\gamma \to \gamma_0$ in $Y_k$. Pointwise convergence: $b(\gamma)(s) = H_k^*(\gamma, 1)(s)$, which is continuous in $\gamma$ (§IX.16). For the velocities, cut $[0, 1]$ at the dyadic points and add over adjacent intervals ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.integral_deriv_sub_sq_add⟧ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.tendsto_integral_brokenΩ⟧. On an interval inside the $j$-th piece, $b(\gamma) = \Phi \circ F_\gamma$ with $F_\gamma(s) = ((\gamma(j/2^k), \gamma((j+1)/2^k)), s\,2^k - j)$ for all $\gamma$ near $\gamma_0$, since nearby paths keep the piece in the good set. The curves $F_\gamma$ converge uniformly to $F_{\gamma_0}$, because their vertices converge, and their velocities are all the constant $((0, 0), 2^k)$. So smooth maps preserving $L^2$ convergence of velocities (§IX.13) gives the claim on the interval.

**The deformation $H : X_k \times [0, 1] \to X_k$** ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.interpΩ⟧ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.interpΩ_eqOn⟧ **is continuous for $d$** ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.continuous_interpΩ⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Let $(\gamma, u) \to (\gamma_0, u_0)$ in $X_k \times [0, 1]$.

1. *Pointwise convergence* goes through $\Omega^*$: $H(\gamma, u)(s) = H_k^*(i\gamma, u)(s)$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.interpΩ_eq_star⟧, which is continuous in $(\gamma, u)$ because $i$ and $H_k^*$ are.
2. *The ingredients converge in $\Omega$.* $\gamma \to \gamma_0$ in $\Omega$, and $b(i\gamma) \to b(i\gamma_0)$ in $\Omega$ by the continuity of $b$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.brokenΩ_toY_eqOn⟧. By §IX.13 both converge uniformly, their velocities converge in $L^2$, and their energies converge.
3. *On a piece.* Cut $[0, 1]$ at the dyadic points and at the break points of $\gamma_0$. On an interval inside the $j$-th piece, $H(\gamma, u) = \Phi \circ F_{\gamma, u}$ with $F_{\gamma, u}(s) = ((\gamma(s), b(i\gamma)(s)), u)$, for all $(\gamma, u)$ near $(\gamma_0, u_0)$. The velocity of $F_{\gamma, u}$ is $((\gamma', b(i\gamma)'), 0)$ off finitely many points ⟦Chapter10/FullPathSpace · PathSp.hasDerivAt_of_notMem⟧. Its squared norm is at most $\|\gamma'\|^2 + \|b(i\gamma)'\|^2$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.norm_pair_zero_sq_le⟧, so $\int \|F_{\gamma, u}'\|^2 \le E(\gamma) + E(b(i\gamma)) \le C$ eventually. Likewise $\int\|F_{\gamma, u}' - F_{\gamma_0, u_0}'\|^2$ is at most the sum of the $L^2$ distances of the two velocities, which tends to $0$ by step 2 (the integrands are integrable, as continuous functions of four piecewise smooth velocities ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.intervalIntegrable_comp_deriv₄⟧). $F_{\gamma, u} \to F_{\gamma_0, u_0}$ uniformly, by step 2 and $u \to u_0$. Smooth maps preserve $L^2$ convergence of velocities (§IX.13), which gives the $L^2$ convergence of the velocities of $H(\gamma, u)$ on the interval.
4. Adding over the intervals and applying the criterion of §IX.13 concludes.

**Lemma 17.C (ii) of the exposition.** For every $k$, the inclusion $i : X_k \to Y_k$ is a homotopy equivalence, with homotopy inverse $b$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.isHomotopyEquiv_toY⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Both homotopies are the deformation, run backwards.

- *$b \circ i \simeq \mathrm{id}$ on $X_k$.* At time $t$ take $H(\gamma, 1 - t)$, except at $t = 1$, where take $\gamma$ itself. At $t = 0$ this is $H(\gamma, 1) = b(i\gamma)$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.interpΩ_one⟧. At $t = 1$, $H(\gamma, 0)$ agrees with $\gamma$ on $[0, 1]$, so it is at distance $0$ from $\gamma$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.edist_interpΩ_zero⟧ but need not equal it as a function on $\mathbb R$. The modified homotopy is at distance $0$ from the continuous $(t, \gamma) \mapsto H(\gamma, 1 - t)$, hence continuous (§IX.1). This is the one place where the pseudometric needs care.
- *$i \circ b \simeq \mathrm{id}$ on $Y_k$.* $(t, \gamma) \mapsto H_k^*(\gamma, 1 - t)$, which is $i(b(\gamma))$ at $t = 0$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathOmega · JoinSystem.toY_brokenΩ⟧ and $\gamma$ at $t = 1$.

### IX.18 Theorem 17.1

It remains to glue the homotopy equivalences $X_k \to Y_k$. The sets $X_k$ and $Y_k$ are open, increasing, and exhaust $\Omega$ and $\Omega^*$. Milnor's Appendix provides the gluing, with two adjustments.

**Example 1, pulled back.** Let $Y$ be paracompact and Hausdorff, $B_0 \subseteq B_1 \subseteq \cdots$ subsets of $Y$ such that every point lies in the interior of some $B_n$, and $g : X \to Y$ continuous. Then $X$ is the homotopy direct limit of the sets $g^{-1}(B_n)$, with no assumption on $X$ ⟦Chapter10/FullPathSpace · isHomotopyDirectLimit_preimage⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Take a partition of unity $(\rho_n)$ on $Y$ subordinate to the open sets $\operatorname{int} B_n$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/PartitionOfUnity · PartitionOfUnity.exists_isSubordinate⟧, and $f = \sum_n n\,\rho_n$, continuous by local finiteness. If $x \notin g^{-1}(B_i)$, then $\rho_j(g(x)) = 0$ for $j \le i$, since the support of $\rho_j$ lies in $B_j \subseteq B_i$. Hence $f(g(x)) = \sum_{j > i} j\,\rho_j(g(x)) \ge (i + 1)\sum_j \rho_j(g(x)) = i + 1$. Lemma 2.1 of the exposition, the mechanism of Example 1, then applies to $f \circ g$ on $X$ ⟦Appendix/Telescope · isHomotopyDirectLimit_of_level⟧. Example 1 itself is the case $g = \mathrm{id}$ ⟦Appendix/Examples · example1⟧.

The point is that $\Omega$ is not Hausdorff: its pseudometric does not separate paths that agree on $[0, 1]$. So Example 1, which the Appendix states for paracompact Hausdorff spaces, is applied to $\Omega^*$, a metric space, which is paracompact by A. H. Stone's theorem (provided by Mathlib), and the partition of unity is pulled back to $\Omega$ along $i$.

**Theorem A on open pieces.** If $X$ and $Y$ are the homotopy direct limits of $\{X_n\}$ and $\{Y_n\}$, and $f : X \to Y$ carries each $X_n$ into $Y_n$ by a homotopy equivalence, then $f$ is a homotopy equivalence ⟦Appendix/MonotoneUnion · theoremA_general⟧ (Part XI). This is Milnor's statement of Theorem A, with no closedness hypothesis. His proof needs closed pieces, and the $X_k$ and $Y_k$ here are open. The Appendix's formal proof compares $X$ and $Y$ with the mapping telescopes of their inclusions ⟦Appendix/MonotoneUnion · isHomotopyEquiv_telescopeProj'⟧, whose truncations are closed, runs Milnor's proof there ⟦Appendix/TheoremA · theoremA⟧, and transfers the result through the projections, which are homotopy equivalences.

**Theorem 17.1 from a system of joins.** For every system of joins $J$ and all $p, q$, the inclusion $i : \Omega(N; p, q) \to \Omega^*(N; p, q)$ is a homotopy equivalence ⟦Chapter10/FullPathTheorem · JoinSystem.isHomotopyEquiv_toPathC⟧.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. Every $\gamma \in \Omega^*$ lies in some $Y_k$, which is open, hence in its interior.
2. $\Omega^*$ is the homotopy direct limit of the $Y_k$ by Example 1, and $\Omega$ is that of the $X_k = i^{-1}(Y_k)$ by Example 1 pulled back along $i$.
3. $i$ maps $X_k$ into $Y_k$, and its restriction there is the inclusion $X_k \to Y_k$ of §IX.17, a homotopy equivalence ⟦Appendix/Telescope · restrictTo⟧.
4. Theorem A on open pieces concludes.

**Theorem 17.1** (Milnor p. 93). For every slice submanifold $N$ of $V$, complete or not, and all $p, q$, the inclusion $i : \Omega(N; p, q) \to \Omega^*(N; p, q)$ of the piecewise smooth paths, with Milnor's metric, into the continuous paths, with the compact-open topology, is a homotopy equivalence ⟦Chapter10/FullPathTheorem · theorem_17_1⟧. It is the previous theorem for the system of joins of good balls (§IX.15).

No completeness is used: good balls exist around every point of every slice submanifold, and the Lebesgue numbers are taken on compact images of paths. Completeness is needed for Theorem 17.3, and its absence is the subject of §IX.11.

### IX.19 What the formalization taught us

- **The one step of the book that does not hold as we read it.** Milnor's claim that $h$ is clearly continuous (p. 94) fails for the metric $d$ of $\Omega$. When $1/A(\omega)$ crosses an integer from below, $h(\omega)$ acquires a new last segment whose duration $\delta$ and length $\ell$ tend to $0$ but whose energy $\ell^2/\delta$ need not, and for $\omega_\lambda = \lambda((1 - t)^{1/3} - (1 - t))$ it is at least $\tfrac{27}{64}\delta^{-1/3}$. The formal proof of non-continuity follows Milnor's definitions exactly, for $M = \mathbb R$ with the admissible choices $f = |\cdot|$ and $\varepsilon_2 \equiv 1$. Theorem 17.1 is true and is proved by another route.
- **What the modified proof needs that Milnor's construction does not provide.** Regions convex for the minimal geodesics, here from the ambient distance and a bound on the second fundamental form rather than from Whitehead's normal-coordinate argument. One smooth minimal-geodesic map on each whole region, obtained by gluing local ones with a partition of unity. A uniform scale of uniqueness. The control of velocities, not only speeds, by $d$. Care with $\Omega$ not being Hausdorff, in Example 1 and in one homotopy. And Theorem A for open pieces, through telescopes. No completeness is needed.
- **Theorem 17.3.** The levels must tend to $\infty$. Milnor's requirement of exactly one critical value per interval is impossible when there are finitely many geodesics, and even at most one is not needed for the homotopy type, only for reading homology off a slab. The ladder needs to commute only up to homotopy, so no nested subdivisions, no Whitehead theorem and no Corollary 17.2 are needed, but a relative Theorem 3.5, for pairs of sublevel sets below a compact one, is.
- **The sphere.** The list of geodesics must be shown complete, which uses uniqueness of geodesics after extending past the end points. The multiplicity $n - 1$ must be exact and the only conjugate points along a great circle must be the multiples of $\pi$, which determining all Jacobi fields vanishing at $0$ gives. Non-conjugacy of $p$ and $q$ must be proved, not assumed, and the index comes from Theorem 15.1.
- **Loops and completeness.** Milnor's proof of 17.4 gives the path space $\Omega(S^n; p, q)$, $q \ne \pm p$, not the loop space. Corollary 17.5 needs the completeness it omits, as $\mathbb R^{n+1} \setminus \{0\}$ shows.
- **Homology from the filtration.** No cell structure on $\Omega$ is needed for its homology: singular chains have compact supports, and compact subsets of $\Omega$ have bounded energy. That is true for Milnor's metric, because $E$ is continuous for it, and false for the topology of uniform convergence. The vanishing of the steps is needed in two consecutive degrees.
