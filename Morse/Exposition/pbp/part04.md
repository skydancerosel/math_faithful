## Part IV. Homology and the Morse inequalities (Milnor §5)

Milnor's §5 turns the cell decompositions of §3 into inequalities between numbers. Let $M$ be a compact manifold, $f$ a function on $M$ whose critical points are non-degenerate, $C_\lambda$ the number of critical points of index $\lambda$, and $R_\lambda(M) = \dim_F H_\lambda(M; F)$ the Betti numbers over a field $F$. Theorem 5.2 gives the weak inequalities $R_\lambda(M) \le C_\lambda$ and the equality $\sum_\lambda (-1)^\lambda R_\lambda(M) = \sum_\lambda (-1)^\lambda C_\lambda$; Lemma 5.3 leads to the strong inequalities $(4_\lambda)$; Corollary 5.4 turns some of them into equalities. The proof has two halves of very unequal weight in the formal text. The algebraic half (subadditive functions, Lemmas 5.1 and 5.3, and the bookkeeping of pp. 28–31) takes a page of the book and one file here. The topological half is a single line on p. 29, which identifies the homology of a slab $(M^{a_i}, M^{a_{i-1}})$ with that of a cell modulo its boundary "by excision"; it rests on relative singular homology, its exact sequences, homotopy invariance, excision and the homology of $(D^\lambda, S^{\lambda-1})$, all quoted as standard.

Mathlib, the library underneath, has the singular chain complex of a space, absolute singular homology with its homotopy invariance, the category of pairs of spaces with the data of an Eilenberg–Steenrod theory, and the long exact homology sequence of a short exact sequence of complexes. It has no relative singular homology, no exact sequence of a pair or of a triple, no excision, and no homology of a sphere. For most of its length this part is therefore an account of the singular homology that the project built: relative homology as a cokernel complex and the sequences of pairs and triples (§§IV.5–IV.6), homotopy invariance for pairs (§IV.7), a concrete model of singular chains (§IV.8), barycentric subdivision and the small-simplex theorem (§§IV.9–IV.12), excision and Mayer–Vietoris (§§IV.13–IV.14), and cells, disks and spheres (§§IV.15–IV.17). The Morse inequalities in singular homology then take the last four sections.

The formal route departs from Milnor's in five places, each explained where it occurs.

1. The algebra is proved once, for an abstract *homology of a filtration* whose only axioms are finite-dimensionality and the exact sequences of triples (§§IV.1–IV.4). Singular homology over a field is an instance (§IV.18).
2. Milnor chooses levels $a_i$ with exactly one critical point between consecutive ones, which presupposes distinct critical values. Here a slab contains one critical *value* and any number of critical points on it, whose cells are attached at once (Remark 3.3; Part II, §II.14), and the homology of the slab comes from a cell-attachment theorem for several disjoint cells (§IV.17).
3. "By excision" is not literally excision. The cell-attachment theorem first removes the centre of the cell and pushes the rest radially onto the bottom set, and then excises (§IV.15).
4. The top of a pair is moved along strong deformation retractions that fix the bottom; the bottom is moved in homology, through the exact sequence of a triple (§IV.19).
5. Everything is proved first for a compact submanifold of a Euclidean space and a function that extends smoothly to the ambient space, and then carried to abstract compact manifolds by a Whitney embedding (§IV.21). There it also identifies the Euler number of Part III with $\sum_i (-1)^i \dim H_i(M; \Bbbk)$, for every field $\Bbbk$.

Results carry the labels V3.7–V3.15 and V3.26–V3.40 of the verified edition (`Exposition/verification/ch03.md`). Throughout, $\Bbbk$ denotes a field (Milnor's $F$; the letter $F$ is kept for ambient functions). The general constructions allow coefficients in an object $R$ of an abelian category with coproducts; from excision on, $R$ is a commutative ring, viewed as a module over itself; the Morse inequalities use a field. One formality of type theory recurs: from excision on, the spaces whose homology is taken live in the universe of the coefficients, as the comparison of chain models in §IV.8 requires; §IV.19 explains how the final statements accommodate it. Singular chains, homology and Betti numbers were introduced in §0.6; this part says how they are built.

**Map.**

- `Chapter3/Inequalities`: alternating sums, truncated exact sequences, subadditive functions, Lemma 5.1, the homology of a filtration, Lemma 5.3, Theorem 5.2, $(4_\lambda)$, Corollary 5.4 (§§IV.1–IV.4).
- `Homology/Relative`, `Homology/Triple`: relative singular homology as a cokernel complex; the sequences of a pair and of a triple; $H_*(X, \emptyset)$, $H_*(X, X)$; the five lemma for pairs (§§IV.5–IV.6).

- `Homology/LinearChains`, `Homology/AffineSimplex`: linear chains, the cone, barycentric subdivision; affine simplices, barycentres, the diameter bound (§§IV.9–IV.10).

### IV.1 Alternating sums and finite exact sequences

The file `Chapter3/Inequalities` contains no space at all: only integers, finite-dimensional vector spaces over a field, and linear maps. Everything Milnor does on pp. 28–31 once the homology of the slabs is known is proved there.

**Definition.** For a sequence of integers $d = (d_0, d_1, \dots)$ and $n \in \mathbb N$, the *truncated alternating sum* is
$$\operatorname{alt}_n(d) = \sum_{i=0}^{n} (-1)^{n-i} d_i = d_n - d_{n-1} + d_{n-2} - \dots \pm d_0$$
⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · altSum⟧. With $d_i = R_i(X, Y)$ and $n = \lambda$ it is Milnor's $S_\lambda(X, Y)$ of Lemma 5.3; with $d_i = C_i$ it is the right side of $(4_\lambda)$.

Its algebra is elementary, and each fact is a lemma. $\operatorname{alt}_0(d) = d_0$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · altSum_zero⟧. Since $(-1)^{n+1-i} = -(-1)^{n-i}$ for $i \le n$, a new term changes the sign of all earlier ones: $\operatorname{alt}_{n+1}(d) = d_{n+1} - \operatorname{alt}_n(d)$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · altSum_succ⟧, hence
$$\operatorname{alt}_{n+1}(d) + \operatorname{alt}_n(d) = d_{n+1}$$
⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · altSum_succ_add_altSum⟧, the identity behind Milnor's remark that adding $(4_{\lambda+1})$ and $(4_\lambda)$ gives the weak inequality in degree $\lambda + 1$. The sum depends only on $d_0, \dots, d_n$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · altSum_congr⟧, is additive and subtractive in $d$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · altSum_add⟧ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · altSum_sub⟧, and commutes with finite sums, $\operatorname{alt}_n\big(\sum_k d^{(k)}\big) = \sum_k \operatorname{alt}_n(d^{(k)})$, by an exchange of two finite sums ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · altSum_sum⟧. The last is Milnor's rewriting of $\sum_i S_\lambda(M^{a_i}, M^{a_{i-1}})$ as $C_\lambda - C_{\lambda-1} + \dots \pm C_0$ (p. 30). The Euler characteristic truncated at $N$ is a signed alternating sum,
$$\sum_{i \le N} (-1)^i d_i = (-1)^N \operatorname{alt}_N(d)$$
⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · sum_neg_one_pow_mul_eq⟧: writing $N = i + k$, the term $(-1)^N (-1)^{N-i} d_i$ is $(-1)^{i + 2k} d_i = (-1)^i d_i$.

**Lemma (telescoping).** If $d_0 = r_0$ and $d_{i+1} = r_{i+1} + r_i$ for all $i < n$, then $\operatorname{alt}_n(d) = r_n$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · altSum_eq_of_telescope⟧.

*Proof.* Induction on $n$: $\operatorname{alt}_{n+1}(d) = d_{n+1} - \operatorname{alt}_n(d) = (r_{n+1} + r_n) - r_n$. $\square$

**Lemma (Milnor's computation, pp. 29–30; V3.12).** Let $V_0, V_1, \dots$ be finite-dimensional vector spaces over a field and $d_k : V_{k+1} \to V_k$ linear maps such that
$$V_{n+1} \xrightarrow{\ d_n\ } V_n \xrightarrow{\ d_{n-1}\ } \cdots \xrightarrow{\ d_0\ } V_0 \to 0$$
is exact at $V_0, \dots, V_n$: $d_0$ is onto, and $\ker d_k = \operatorname{im} d_{k+1}$ for $k < n$. Then
$$\operatorname{rank} d_n = \operatorname{alt}_n(\dim V_\bullet) = \dim V_n - \dim V_{n-1} + \dots \pm \dim V_0$$
⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · finrank_range_eq_altSum⟧.

*Proof.* The telescoping lemma with $r_k = \operatorname{rank} d_k$. First, $r_0 = \dim V_0$ because $d_0$ is onto. Second, rank–nullity for $d_k$ ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/FiniteDimensional/Lemmas · finrank_range_add_finrank_ker⟧ together with $\ker d_k = \operatorname{im} d_{k+1}$ gives $\dim V_{k+1} = r_k + r_{k+1}$. $\square$

In Milnor's notation $h = d_n$, $A = V_n$, $B = V_{n-1}$, …, $D = V_0$. His first step, $\operatorname{rank} h + \operatorname{rank} i = \dim A$, is the second equation for $k = n - 1$, and his unwinding along the sequence is the telescoping lemma. If moreover $d_n = 0$, so that the sequence is a finite exact sequence $0 \to V_n \to \dots \to V_0 \to 0$, the alternating sum of the dimensions vanishes ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · altSum_finrank_eq_zero_of_exact⟧. This is the argument behind the additivity of the Euler characteristic, which Milnor asserts without proof on p. 28. (Mathlib has the vanishing for sequences indexed by a finite interval; the triples need the form for a sequence indexed by $\mathbb N$ and cut off at $n$.)

**Definition (long exact sequences; V3.9).** A *long exact sequence* over a field $\Bbbk$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · LongExactSeq⟧ consists of three families $A_i, B_i, C_i$ ($i \in \mathbb N$) of $\Bbbk$-vector spaces and linear maps $f_i : A_i \to B_i$, $g_i : B_i \to C_i$, $\delta_i : C_{i+1} \to A_i$, such that
$$\cdots \to A_{i+1} \xrightarrow{f} B_{i+1} \xrightarrow{g} C_{i+1} \xrightarrow{\ \delta_i\ } A_i \xrightarrow{f} B_i \xrightarrow{g} C_i \to \cdots \to A_0 \to B_0 \to C_0 \to 0$$
is exact at every node: $\ker g_i = \operatorname{im} f_i$, $\ker \delta_i = \operatorname{im} g_{i+1}$, $\ker f_i = \operatorname{im} \delta_i$, and $g_0$ is onto. For a triple $X \supset Y \supset Z$ the intended values are $A_i = H_i(Y, Z)$, $B_i = H_i(X, Z)$, $C_i = H_i(X, Y)$, with $\delta$ the boundary of the triple. Milnor's sequence ends with "$\to D \to 0$" (p. 29); for a triple this is the surjectivity of $H_0(X, Z) \to H_0(X, Y)$, true because there is no homology in degree $-1$. Here it is an axiom of the structure, and §IV.6 proves it for singular homology.

Three lemmas about a long exact sequence carry Milnor's algebra.

- *The middle is finite-dimensional when the ends are.* If $A_i$ and $C_i$ are finite-dimensional, so is $B_i$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · LongExactSeq.finiteDimensional_middle⟧, since $A_i \to B_i \to \operatorname{im} g_i \to 0$ is exact and $\operatorname{im} g_i$ is a subspace of $C_i$. Milnor defines $R_\lambda$ only for pairs whose $\lambda$-th homology is finite-dimensional (p. 28). This lemma is the closedness of that class of pairs under $(X, Y), (Y, Z) \mapsto (X, Z)$, which his statement that $R_\lambda$ is subadditive presupposes.
- *$R_\lambda$ is subadditive* (p. 28, left to the reader; V3.10). If all the spaces are finite-dimensional, then $\dim B_i \le \dim A_i + \dim C_i$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · LongExactSeq.finrank_le⟧: by rank–nullity for $g_i$ and $\ker g_i = \operatorname{im} f_i$, $\dim B_i = \operatorname{rank} g_i + \operatorname{rank} f_i \le \dim C_i + \dim A_i$.
- *The key identity of Lemma 5.3* (p. 30). If all the spaces are finite-dimensional, then for every $\lambda$
$$\operatorname{rank} \delta_\lambda = \operatorname{alt}_\lambda(\dim A_\bullet) - \operatorname{alt}_\lambda(\dim B_\bullet) + \operatorname{alt}_\lambda(\dim C_\bullet)$$
⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · LongExactSeq.finrank_range_δ⟧. In Milnor's notation, $\operatorname{rank} \partial = S_\lambda(Y, Z) - S_\lambda(X, Z) + S_\lambda(X, Y)$.

*Proof of the key identity.* Rank–nullity at the three kinds of nodes gives
$$\dim A_i = \operatorname{rank} f_i + \operatorname{rank}\delta_i, \quad \dim B_i = \operatorname{rank} g_i + \operatorname{rank} f_i, \quad \dim C_{i+1} = \operatorname{rank}\delta_i + \operatorname{rank} g_{i+1}, \quad \dim C_0 = \operatorname{rank} g_0 .$$
So $e_i = \dim A_i - \dim B_i + \dim C_i$ satisfies $e_0 = \operatorname{rank}\delta_0$ and $e_{i+1} = \operatorname{rank}\delta_{i+1} + \operatorname{rank}\delta_i$. The telescoping lemma gives $\operatorname{alt}_\lambda(e) = \operatorname{rank}\delta_\lambda$, and linearity splits $\operatorname{alt}_\lambda(e)$ into the three sums. $\square$

Milnor unwinds the sequence $H_\lambda(Y, Z) \to H_\lambda(X, Z) \to H_\lambda(X, Y) \to H_{\lambda-1}(Y, Z) \to \dots \to 0$ term by term; the formal proof groups the terms by degree. Two corollaries follow at once. A rank is non-negative, so
$$\operatorname{alt}_\lambda(\dim B_\bullet) \le \operatorname{alt}_\lambda(\dim A_\bullet) + \operatorname{alt}_\lambda(\dim C_\bullet)$$
⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · LongExactSeq.altSum_le⟧, which is Lemma 5.3 for one triple. And if $C_{N+1} = 0$, then $\delta_N$ has rank $0$ and the truncated sums are additive, $\operatorname{alt}_N(\dim B_\bullet) = \operatorname{alt}_N(\dim A_\bullet) + \operatorname{alt}_N(\dim C_\bullet)$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · LongExactSeq.altSum_eq_of_finrank_eq_zero⟧. The additivity of the Euler characteristic on a triple therefore needs only $H_{N+1}(X, Y) = 0$, not the vanishing of all homology above $N$.

### IV.2 Subadditive functions and Lemma 5.1

The formalization replaces spaces by indices. A preorder $\iota$ indexes the spaces, $a \le b$ standing for $X_a \subseteq X_b$; for Milnor, $\iota = \mathbb R$ with $X_a = M^a$, or $\iota = \mathbb N$ with $X_k = M^{a_k}$. A function on pairs is a function $S$ of two indices with integer values, of which only the values at $a \le b$ are ever used, and $S(a, b)$ stands for Milnor's $S(X_b, X_a)$: Milnor writes the bigger space first, the formal text lists the indices in increasing order.

**Definition (p. 28; V3.7).** $S$ is *subadditive* if $S(a, c) \le S(a, b) + S(b, c)$ whenever $a \le b \le c$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · IsSubadditive⟧, and *additive* if equality always holds ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · IsAdditive⟧. An additive function is subadditive ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · IsAdditive.isSubadditive⟧. In Milnor's notation, $S(X, Z) \le S(X, Y) + S(Y, Z)$ for $X \supset Y \supset Z$.

Milnor does not say on which pairs $S$ is defined. Here $S$ is defined on all pairs, and the question which pairs have finite-dimensional homology is moved into the homology structure of §IV.3, where it is settled once.

**Lemma 5.1 (V3.8).** Let $S$ be subadditive and $X_0 \le X_1 \le \dots \le X_n$ with $n \ge 1$. Then
$$S(X_0, X_n) \le \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} S(X_k, X_{k+1}),$$
with equality if $S$ is additive ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · IsSubadditive.le_sum⟧ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · IsAdditive.eq_sum⟧.

*The proof as formalized* is Milnor's induction, with the hypothesis it actually uses isolated: subadditivity on the triples $X_0 \le X_m \le X_{m+1}$, $0 < m < n$, suffices ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · le_sum_of_chain⟧, and in the equality case additivity on those triples ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · eq_sum_of_chain⟧. For $n = 1$ both sides are $S(X_0, X_1)$. From $n$ to $n + 1$,
$$S(X_0, X_{n+1}) \le S(X_0, X_n) + S(X_n, X_{n+1}) \le \sum_{k<n} S(X_k, X_{k+1}) + S(X_n, X_{n+1}).$$
That these are triples, $X_0 \le X_m$, follows from the chain by induction ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · chain_le⟧. $\square$

The hypothesis $n \ge 1$ is not decoration. For the empty chain the statement would read $S(X_0, X_0) \le 0$, which subadditivity does not give: it gives only $S(X, X) \le 2S(X, X)$, that is, $S(X, X) \ge 0$. Milnor's induction starts at $n = 1$. With $X_0 = \emptyset$, Lemma 5.1 is Milnor's inequality (1).

### IV.3 The homology of a filtration: Lemma 5.3, Theorem 5.2 and the inequalities $(4_\lambda)$

**Definition (V3.9).** A *homology of a filtration* over a field $\Bbbk$, indexed by a preorder $\iota$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · FiltrationHomology⟧, is a family of $\Bbbk$-vector spaces $H(i, a, b)$, $i \in \mathbb N$, $a, b \in \iota$, to be read as $H_i(X_b, X_a; \Bbbk)$, such that

- $H(i, a, b)$ is finite-dimensional whenever $a \le b$, and
- for every $a \le b \le c$ there is a long exact sequence (§IV.1) with $A_i = H(i, a, b)$, $B_i = H(i, a, c)$ and $C_i = H(i, b, c)$: the sequence of the triple $X_c \supseteq X_b \supseteq X_a$.

Nothing else is assumed: no functoriality, no compatibility between the sequences of different triples, no vanishing of $H(i, a, a)$, no excision. Values with $a \not\le b$ are never used. The structure records exactly what §5 uses, and the topology enters only through two hypotheses kept apart: the sequences of triples, an axiom here, and the homology of the slabs, a hypothesis of the final theorems.

For a chain $X_0 \le X_1 \le \dots \le X_n$ in $\iota$ (a sequence $X$ indexed by $\mathbb N$ with $X_k \le X_{k+1}$ for $k < n$), the *Morse numbers* are
$$C_i = \sum_{k<n} \dim H(i, X_k, X_{k+1})$$
⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · morseNumber⟧, the total dimension of the homology of the slabs in degree $i$. When the slabs have the homology of attached cells, $C_i$ is Milnor's number of critical points of index $i$ (below).

Fix a homology of a filtration. Three functions on pairs are subadditive or additive (V3.10, V3.12).

- $R_\lambda(a, b) = \dim H(\lambda, a, b)$ is subadditive ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · FiltrationHomology.isSubadditive_finrank⟧, by the second lemma of §IV.1 for the sequence of $a \le b \le c$, all three of whose families are finite-dimensional by the first axiom.
- **Lemma 5.3.** $S_\lambda(a, b) = \operatorname{alt}_\lambda\big(\dim H(\bullet, a, b)\big) = R_\lambda - R_{\lambda-1} + \dots \pm R_0$ is subadditive ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · FiltrationHomology.isSubadditive_altSum⟧, by the corollary of the key identity.
- *The Euler characteristic is additive* (p. 28). Truncate it at $N$: $\chi_N(a, b) = \sum_{i \le N} (-1)^i \dim H(i, a, b)$. If $H(N + 1, a, b) = 0$ for all $a \le b$, then $\chi_N$ is additive ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · FiltrationHomology.isAdditive_eulerChar⟧: by $\chi_N = (-1)^N \operatorname{alt}_N$ the claim is the additivity of $\operatorname{alt}_N$ on a triple whose third family vanishes in degree $N + 1$. If the homology vanishes in all degrees above $N$, $\chi_N$ is Milnor's $\chi$.

Lemma 5.1 then gives the statements of §5 for an arbitrary chain $X_0 \le \dots \le X_n$ with $n \ge 1$, writing $b_i = \dim H(i, X_0, X_n)$ for its Betti numbers and $C_i$ for its Morse numbers.

- **Theorem 5.2 (2), for any filtration (V3.11):** $b_\lambda \le C_\lambda$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · FiltrationHomology.finrank_le_morseNumber⟧, Lemma 5.1 for $R_\lambda$.
- **The inequalities $(4_\lambda)$, for any filtration (V3.13):** $\operatorname{alt}_\lambda(b) \le \operatorname{alt}_\lambda(C)$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · FiltrationHomology.altSum_le_morseNumber⟧, Lemma 5.1 for $S_\lambda$, after which $\operatorname{alt}_\lambda$ is moved inside the sum over the slabs.
- **Theorem 5.2 (3), for any filtration (V3.11):** if no slab has homology in degree $N + 1$, then $\sum_{i \le N} (-1)^i b_i = \sum_{i \le N} (-1)^i C_i$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · FiltrationHomology.sum_neg_one_pow_finrank_eq⟧. The proof is the equality case of Milnor's induction for $\chi_N$ on the triples $X_0 \le X_m \le X_{m+1}$. On each of them additivity needs only $H(N + 1, X_m, X_{m+1}) = 0$, a slab, which is why the hypothesis concerns slabs and not the pairs $(X_m, X_0)$. An exchange of two finite sums then rewrites $\sum_k \chi_N(X_k, X_{k+1})$ as $\sum_{i \le N} (-1)^i C_i$.

**Finiteness along a chain.** Let $\iota = \mathbb N$, and suppose only that the sequences of triples exist and that every slab $H(i, k, k + 1)$ and every $H(i, k, k)$ is finite-dimensional. Then every $H(i, a, b)$ with $a \le b$ is finite-dimensional ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · finiteDimensional_of_steps⟧: by induction on $b \ge a$, starting from $H(i, a, a)$ and passing from $b$ to $b + 1$ through the sequence of $a \le b \le b + 1$, whose middle is finite-dimensional when its ends are (§IV.1). This gives a constructor ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · FiltrationHomology.ofSteps⟧ that produces a homology of a filtration from the sequences of triples and the finiteness of the slabs alone. In singular homology $H_*(X_k, X_k) = 0$, so the finiteness of $H_*(M; \Bbbk)$ is, as in Milnor, a consequence of the computation, not an assumption.

**Critical points.** The topological input of p. 29 enters as a hypothesis about a finite set $P$ (the critical points) carrying two functions, an index $\operatorname{ind}(p)$ and a step $\operatorname{step}(p) < n$ (the $k$ such that $p$ lies in the slab $a_k < f(p) < a_{k+1}$):
$$\dim H(i, X_k, X_{k+1}) = \#\{p \in P : \operatorname{step}(p) = k,\ \operatorname{ind}(p) = i\} \qquad (k < n,\ i \in \mathbb N). \tag{cells}$$
In words, step $k$ attaches one $i$-cell for each critical point of index $i$ in the slab. Under (cells) the Morse numbers count critical points, $C_i = \#\{p \in P : \operatorname{ind}(p) = i\}$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · morseNumber_eq_card⟧: the sizes of the fibres of $\operatorname{step}$ over $k < n$, within the points of index $i$, add up to the size of that set, because $\operatorname{step}$ maps it into $\{0, \dots, n - 1\}$. In Milnor's case of one critical point per slab, of index $\operatorname{idx}(k)$, (cells) reads $\dim H(i, X_k, X_{k+1}) = [\operatorname{idx}(k) = i]$, and $C_i = \#\{k < n : \operatorname{idx}(k) = i\}$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · morseNumber_eq_card_of_single⟧.

Substituting, for every homology of a filtration and every chain satisfying (cells):

- **Theorem 5.2 (2):** $b_\lambda \le \#\{p \in P : \operatorname{ind}(p) = \lambda\}$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · FiltrationHomology.weak_morse_inequality⟧;
- **$(4_\lambda)$**, with $C_i$ the number of points of index $i$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · FiltrationHomology.morse_inequality⟧;
- if no point of $P$ has index $l$, then $b_l = 0$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · FiltrationHomology.finrank_eq_zero_of_forall_ne⟧, by the weak inequality;
- **Theorem 5.2 (3):** if every index is at most $N$, then $\sum_{i \le N} (-1)^i b_i = \sum_{p \in P} (-1)^{\operatorname{ind}(p)}$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · FiltrationHomology.euler_characteristic_eq⟧. No slab has homology in degree $N + 1$, its dimension being by (cells) a number of points of index $N + 1$; so Theorem 5.2 (3) above applies, and its right side, regrouped by the value of $\operatorname{ind}$, is $\sum_{p \in P} (-1)^{\operatorname{ind}(p)}$. By the previous item, the left side is the whole Euler characteristic of $(X_n, X_0)$.

*Where the formal statements depart from Milnor.* Milnor chooses $a_1 < \dots < a_k$ so that $M^{a_i}$ contains exactly $i$ critical points (p. 29), which requires distinct critical points to have distinct critical values; that is not assumed, and (cells) allows any number of critical points per slab, the form in which the computation of a slab holds in general (§IV.19). Milnor's chain starts at $M^{a_0} = \emptyset$ although only $a_1, \dots, a_k$ were introduced; here the bottom $X_0$ is arbitrary, and $b_i$ is the homology of the pair $(X_n, X_0)$. The last term $\pm C_n$ of his formula for $\chi(M)$ silently takes $n = \dim M$; here the bound $N$ on the indices is a hypothesis.

### IV.4 Corollary 5.4 and the lacunary principle

The consequences on pp. 30–31 concern only two integer sequences $b$ and $c$ that satisfy $(4_l)$ for every $l$, $\operatorname{alt}_l(b) \le \operatorname{alt}_l(c)$, and they are proved in that generality (V3.14, V3.15). For a filtration, $b_i = \dim H(i, X_0, X_n) \ge 0$ and $c_i = C_i$.

- *The strong inequalities imply the weak ones* (p. 30): $b_l \le c_l$ for every $l$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · le_of_altSum_le⟧. For $l = 0$, $(4_0)$ is $b_0 \le c_0$. For $l + 1$, add $(4_{l+1})$ and $(4_l)$ and use $\operatorname{alt}_{l+1} + \operatorname{alt}_l = (\text{term of index } l + 1)$.
- *An equality* (p. 30): if $c_{l+1} = 0$ and $b_{l+1} \ge 0$, then $(4_l)$ is an equality, $\operatorname{alt}_l(b) = \operatorname{alt}_l(c)$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · altSum_eq_of_altSum_le⟧. Indeed $(4_{l+1})$ reads $b_{l+1} - \operatorname{alt}_l(b) \le 0 - \operatorname{alt}_l(c)$, so $\operatorname{alt}_l(c) \le \operatorname{alt}_l(b) - b_{l+1} \le \operatorname{alt}_l(b)$, and $(4_l)$ is the reverse inequality. Milnor first deduces $R_{\lambda+1} = 0$; only $R_{\lambda+1} \ge 0$ is needed.
- *Formula (3) from the strong inequalities* (p. 30): if $b \ge 0$ and $c_{N+1} = 0$, then $\sum_{i \le N} (-1)^i b_i = \sum_{i \le N} (-1)^i c_i$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · sum_neg_one_pow_eq_of_altSum_le⟧, by the previous item and $\chi_N = (-1)^N \operatorname{alt}_N$. Milnor compares $(4_\lambda)$ and $(4_{\lambda-1})$ for all $\lambda > n$; only $\lambda = n + 1$ matters.

**Corollary 5.4 (p. 31), for $\lambda = l + 1 \ge 1$.** If $b \ge 0$ and $c_{l+2} = c_l = 0$, then $b_{l+1} = c_{l+1}$ and $b_{l+2} = b_l = 0$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · eq_of_altSum_le_succ⟧.

*Proof.* $b_{l+2} = b_l = 0$, because $0 \le b \le c$ there. $(4_{l+1})$ is an equality by the second item, since $c_{l+2} = 0$. $(4_l)$ is an equality too: for $l = 0$ both sides are $b_0 = c_0 = 0$; for $l = m + 1$, $\operatorname{alt}_l = (\text{term of index } l) - \operatorname{alt}_m$, the terms of index $l$ vanish on both sides, and $(4_m)$ is an equality by the second item, since $c_{m+1} = c_l = 0$. Subtracting, $b_{l+1} = \operatorname{alt}_{l+1}(b) + \operatorname{alt}_l(b) = \operatorname{alt}_{l+1}(c) + \operatorname{alt}_l(c) = c_{l+1}$. $\square$

**Corollary 5.4 for $\lambda = 0$**, where $C_{\lambda-1}$ is meaningless and is read as $0$: if $b \ge 0$ and $c_1 = 0$, then $b_0 = c_0$ and $b_1 = 0$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · eq_of_altSum_le_zero⟧.

**The lacunary principle** (the remark on $\mathbb CP^n$, p. 31). If $b \ge 0$ and no two consecutive $c_i$ are nonzero ($c_i = 0$ or $c_{i+1} = 0$ for every $i$), then $b_i = c_i$ for every $i$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · eq_of_altSum_le_of_lacunary⟧. If $c_i = 0$, then $0 \le b_i \le c_i = 0$. Otherwise $c_{i+1} = 0$, and for $i \ge 1$ also $c_{i-1} = 0$, since $c_{i-1}$ or $c_i$ vanishes; Corollary 5.4 applies at $i$. Sequences vanishing in all odd degrees, such as the Morse numbers of $\mathbb CP^n$, satisfy the hypothesis ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · lacunary_of_odd⟧.

For a homology of a filtration and a chain, $b_i = \dim H(i, X_0, X_n)$ is non-negative and the inequalities $(4_l)$ hold for every $l$ (§IV.3), so these statements apply verbatim: Corollary 5.4 ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · FiltrationHomology.finrank_eq_morseNumber_of_succ⟧ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · FiltrationHomology.finrank_eq_morseNumber_of_zero⟧ and the lacunary principle ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · FiltrationHomology.finrank_eq_morseNumber_of_lacunary⟧. Milnor remarks that Corollary 5.4 computes the homology of $\mathbb CP^n$ without Theorem 3.5. What it gives, here as in the book, is the Betti numbers over every field, not the integral groups. $\mathbb CP^n$ is not constructed in the project; the spheres, for which the lacunary principle gives the whole homology, appear in §IV.21.

### IV.5 Relative singular homology and the exact sequence of a pair

*What Mathlib provides.* For an object $R$ of a preadditive category with coproducts, Mathlib's singular chain complex with coefficients $R$ is a functor from topological spaces to chain complexes indexed by $\mathbb N$ ⟦Mathlib/AlgebraicTopology/SingularHomology/Basic · singularChainComplexFunctor⟧, and singular homology is its homology ⟦Mathlib/AlgebraicTopology/SingularHomology/Basic · singularHomologyFunctor⟧. A *pair* is an embedding $A \hookrightarrow X$ of topological spaces, viewed as an arrow; a map of pairs is a commutative square ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Category/TopPair · TopPair⟧. In an abelian category, a short exact sequence of chain complexes has a connecting morphism ⟦Mathlib/Algebra/Homology/HomologySequence · ShortExact.δ⟧ and a long exact homology sequence, exact at each of its three kinds of places ⟦Mathlib/Algebra/Homology/HomologySequence · ShortExact.homology_exact₁⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Algebra/Homology/HomologySequence · ShortExact.homology_exact₂⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Algebra/Homology/HomologySequence · ShortExact.homology_exact₃⟧. In this section the coefficients are an object $R$ of an abelian category with coproducts; the case in view is the category of modules over a commutative ring, with $R$ the ring itself.

**The cokernel sequence.** In any abelian category, sending an arrow $f : A \to B$ to the short complex $A \xrightarrow{f} B \to \operatorname{coker} f$ is a functor from arrows to short complexes ⟦Homology/Relative · cokernelSequence⟧. When $f$ is a monomorphism the short complex is short exact ⟦Homology/Relative · cokernelSequence_shortExact⟧: it is exact at $B$, being a map followed by its cokernel; $f$ is mono by hypothesis; and the projection onto a cokernel is epi.

**Definition (relative chains and relative homology; V3.26).** Write $C_\bullet(X)$ for the singular chain complex with coefficients $R$ ⟦Homology/Relative · singularChains⟧. For a pair $(X, A)$ the inclusion $A \hookrightarrow X$ is injective, hence a monomorphism of spaces, and $C_\bullet(A) \to C_\bullet(X)$ is a monomorphism of complexes, because Mathlib's singular chain functor preserves monomorphisms: a singular simplex of $A$ is determined by its image in $X$. The *relative short complex* is the composite functor from pairs to arrows of spaces, to arrows of complexes, to short complexes ⟦Homology/Relative · relShortComplex⟧:
$$0 \to C_\bullet(A) \to C_\bullet(X) \to C_\bullet(X, A) \to 0,$$
short exact for every pair ⟦Homology/Relative · relShortComplex_shortExact⟧. The *relative chain complex* $C_\bullet(X, A)$ is its third term ⟦Homology/Relative · relChains⟧, by definition the cokernel of $C_\bullet(A) \to C_\bullet(X)$ ⟦Homology/Relative · relChains_obj⟧, and the *relative homology* $H_n(X, A; R)$ is its $n$-th homology ⟦Homology/Relative · relHomology⟧ ⟦Homology/Relative · relHomology_obj⟧. Both are functors on pairs; a map of pairs acts on relative homology through the homology of the induced map of relative chains ⟦Homology/Relative · relHomology_map⟧.

Three facts make the cokernel description usable. The projection $\pi : C_\bullet(X) \to C_\bullet(X, A)$ ⟦Homology/Relative · relπ⟧ is an epimorphism that kills $C_\bullet(A)$ ⟦Homology/Relative · map_relπ⟧. The map of relative chains induced by $\varphi : (X, A) \to (Y, B)$ is characterized by $\varphi_\sharp \circ \pi_{(X, A)} = \pi_{(Y, B)} \circ (\varphi_X)_\sharp$, where $\varphi_X : X \to Y$ is the map of the big spaces ⟦Homology/Relative · relπ_relChains_map⟧. And a map out of $C_\bullet(X, A)$ is determined by its composite with $\pi$ ⟦Homology/Relative · relChains_hom_ext⟧. Most identities between maps of relative chains below are proved by this last fact: compose with $\pi$ and compute in $C_\bullet(X)$.

**Theorem (the exact sequence of a pair; V3.26).** For every pair $(X, A)$ the sequence
$$\cdots \to H_{n+1}(X, A) \xrightarrow{\ \partial\ } H_n(A) \to H_n(X) \to H_n(X, A) \xrightarrow{\ \partial\ } H_{n-1}(A) \to \cdots \to H_0(X) \to H_0(X, A) \to 0$$
is exact at every place, and $\partial$ is natural in maps of pairs.

*The proof as formalized.* The boundary $\partial : H_{n+1}(X, A) \to H_n(A)$ is Mathlib's connecting morphism of the short exact sequence of the pair ⟦Homology/Relative · relδ⟧. Consecutive composites vanish ⟦Homology/Relative · relδ_comp⟧ ⟦Homology/Relative · comp_relδ⟧ ⟦Homology/Relative · homologyMap_map_comp_relπ⟧, and exactness at $H_n(A)$, at $H_n(X)$ and at $H_{n+1}(X, A)$ is Mathlib's exactness of the homology sequence, specialized ⟦Homology/Relative · relHomology_exact₁⟧ ⟦Homology/Relative · relHomology_exact₂⟧ ⟦Homology/Relative · relHomology_exact₃⟧; a six-term form around degree $n$ is recorded for diagram lemmas ⟦Homology/Relative · relHomologySequence_exact⟧. The end of the sequence, the surjectivity of $H_0(X) \to H_0(X, A)$ ⟦Homology/Relative · epi_homologyMap_relπ_zero⟧, is not part of Mathlib's sequence, which stops at the last connecting morphism. With the homological grading no differential leaves degree $0$, so $H_0$ is a quotient of the $0$-chains, and an epimorphism of $0$-chains induces an epimorphism of $H_0$ ⟦Mathlib/Algebra/Homology/ShortComplex/HomologicalComplex · epi_homologyMap_of_epi_of_not_rel⟧. Naturality, $\partial \circ \varphi_* = (\varphi|_A)_* \circ \partial$, is Mathlib's naturality of connecting morphisms for morphisms of short exact sequences ⟦Mathlib/Algebra/Homology/HomologySequenceLemmas · HomologySequence.δ_naturality⟧, applied to the morphism of relative short complexes induced by $\varphi$ ⟦Homology/Relative · relδ_naturality⟧. It is packaged as a natural transformation of functors on pairs ⟦Homology/Relative · relδNatTrans⟧ ⟦Homology/Relative · relδNatTrans_app_succ⟧; the projections $H_n(X) \to H_n(X, A)$ form one as well ⟦Homology/Relative · homologyToRelHomology⟧. $\square$

Three consequences of exactness are recorded. If $H_n(A) \to H_n(X)$ is onto and $H_{n-1}(A) \to H_{n-1}(X)$ is injective, then $H_n(X, A) = 0$ ⟦Homology/Relative · isZero_relHomology_of_epi_of_mono⟧; in particular, if $A \hookrightarrow X$ induces isomorphisms in all degrees (for instance if it is a homotopy equivalence), then $H_*(X, A) = 0$ ⟦Homology/Relative · isZero_relHomology_of_isIso_homologyMap⟧. If $H_{n+1}(X) = H_n(X) = 0$, then $\partial : H_{n+1}(X, A) \to H_n(A)$ is an isomorphism ⟦Homology/Relative · isIso_relδ⟧.

**The pair $(X, \emptyset)$.** The chains of an empty space vanish in every degree ⟦Homology/Relative · isZero_singularChains_X_of_isEmpty⟧: a generator of $C_n(X)$ is a singular simplex $\Delta^n \to X$, and there is none, $\Delta^n$ being nonempty. So the whole complex is zero ⟦Homology/Relative · isZero_singularChains_of_isEmpty⟧. For $A = \emptyset$ the projection $\pi$ is then the cokernel of a zero map, an isomorphism ⟦Homology/Relative · isIso_relπ_of_isEmpty⟧, and so is its effect on homology ⟦Homology/Relative · isIso_homologyMap_relπ_of_isEmpty⟧. This is the natural isomorphism $H_n(X) \cong H_n(X, \emptyset)$ ⟦Homology/Relative · relHomologyInclIso⟧, natural because the projections are.

**The pair $(X, X)$.** If $A \hookrightarrow X$ is an isomorphism, then so is $C_\bullet(A) \to C_\bullet(X)$; in particular it is an epimorphism, and its cokernel is zero ⟦Homology/Relative · isZero_relChains_of_isIso⟧. So $H_n(X, A) = 0$ ⟦Homology/Relative · isZero_relHomology_of_isIso⟧; in particular $H_n(X, X) = 0$ ⟦Homology/Relative · isZero_relHomology_diag⟧.

**The five lemma for pairs.** If a map of pairs $\varphi : (X, A) \to (Y, B)$ induces quasi-isomorphisms $C_\bullet(A) \to C_\bullet(B)$ and $C_\bullet(X) \to C_\bullet(Y)$, it induces a quasi-isomorphism of relative chains ⟦Homology/Relative · quasiIso_relChains_map⟧, by Mathlib's five lemma for morphisms of short exact sequences of complexes ⟦Mathlib/Algebra/Homology/HomologySequenceLemmas · HomologySequence.quasiIso_τ₃⟧, and therefore isomorphisms in relative homology ⟦Homology/Relative · isIso_relHomology_map_of_quasiIso⟧. Stated with homology: if $\varphi$ induces isomorphisms $H_*(A) \cong H_*(B)$ and $H_*(X) \cong H_*(Y)$ in all degrees, then $H_n(X, A) \cong H_n(Y, B)$ for every $n$ ⟦Homology/Relative · isIso_relHomology_map⟧. A version in one degree asks for an epimorphism on $H_n(A)$, isomorphisms on $H_n(X)$ and $H_{n-1}(A)$, and a monomorphism on $H_{n-1}(X)$ ⟦Homology/Relative · isIso_relHomology_map_of_degree⟧.

**Pairs of subsets.** Milnor's pairs are pairs of subsets, $(M^b, M^a)$. For subsets $A \subseteq B$ of a space $E$, the inclusion of subspaces ⟦Homology/Relative · subsetInclusion⟧ is an embedding, and $(B, A)$ is a pair ⟦Homology/Relative · subsetPair⟧. The inclusions $(B, A) \to (B', A')$ for $A \subseteq A'$, $B \subseteq B'$ are maps of pairs ⟦Homology/Relative · subsetPairHom⟧, and so are the restrictions of a continuous map $f$ with $f(A) \subseteq A'$, $f(B) \subseteq B'$ ⟦Homology/Relative · subsetMap⟧ ⟦Homology/Relative · subsetPairMap⟧. Equal subsets give isomorphic pairs ⟦Homology/Relative · subsetPairIsoOfEq⟧, which is needed because two pairs whose subsets are equal only by a proof are not literally the same pair.

### IV.6 The exact sequence of a triple

Milnor uses the sequence of a triple $X \supset Y \supset Z$ (p. 30). It comes from a short exact sequence of relative chain complexes, the third isomorphism theorem $\big(C(X)/C(Z)\big) \big/ \big(C(Y)/C(Z)\big) \cong C(X)/C(Y)$.

**Lemma (the third isomorphism theorem).** In an abelian category, let $f : A \to B$, let $g : B \to C$ be a monomorphism, and $h = g \circ f$. Then $0 \to \operatorname{coker} f \to \operatorname{coker} h \to \operatorname{coker} g \to 0$, with the maps induced by $g$ and by the identity of $C$, is short exact ⟦Homology/Triple · cokernel_comp_shortExact'⟧ ⟦Homology/Triple · cokernel_comp_shortExact⟧.

*Proof.* Mathlib has the exact sequence of a composition ⟦Mathlib/CategoryTheory/Abelian/DiagramLemmas/KernelCokernelComp · kernelCokernelCompSequence_exact⟧,
$$0 \to \ker f \to \ker h \to \ker g \to \operatorname{coker} f \to \operatorname{coker} h \to \operatorname{coker} g \to 0 .$$
Exactness at $\operatorname{coker} h$ is part of it. The map $\operatorname{coker} f \to \operatorname{coker} h$ is mono by exactness at $\operatorname{coker} f$, the map into $\operatorname{coker} f$ coming from $\ker g = 0$. The map $\operatorname{coker} h \to \operatorname{coker} g$ is epi because its composite with the epimorphism $C \to \operatorname{coker} h$ is the epimorphism $C \to \operatorname{coker} g$. $\square$

**Definition (triples; V3.27).** A *triple of spaces* $Z \subseteq Y \subseteq X$ ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple⟧ consists of embeddings $i : Z \to Y$ and $j : Y \to X$ and a map $k : Z \to X$ with $k = j \circ i$, which is then an embedding too ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.isEmbedding_k⟧. The redundant field $k$ allows the pair $(X, Z)$ to be literally the pair one wants. The three pairs ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.pairYZ⟧ ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.pairXZ⟧ ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.pairXY⟧ are joined by the inclusions $(Y, Z) \to (X, Z)$ ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.map₁⟧ and $(X, Z) \to (X, Y)$ ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.map₂⟧. For subsets $A \subseteq B \subseteq D$ of a space there is a triple ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.ofSubsets⟧ whose three pairs are exactly the pairs of subsets $(B, A)$, $(D, A)$, $(D, B)$ of §IV.5 ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.ofSubsets_pairYZ⟧ ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.ofSubsets_pairXZ⟧ ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.ofSubsets_pairXY⟧.

**Theorem (the exact sequence of a triple; V3.27).** For every triple the sequence
$$\cdots \to H_{n+1}(X, Y) \xrightarrow{\ \partial\ } H_n(Y, Z) \to H_n(X, Z) \to H_n(X, Y) \xrightarrow{\ \partial\ } H_{n-1}(Y, Z) \to \cdots \to H_0(X, Z) \to H_0(X, Y) \to 0$$
is exact at every place, and its $\partial$ is the boundary of the pair $(X, Y)$ followed by $H_n(Y) \to H_n(Y, Z)$.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. The two inclusions of pairs give a short complex $C_\bullet(Y, Z) \to C_\bullet(X, Z) \to C_\bullet(X, Y)$ ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.shortComplex⟧ whose composite vanishes ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.relChains_map_map₁_comp_map₂⟧: composed with the projection from $C_\bullet(Y)$, it is $C_\bullet(Y) \to C_\bullet(X) \to C_\bullet(X, Y)$, which is zero (§IV.5).
2. The two maps are the cokernel maps of the lemma: $C_\bullet(Y, Z) \to C_\bullet(X, Z)$ is induced on cokernels by $C_\bullet(Y) \to C_\bullet(X)$ ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.relChains_map_map₁_eq⟧, and $C_\bullet(X, Z) \to C_\bullet(X, Y)$ by the identity of $C_\bullet(X)$ ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.relChains_map_map₂_eq⟧; both identities are checked after the projections.
3. The lemma, with $f = C_\bullet(Z) \to C_\bullet(Y)$, the monomorphism $g = C_\bullet(Y) \to C_\bullet(X)$, and $g \circ f = C_\bullet(Z) \to C_\bullet(X)$ ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.singularChains_map_fac⟧, makes the short complex short exact ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.shortComplex_shortExact⟧.
4. The boundary of the triple is the connecting morphism ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.δ⟧. The composites vanish ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.δ_comp⟧ ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.comp_δ⟧ ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.map₁_comp_map₂⟧, and exactness at the three kinds of places is Mathlib's ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.exact₁⟧ ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.exact₂⟧ ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.exact₃⟧. The end, the surjectivity of $H_0(X, Z) \to H_0(X, Y)$, is the degree-$0$ argument of §IV.5 for the epimorphism $C_\bullet(X, Z) \to C_\bullet(X, Y)$ ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.epi_map₂_zero⟧.
5. The projections $C_\bullet(Y) \to C_\bullet(Y, Z)$ and $C_\bullet(X) \to C_\bullet(X, Z)$, with the identity of $C_\bullet(X, Y)$, form a morphism from the short exact sequence of the pair $(X, Y)$ to that of the triple ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.fromPair⟧. Naturality of connecting morphisms, with the identity on the third terms, gives $\partial_{\mathrm{triple}} = \pi_* \circ \partial_{(X, Y)}$ ⟦Homology/Triple · TopTriple.δ_eq⟧. $\square$

For $Z = \emptyset$ the sequence of the triple is that of the pair $(X, Y)$, by $H_*(X, \emptyset) = H_*(X)$.

### IV.7 Homotopy invariance for pairs, strong deformation retracts, and the Eilenberg–Steenrod pretheory

Mathlib proves that homotopic maps induce chain-homotopic maps of singular chain complexes ⟦Mathlib/AlgebraicTopology/SingularHomology/HomotopyInvariance · singularChainComplexFunctorObjMap⟧. Everything in this section is deduced from that one theorem.

**Homotopy equivalences.** Maps $f : X \to Y$ and $g : Y \to X$ with homotopies $g \circ f \simeq \mathrm{id}$ and $f \circ g \simeq \mathrm{id}$ induce a homotopy equivalence of singular chain complexes ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · singularChainsHomotopyEquivOfHomotopy⟧: by functoriality $(g \circ f)_\sharp = g_\sharp \circ f_\sharp$, Mathlib's theorem turns $g \circ f \simeq \mathrm{id}$ into a chain homotopy $(g \circ f)_\sharp \simeq \mathrm{id}_\sharp$, and $\mathrm{id}_\sharp = \mathrm{id}$. A homotopy equivalence of spaces (Mathlib's notion ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Homotopy/Equiv · HomotopyEquiv⟧) therefore gives one of chain complexes ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · singularChainsHomotopyEquiv⟧; a map that is a homotopy equivalence induces a quasi-isomorphism ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · quasiIso_map_of_homotopyEquiv⟧ and isomorphisms $H_n(X) \cong H_n(Y)$ ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · isIso_homologyMap_of_homotopyEquiv⟧; and a map of pairs both of whose components are homotopy equivalences induces isomorphisms in relative homology ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · isIso_relHomology_map_of_homotopyEquiv⟧, by the five lemma of §IV.5. A contractible space has the homology of a point, $H_n = 0$ for $n \ne 0$ ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · isZero_homology_of_contractibleSpace⟧: it is homotopy equivalent to a point ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Homotopy/Contractible · ContractibleSpace.hequiv⟧, and a point, being totally disconnected, has no homology in positive degrees ⟦Mathlib/AlgebraicTopology/SingularHomology/Basic · isZero_singularHomologyFunctor_of_totallyDisconnectedSpace⟧. Hence, for a pair $(X, A)$ with $X$ contractible, $\partial : H_{n+1}(X, A) \to H_n(A)$ is an isomorphism for $n \ne 0$ ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · isIso_relδ_of_contractibleSpace⟧, as for $(D^n, S^{n-1})$.

**Theorem (homotopy invariance for pairs; V3.26).** Maps of pairs $f, g : (X, A) \to (Y, B)$ that are homotopic through maps of pairs induce the same maps $H_n(X, A) \to H_n(Y, B)$ in every degree ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · relHomology_map_eq_of_homotopy⟧ ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · relHomology_map_eq_of_homotopic⟧. A homotopy of maps of pairs is Mathlib's: a homotopy of the maps of the big spaces and one of the maps of the small spaces, compatible with the inclusions ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Category/TopPair · TopPair.Homotopy⟧.

*The idea.* The textbook proof restricts the chain homotopy of $X$ to $A$ and passes to quotients. Mathlib's chain homotopy is built through several layers (singular simplicial sets, simplicial homotopies, the alternating face map complex), and its naturality in the space, which the restriction needs, is not available. The formal proof avoids it, using only the absolute theorem and the five lemma, applied to the cylinder of the pair.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. *The cylinder contracts onto its base.* For a space $Z$, $((z, s), t) \mapsto (z, ts)$ is a homotopy from $(z, s) \mapsto (z, 0)$ to the identity of $Z \times I$ ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · cylinderContraction⟧. So the projection $Z \times I \to Z$ is a homotopy equivalence, with inverse $z \mapsto (z, 0)$, and it induces isomorphisms in homology ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · isIso_homologyMap_fst⟧.
2. *The cylinder of a pair* is $(X \times I, A \times I)$ ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · cylinder⟧, a pair because a product of embeddings is an embedding. It has two ends $\iota_0, \iota_1 : (X, A) \to (X \times I, A \times I)$, $x \mapsto (x, 0)$ and $x \mapsto (x, 1)$ ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · cylinderι₀⟧ ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · cylinderι₁⟧, and a projection $\pi$ ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · cylinderπ⟧, with $\pi \circ \iota_0 = \mathrm{id} = \pi \circ \iota_1$ ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · cylinderι₀_π⟧ ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · cylinderι₁_π⟧. Both components of $\pi$ are projections of cylinders, so by step 1 and the five lemma $\pi$ induces isomorphisms in relative homology ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · isIso_relHomology_map_cylinderπ⟧.
3. *The two ends agree in homology.* From $\pi_* \circ (\iota_0)_* = \mathrm{id}$ and the invertibility of $\pi_*$, $(\iota_0)_* = \pi_*^{-1}$; likewise $(\iota_1)_* = \pi_*^{-1}$ ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · relHomology_map_cylinderι₀_eq_ι₁⟧.
4. *A homotopy is a map out of the cylinder.* A homotopy of pairs from $f$ to $g$ is a map of pairs $F : (X \times I, A \times I) \to (Y, B)$ ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · cylinderHom⟧ with $F \circ \iota_0 = f$ and $F \circ \iota_1 = g$ ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · cylinderι₀_cylinderHom⟧ ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · cylinderι₁_cylinderHom⟧; that $F$ is a map of pairs is the compatibility built into a homotopy of pairs.
5. Hence $f_* = F_* \circ (\iota_0)_* = F_* \circ (\iota_1)_* = g_*$. $\square$

**Strong deformation retracts.** Recall from §0.4 (and Part II, §II.1) that $A \subseteq X$ is a strong deformation retract if there are a continuous $r : X \to X$ with values in $A$ and a homotopy from the identity to $r$ fixing $A$ pointwise at all times ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsStrongDeformationRetract⟧; the inclusion is then a homotopy equivalence, with homotopy inverse $r$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · IsStrongDeformationRetract.exists_homotopyEquiv⟧. This is the form in which Theorem 3.1 and Remark 3.3 are proved (Part II, §§II.5, 2.14). For subsets $A \subseteq B \subseteq X$ of a space, with $B$ a strong deformation retract of $X$ (inside the subspace $X$), the following hold (V3.26):

- $H_n(B) \to H_n(X)$ is an isomorphism ⟦Homology/RelativeRetract · isIso_homologyMap_of_isStrongDeformationRetract⟧;
- $(B, A) \to (X, A)$ induces isomorphisms $H_n(B, A) \cong H_n(X, A)$ ⟦Homology/RelativeRetract · isIso_relHomology_map_of_isStrongDeformationRetract⟧ ⟦Homology/RelativeRetract · relHomologyIsoOfIsStrongDeformationRetract⟧, by the five lemma with the identity of $A$;
- $H_n(X, B) = 0$ ⟦Homology/RelativeRetract · isZero_relHomology_of_isStrongDeformationRetract⟧, by §IV.5.

The bottom of a pair can be moved as well: if $A \subseteq B \subseteq X$ and $A$ is a strong deformation retract of $B$, then $(X, A) \to (X, B)$ induces isomorphisms ⟦Homology/CellExcision · isIso_relHomology_map_of_isStrongDeformationRetract_snd⟧, by the five lemma with the identity of $X$ on top. Nothing requires the deformation of $B$ onto $A$ to extend to $X$. (Over a field the same fact is proved a second time in §IV.19, from the sequence of a triple.) Inclusions of pairs of subsets compose ⟦Homology/CellExcision · subsetPairHom_comp⟧, which is used to compare routes around squares of inclusions.

**The Eilenberg–Steenrod pretheory.** Mathlib formulates the data of an Eilenberg–Steenrod homology theory as a *homology pretheory* ⟦Mathlib/AlgebraicTopology/EilenbergSteenrod · HomologyPretheory⟧: relative homology functors on pairs, absolute homology functors on spaces, natural isomorphisms between absolute homology and the relative homology of $(X, \emptyset)$, and natural boundary maps from the relative homology of $(X, A)$ to the absolute homology of $A$, zero unless the degrees are adjacent. Homotopy invariance, the first axiom, is a separate class ⟦Mathlib/AlgebraicTopology/EilenbergSteenrod · IsHomotopyInvariant⟧. Mathlib has no instance. The project provides singular homology as one ⟦Homology/Pretheory · singularHomologyPretheory⟧: the relative homology of §IV.5 ⟦Homology/Pretheory · singularHomologyPretheory_Hₚ⟧, Mathlib's absolute homology, the isomorphism $H_n(X) \cong H_n(X, \emptyset)$, and as boundary in degrees $(n + 1, n)$ the connecting morphism of the pair ⟦Homology/Pretheory · singularHomologyPretheory_δ_app⟧, zero in all other degrees. It is homotopy invariant ⟦Homology/Pretheory · isHomotopyInvariant_singularHomologyPretheory⟧, by the theorem above. Exactness and excision are not part of Mathlib's pretheory; here they are the theorems of §§IV.5–IV.6 and §IV.13.

### IV.8 Singular chains as combinations of maps from the simplex

Mathlib's $C_\bullet(X; R)$ is the alternating face map complex of the simplicial object $n \mapsto \coprod_\sigma R$, a categorical coproduct indexed by the $n$-simplices of the singular simplicial set. This is convenient for functoriality, and inconvenient for barycentric subdivision, which needs a chain to be a finite formal combination $\sum r_i \sigma_i$ of continuous maps $\sigma_i : \Delta^n \to X$ and operates on the maps $\sigma_i$ themselves. The project sets up the textbook description and proves it isomorphic to Mathlib's, naturally in the space.

**Complexes of modules, degree by degree.** A chain map between complexes indexed by $\mathbb N$ is given by components commuting with the differentials ⟦Homology/ModuleComplex · natHomMk⟧, and a homotopy $f \simeq g$ by components $h_n : C_n \to D_{n+1}$ with $f_0 = d h_0 + g_0$ and $f_{n+1} = h_n d + d h_{n+1} + g_{n+1}$ ⟦Homology/ModuleComplex · natHomotopyMk⟧. For a complex $K$ of modules and submodules $L_n \subseteq K_n$ with $d(L_{n+1}) \subseteq L_n$ there are the subcomplex $L$ ⟦Homology/ModuleComplex · subcomplex⟧, with its injective inclusion ⟦Homology/ModuleComplex · subcomplexι⟧, and the quotient complex $K/L$, degreewise $K_n/L_n$ ⟦Homology/ModuleComplex · quotient⟧, with its surjective projection ⟦Homology/ModuleComplex · quotientπ⟧, which kills every chain map with values in $L$ ⟦Homology/ModuleComplex · quotientπ_comp_eq_zero⟧. If $f : K' \to K$ is injective in each degree with image $L$, then $0 \to K' \to K \to K/L \to 0$ is short exact ⟦Homology/ModuleComplex · shortExact_quotient⟧ and $K/L$ is a cokernel of $f$ ⟦Homology/ModuleComplex · isColimitQuotient⟧; this is the bridge between concrete quotients and the cokernels of §IV.5. Chain maps preserving the submodules restrict to the subcomplexes ⟦Homology/ModuleComplex · subcomplexMap⟧ and descend to the quotients ⟦Homology/ModuleComplex · quotientMap⟧, functorially ⟦Homology/ModuleComplex · quotientMap_id⟧ ⟦Homology/ModuleComplex · quotientMap_comp⟧; so do homotopies whose components preserve the submodules ⟦Homology/ModuleComplex · quotientHomotopy⟧.

**Free chains of a simplicial set.** Let $S \mapsto (S \to_0 R)$ be the free module on a set ⟦Homology/Simplex · freeFunctor⟧. The free chain complex of a simplicial set $K$ is the alternating face map complex of $K$ followed by this functor ⟦Homology/Simplex · freeChainsFunctor⟧ ⟦Homology/Simplex · freeChains⟧. Its $n$-chains are the finite combinations of $n$-simplices, and its differential is $\partial x = \sum_i (-1)^i d_i x$ on simplices ⟦Homology/Simplex · bd⟧ ⟦Homology/Simplex · freeChains_d⟧ ⟦Homology/Simplex · bd_single⟧. That $\partial\partial = 0$ comes for free from Mathlib's alternating face map complex ⟦Homology/Simplex · bd_bd⟧, and simplicial maps commute with $\partial$ ⟦Homology/Simplex · lmapDomain_bd⟧.

**The standard simplex.** $\Delta^n = \{t \in \mathbb R^{n+1} : t_i \ge 0,\ \sum_i t_i = 1\}$ ⟦Homology/Simplex · Simplex⟧ is Mathlib's standard simplex ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Convex/StdSimplex · stdSimplex⟧, a subset of the space of functions $\{0, \dots, n\} \to \mathbb R$, whose norm is the maximum norm; distances and diameters below are taken for that norm. A map of vertices $f$ defines the affine map $\Delta^m \to \Delta^n$ with $e_i \mapsto e_{f(i)}$ ⟦Homology/Simplex · simplexMap⟧, functorially ⟦Homology/Simplex · simplexMap_id⟧ ⟦Homology/Simplex · simplexMap_comp⟧. The face $\delta^i : \Delta^n \to \Delta^{n+1}$ is the affine map of the increasing injection that skips $i$ ⟦Homology/Simplex · faceMap⟧.

**Singular chains.** The singular simplicial set of $X$ is redefined so that its $n$-simplices are literally the continuous maps $\Delta^n \to X$, simplicial operators acting by precomposition with the affine maps ⟦Homology/Simplex · sing⟧; its faces are $\sigma \mapsto \sigma \circ \delta^i$ ⟦Homology/Simplex · sing_δ⟧, and it is a functor of $X$ ⟦Homology/Simplex · singFunctor⟧. The $n$-chains are $C_n(X) = C(\Delta^n, X) \to_0 R$ ⟦Homology/Simplex · Chains⟧, the boundary is $\partial\sigma = \sum_i (-1)^i \sigma \circ \delta^i$ ⟦Homology/Simplex · sbd⟧ ⟦Homology/Simplex · sbd_single⟧, with $\partial\partial = 0$ ⟦Homology/Simplex · sbd_sbd⟧, and the complex ⟦Homology/Simplex · singChains⟧ is defined directly, so that its modules of chains are the free modules themselves and elementwise reasoning needs no unfolding. A continuous map $f$ acts by $\sigma \mapsto f \circ \sigma$ ⟦Homology/Simplex · push⟧ ⟦Homology/Simplex · push_single⟧, functorially ⟦Homology/Simplex · push_id⟧ ⟦Homology/Simplex · push_comp⟧ and commuting with $\partial$ ⟦Homology/Simplex · push_sbd⟧; this gives a chain map $f_\sharp$ ⟦Homology/Simplex · pushHom⟧ and the singular chain functor ⟦Homology/Simplex · singChainsFunctor⟧. It is the free chain complex of the singular simplicial set, by the identity in each degree ⟦Homology/Simplex · freeChainsIso⟧.

**Theorem (comparison with Mathlib).** For a commutative ring $R$, Mathlib's singular chain functor with coefficients $R$ is naturally isomorphic to the concrete one ⟦Homology/Simplex · singChainsIso⟧, and on the summand indexed by a singular simplex the isomorphism sends the generator to that simplex, viewed as a map $\Delta^n \to X$ ⟦Homology/Simplex · ι_singChainsIso_hom_app_f⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Three natural isomorphisms are composed. First, the coproduct of copies of $R$ indexed by a set $S$ is the free module $S \to_0 R$ ⟦Homology/Simplex · sigmaIsoFinsupp⟧, by Mathlib's description of that coproduct ⟦Mathlib/Algebra/Category/ModuleCat/Colimits · finsuppCoconeIsColimit⟧, naturally in $S$ ⟦Homology/Simplex · sigmaConstIsoFree⟧ ⟦Homology/Simplex · ι_sigmaConstIsoFree_hom_app⟧; whiskering, Mathlib's chain complex functor on simplicial sets is isomorphic to the free one ⟦Homology/Simplex · chainComplexFunctorIso⟧. Second, Mathlib's singular simplicial set is isomorphic to the concrete one through Mathlib's own identification of its simplices with continuous maps, naturality holding by definition ⟦Homology/Simplex · toSSetIsoSing⟧. Third, the free chains of the concrete singular simplicial set are the concrete singular chains, as above. $\square$

The comparison is stated for spaces in the universe of the ring. This is why excision, and everything that uses it, is stated with the coefficients and the spaces in one universe.

### IV.9 Linear chains, the cone and barycentric subdivision

Hatcher's proof of excision (*Algebraic Topology*, Prop. 2.21) subdivides *linear* chains first. A linear simplex $[w_0, \dots, w_k]$ in a convex set is determined by its ordered tuple of vertices, and the operations of the subdivision are combinatorial operations on tuples together with one geometric input, the barycentre. The formalization separates the two. This section is the combinatorics; §IV.10 is the geometry.

**Linear chains.** For a set $V$ of vertices, the simplicial set of tuples has as $k$-simplices the tuples $w = (w_0, \dots, w_k)$ of elements of $V$, simplicial operators acting by precomposition ⟦Homology/LinearChains · tuples⟧; the $i$-th face deletes $w_i$ ⟦Homology/LinearChains · tuples_δ⟧. The linear $k$-chains are its free chains, finite combinations of $(k+1)$-tuples ⟦Homology/LinearChains · LinChain⟧, with boundary
$$\partial[w_0, \dots, w_{k+1}] = \sum_i (-1)^i [w_0, \dots, \widehat{w_i}, \dots, w_{k+1}]$$
⟦Homology/LinearChains · lbd⟧ ⟦Homology/LinearChains · lbd_single⟧; $\partial\partial = 0$ is inherited from §IV.8 ⟦Homology/LinearChains · lbd_lbd⟧. A map of vertex sets acts on linear chains, commuting with $\partial$ ⟦Homology/LinearChains · lmap⟧ ⟦Homology/LinearChains · lmap_lbd⟧.

**The cone.** For $b \in V$, $b \cdot [w_0, \dots, w_k] = [b, w_0, \dots, w_k]$ ⟦Homology/LinearChains · cone⟧ ⟦Homology/LinearChains · cone_single⟧. Let $\varepsilon$ be the augmentation of $0$-chains, the sum of the coefficients ⟦Homology/LinearChains · aug⟧, which vanishes on boundaries ⟦Homology/LinearChains · aug_lbd⟧. Then:

- in degree $0$, $\partial(b \cdot \alpha) = \alpha - \varepsilon(\alpha)[b]$ ⟦Homology/LinearChains · lbd_cone_zero⟧, since $\partial[b, w_0] = [w_0] - [b]$;
- in positive degrees, $\partial(b \cdot \alpha) = \alpha - b \cdot \partial\alpha$ ⟦Homology/LinearChains · lbd_cone_succ⟧. In $\partial[b, w_0, \dots, w_{k+1}]$, deleting $b$ gives $[w]$ with the sign $+$; deleting $w_j$ gives the cone over the $j$-th face of $w$ ⟦Homology/LinearChains · cons_comp_succAbove_succ⟧ with the sign $(-1)^{j+1}$, and these terms add up to $-b \cdot \partial[w]$.

Hatcher combines the two formulas through the augmented complex; the formalization keeps them apart and never needs degree $-1$. The cone is natural for vertex maps ⟦Homology/LinearChains · lmap_cone⟧.

**Definition (subdivision and the subdivision homotopy).** Let $\beta$ assign a vertex $\beta(w)$, a "barycentre", to every tuple. The subdivision operator $S$ and the homotopy $T$ are defined by recursion on the degree ⟦Homology/LinearChains · sd⟧ ⟦Homology/LinearChains · sdH⟧:
$$S = \mathrm{id} \text{ and } T = 0 \text{ in degree } 0, \qquad S[w] = \beta(w) \cdot S(\partial[w]), \quad T[w] = \beta(w) \cdot \big([w] - T(\partial[w])\big) \text{ in degree } k + 1$$
⟦Homology/LinearChains · sd_succ_single⟧ ⟦Homology/LinearChains · sdH_succ_single⟧. Hatcher's $T$ in degree $0$ is $[w_0] \mapsto [w_0, w_0]$; with $T = 0$ the identities below still hold, because $S = \mathrm{id}$ in degree $0$.

**Theorem.** $S$ is a chain map, $\partial S = S\partial$ ⟦Homology/LinearChains · lbd_sd⟧, and $\partial T + T\partial = 1 - S$ in every positive degree ⟦Homology/LinearChains · lbd_sdH⟧; in degree $0$ both sides vanish ⟦Homology/LinearChains · lbd_sdH_zero⟧. Both operators are natural for vertex maps $f$ compatible with the barycentres, $f(\beta(w)) = \beta'(f \circ w)$ ⟦Homology/LinearChains · lmap_sd⟧ ⟦Homology/LinearChains · lmap_sdH⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Everything is checked on generators $[w]$, by induction on the degree.

1. *$\partial S = S\partial$.* In degree $1$: $\partial S[w] = \partial(\beta \cdot \partial[w]) = \partial[w] - \varepsilon(\partial[w])[\beta] = \partial[w] = S\partial[w]$, because $\varepsilon$ kills boundaries and $S = \mathrm{id}$ on $0$-chains. In degree $k + 2$: $\partial(\beta \cdot S\partial[w]) = S\partial[w] - \beta \cdot \partial S\partial[w] = S\partial[w] - \beta \cdot S\partial\partial[w] = S\partial[w]$, by the induction hypothesis.
2. *Reduction to a key identity.* $\partial T + T\partial = 1 - S$ in degree $k + 1$ follows from the identity $\partial([w] - T\partial[w]) = S\partial[w]$ for the generators of that degree ⟦Homology/LinearChains · lbd_sdH_of_key⟧: granted it,
$$\partial T[w] = \big([w] - T\partial[w]\big) - \beta \cdot \partial\big([w] - T\partial[w]\big) = [w] - T\partial[w] - \beta \cdot S\partial[w] = [w] - T\partial[w] - S[w].$$
3. *The key identity*, by induction. For $1$-simplices, $T = 0$ on $0$-chains and $\partial[w] = S\partial[w]$. In higher degrees the identity $\partial T + T\partial = 1 - S$ one degree down, applied to $\partial[w]$ with $T\partial\partial[w] = 0$, reads $\partial T\partial[w] = \partial[w] - S\partial[w]$; hence $\partial([w] - T\partial[w]) = S\partial[w]$. $\square$

### IV.10 Affine simplices and the diameter bound

Let $s$ be a convex set in a real topological vector space $E$. A tuple $w$ of points of $s$ spans the *affine simplex* $[w] : \Delta^k \to s$, $t \mapsto \sum_i t_i w_i$ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · aff⟧, which lands in $s$ by convexity ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · sum_smul_mem⟧ and sends the vertex $e_i$ to $w_i$ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · aff_vertex⟧. $\Delta^n$ itself is convex ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · convex_simplex⟧, with vertices $e_0, \dots, e_n$ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · verts⟧. The calculus of affine simplices:

- they compose, $[u] \circ [v] = [[u] \circ v]$ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · aff_comp_aff⟧, an exchange of two finite sums;
- the identity of $\Delta^n$ is $[e_0, \dots, e_n]$ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · aff_verts⟧, and the affine map of a vertex map $f$ is $[e_{f(0)}, \dots, e_{f(m)}]$ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · simplexMap_eq_aff⟧, so precomposition reorders or repeats vertices, $[w] \circ \Delta(f) = [w \circ f]$ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · aff_comp_simplexMap⟧, where $\Delta(f)$ denotes the affine map of $f$;
- the barycentre $b(w) = (w_0 + \dots + w_k)/(k + 1)$ is the image of the barycentre of $\Delta^k$ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · bary⟧ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · coe_bary⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Convex/StdSimplex · stdSimplex.barycenter⟧, and affine simplices preserve barycentres ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · aff_bary⟧.

*Realization.* $w \mapsto [w]$ is a morphism of simplicial sets from the tuples of $s$ to the singular simplicial set of $s$ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · realize⟧, naturality being the reordering rule; on chains it is a chain map from linear to singular chains ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · lreal⟧ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · lreal_single⟧ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · sbd_lreal⟧. Pushing a realized linear chain of $\Delta^n$ forward along $[u]$ realizes the chain whose vertices are mapped by $[u]$ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · push_aff_lreal⟧. Barycentric subdivision of linear chains in $s$ is §IV.9 with $\beta = b$ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · bsd⟧ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · bsdH⟧; since affine simplices preserve barycentres, it is natural with respect to them ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · lmap_aff_bsd⟧ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · lmap_aff_bsdH⟧.

*Supports.* A chain is supported in a set $P$ of generators if all its terms lie in $P$. A linear map between free modules sends chains supported in $P$ to chains supported in $Q$ as soon as it does so on the generators in $P$ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · map_mem_supported⟧ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · mapDomain_mem_supported⟧. Statements of the form "every simplex of the chain $c$ has property $Q$" are formalized as "$c$ is supported in $Q$".

**The diameter bound** (Hatcher, step (1) of the proof of Prop. 2.21). Let now $E$ be normed, and write $\operatorname{vset}(w) = \{w_0, \dots, w_k\}$ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · vset⟧. The image of $[w]$ lies in the convex hull of $\operatorname{vset}(w)$ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · range_aff_subset⟧ and has the same diameter ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · diam_range_aff_le⟧ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · diam_range_aff⟧, because a convex hull has the diameter of the set ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Normed/Module/Convex · convexHull_diam⟧. The barycentre lies in the hull ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · bary_mem_convexHull⟧.

**Lemma (the barycentre is central).** For points $w_0, \dots, w_n$ and $x$ in their convex hull,
$$\operatorname{dist}(x, b(w)) \le \frac{n}{n+1} \operatorname{diam} \operatorname{vset}(w)$$
⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · dist_bary_le⟧.

*Proof.* Some vertex $w_j$ is at least as far from $b(w)$ as $x$ is ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Normed/Module/Convex · convexHull_exists_dist_ge⟧. And $w_j - b(w) = \frac{1}{n+1} \sum_i (w_j - w_i)$, a sum in which the term $i = j$ vanishes and each of the other $n$ terms has norm at most the diameter. $\square$

**Theorem (the diameter bound).** Let $\operatorname{Shrunk}(w, r)$ be the set of tuples $u$ with $\operatorname{vset}(u)$ in the convex hull of $\operatorname{vset}(w)$ and $\operatorname{diam}\operatorname{vset}(u) \le r$ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · Shrunk⟧, monotone in the hull and in $r$ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · Shrunk.mono⟧. For a linear $n$-simplex $[w]$ and every $m$, every simplex of $S^m[w]$ lies in $\operatorname{Shrunk}\big(w, (\tfrac{n}{n+1})^m \operatorname{diam}\operatorname{vset}(w)\big)$ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · bsd_pow_mem_supported⟧. For the images: every simplex $[u]$ of $S^m[w]$ has $\operatorname{diam}[u] \le (\tfrac{n}{n+1})^m \operatorname{diam}[w]$ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · diam_range_aff_le_of_mem_support⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* One subdivision first ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · bsd_mem_supported⟧, by induction on $n$; write $D = \operatorname{diam}\operatorname{vset}(w)$.

1. For $n = 0$, $S[w] = [w]$, a point, of diameter $0$.
2. For $n = k + 1$, $S[w] = b \cdot S(\partial[w])$ with $b = b(w)$. The simplices $u$ of $S(\partial[w])$ come from the faces of $w$, which are $k$-simplices; by induction each lies in the hull of a face, which lies in the hull of $w$ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · convexHull_vset_comp_subset⟧, and has diameter at most $\frac{k}{k+1}$ times that of the face, which is at most $D$ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · diam_vset_comp_le⟧.
3. The coned simplex $[b, u]$ has vertex set $\{b\} \cup \operatorname{vset}(u)$ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · vset_cons⟧, contained in the hull of $w$. Two vertices of $u$ are at distance at most $\frac{k}{k+1} D \le \frac{k+1}{k+2} D$; a vertex of $u$ is at distance at most $\frac{k+1}{k+2} D$ from $b$, by the lemma, since it lies in the hull of $w$. So $\operatorname{diam}\big(\{b\} \cup \operatorname{vset}(u)\big) \le \frac{n}{n+1} D$.

Iterating, by induction on $m$: every simplex $u$ of $S^m[w]$ lies in the hull of $w$ with diameter at most $(\frac{n}{n+1})^m D$; one more subdivision of $u$ multiplies the bound by $\frac{n}{n+1}$, and the hull of $u$ lies in that of $w$. $\square$

Two small facts complete the geometry. Every point of $[u]$ is within $\operatorname{diam}\operatorname{vset}(u)$ of the vertex $u_0$ ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · dist_aff_le⟧, and the vertices of $\Delta^n$ are at distance at most $1$ from each other in the maximum norm ⟦Homology/AffineSimplex · diam_vset_verts_le⟧.

### IV.11 Subdivision of singular chains and the homotopy $T$

**Natural operators from models.** A singular simplex $\sigma : \Delta^n \to X$ is the image of the identity simplex $\iota_n = \mathrm{id}_{\Delta^n}$ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · idSimplex⟧ under $\sigma_\sharp$ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · push_idSimplex⟧. So an operator on singular chains that is natural in $X$ is determined by its value on $\iota_n$, its *model*. Conversely every chain $m \in C_k(\Delta^n)$ defines a natural operator $\sigma \mapsto \sigma_\sharp m$ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · modelOp⟧ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · modelOp_single⟧ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · push_modelOp⟧. The model $\iota_n$ gives the identity ⟦Homology/Subdivision · modelOp_idSimplex⟧; operators add and subtract with their models ⟦Homology/Subdivision · modelOp_add_model⟧ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · modelOp_sub_model⟧ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · modelOp_zero_model⟧; composites of such operators are such operators ⟦Homology/Subdivision · modelOp_modelOp⟧; and so are the boundary after an operator, whose model is $\partial m$ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · sbd_modelOp⟧, and an operator after the boundary, whose model is the operator applied to $\partial\iota_{n+1}$ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · modelOp_sbd⟧. Identities between natural operators thus reduce to identities between chains of standard simplices. Every simplex of $\sigma_\sharp m$ has the form $\sigma \circ \tau$, so these operators preserve chains made of simplices from any family closed under precomposition ⟦Homology/Subdivision · IsPrecompClosed⟧ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · modelOp_mem_supported⟧, for instance the simplices lying in a given subset.

**The models.** The model of subdivision is the barycentric subdivision of $\Delta^n$, the linear chain $S[e_0, \dots, e_n]$ realized as a singular chain on $\Delta^n$ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · sdModel⟧; that of the homotopy is $T[e_0, \dots, e_n]$, realized ⟦Homology/Subdivision · sdHModel⟧. Pushed forward along an affine simplex $[u]$ they give the subdivision and the homotopy of $[u]$ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · push_aff_sdModel⟧ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · push_aff_sdHModel⟧, by the naturality of linear subdivision for affine maps and $[u](e_i) = u_i$ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · aff_comp_verts⟧. The identity simplex is the realization of $[e_0, \dots, e_n]$ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · lreal_verts⟧, and its boundary is the alternating sum of its faces, each an affine simplex ⟦Homology/Subdivision · sbd_idSimplex⟧. From the linear identities of §IV.9 follow the model identities
$$\partial(S\iota_{n+1}) = S(\partial\iota_{n+1}), \qquad \partial(T\iota_{n+1}) + T(\partial\iota_{n+1}) = \iota_{n+1} - S\iota_{n+1}$$
⟦Homology/Subdivision · sbd_sdModel⟧ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · sbd_sdHModel⟧, where $S$ and $T$ on the right act on the faces through their models; and in degree $0$, $S\iota_0 = \iota_0$ and $T\iota_0 = 0$ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · sdModel_zero⟧ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · sdHModel_zero⟧.

**Theorem (subdivision of singular chains).** The operators $S\sigma = \sigma_\sharp(S\iota_n)$ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · ssd⟧ and $T\sigma = \sigma_\sharp(T\iota_n)$ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · ssdH⟧ satisfy:

- $\partial S = S\partial$ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · sbd_ssd⟧ and $\partial T + T\partial = 1 - S$ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · sbd_ssdH⟧, with $S = 1$ and $T = 0$ in degree $0$ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · ssd_zero⟧ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · ssdH_zero⟧;
- both are natural ⟦Homology/Subdivision · push_ssd⟧ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · push_ssdH⟧ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · push_ssd_pow⟧, and both preserve chains made of simplices from a family closed under precomposition ⟦Homology/Subdivision · ssd_mem_supported⟧ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · ssdH_mem_supported⟧ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · ssd_pow_mem_supported⟧;
- on realized linear chains $S$ is linear subdivision ⟦Homology/Subdivision · ssd_lreal⟧ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · ssd_pow_lreal⟧, so that $S^m\sigma$ is $\sigma_\sharp$ of the realized $m$-th barycentric subdivision of $\Delta^n$ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · ssd_pow_single⟧.

*Proof.* By the rules for models, $\partial(Sc)$ is the operator with model $\partial(S\iota_{n+1})$ applied to $c$, and $S(\partial c)$ the operator with model $S(\partial\iota_{n+1})$; the first model identity concludes. The same argument with the second identity, whose right side is the model of $1 - S$, gives $\partial T + T\partial = 1 - S$. The rest is the naturality of $\sigma_\sharp$. $\square$

As morphisms of complexes: subdivision is a chain map ⟦Homology/Subdivision · subdivisionHom⟧, natural in the space ⟦Homology/Subdivision · subdivision⟧, and $T$ is a chain homotopy from the identity to $S$ ⟦Homology/Subdivision · subdivisionHomotopy⟧, assembled degree by degree as in §IV.8.

### IV.12 The small-simplex theorem

Let $U = (U_i)$ be a family of subsets of a space $X$ whose interiors cover $X$. A singular simplex is *$U$-small* if its image lies in some $U_i$ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · smallSet⟧. The simplices lying in a set $A$ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · inSet⟧ form a family closed under precomposition ⟦Homology/SmallChains · isPrecompClosed_inSet⟧, and so do the small ones ⟦Homology/SmallChains · isPrecompClosed_smallSet⟧ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · inSet_subset_smallSet⟧. The boundary preserves chains made of simplices from such a family ⟦Homology/SmallChains · sbd_mem_supported⟧, and $\partial S^m = S^m\partial$ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · sbd_ssd_pow⟧.

**Theorem (Hatcher, Prop. 2.21; V3.28).** The inclusion $\iota : C^U_\bullet(X) \to C_\bullet(X)$ of the complex of $U$-small chains is a chain homotopy equivalence ⟦Homology/SmallChains · smallChainsHomotopyEquiv⟧, with a homotopy inverse $\rho$ satisfying $\rho\iota = 1$; in particular it induces isomorphisms in homology ⟦Homology/SmallChains · quasiIso_smallChainsι⟧. Moreover $\rho$ and the homotopy preserve the chains of every family of simplices closed under precomposition, so that for such a family $P$ the induced map $C^U_\bullet(X)/C^P_\bullet \to C_\bullet(X)/C^P_\bullet$ is again a chain homotopy equivalence ⟦Homology/SmallChains · smallQuotHomotopyEquiv⟧ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · quasiIso_smallQuotMap⟧. Here $C^P_\bullet$ denotes the chains made of simplices in $P$.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. *Fineness.* Let $r(n, m) = (\frac{n}{n+1})^m$ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · subdivRadius⟧, increasing in $n$ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · subdivRadius_mono_left⟧. A simplex $\sigma : \Delta^n \to X$ is *$m$-fine* if it maps every closed ball of radius $r(n, m)$ of $\Delta^n$ into some $U_i$ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · IsFine⟧. Small simplices are $0$-fine ⟦Homology/SmallChains · isFine_zero_of_mem_smallSet⟧.
2. *Every simplex is fine for some $m$* ⟦Homology/SmallChains · exists_isFine⟧. The sets $\sigma^{-1}(\operatorname{int} U_i)$ form an open cover of the compact metric space $\Delta^n$, which has a Lebesgue number $\delta > 0$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/MetricSpace/Pseudo/Lemmas · lebesgue_number_lemma_of_metric⟧. Since $\frac{n}{n+1} < 1$ there is $m$ with $r(n, m) < \delta$ ⟦Mathlib/Algebra/Order/Archimedean/Basic · exists_pow_lt_of_lt_one⟧, and a closed ball of radius $r(n, m)$ lies in the open ball of radius $\delta$ about the same centre.
3. *Fineness passes to faces.* The affine map of an injective vertex map does not increase distances ⟦Homology/SmallChains · dist_simplexMap_le⟧: each coordinate of its value is a coordinate of the argument or $0$. So $\delta^i$ maps the closed ball of radius $r(n, m)$ about $x$ into the closed ball of radius $r(n, m) \le r(n + 1, m)$ about $\delta^i x$; hence $\sigma \circ \delta^i$ is $m$-fine if $\sigma$ is ⟦Homology/SmallChains · IsFine.comp_faceMap⟧.
4. *A fine simplex becomes small* ⟦Homology/SmallChains · IsFine.ssd_pow_mem⟧. If $\sigma$ is $m$-fine, then $S^m\sigma = \sigma_\sharp$ (realized $m$-th subdivision of $\Delta^n$) by §IV.11. Each simplex $[u]$ of that subdivision has $\operatorname{diam}\operatorname{vset}(u) \le r(n, m) \operatorname{diam}\{e_0, \dots, e_n\} \le r(n, m)$ by §IV.10, and every point of $[u]$ is within that distance of $u_0$. So $[u]$ lies in the closed ball of radius $r(n, m)$ about $u_0$, which $\sigma$ maps into some $U_i$.
5. *The order of fineness.* Let $m(\sigma)$ be the least $m$ for which $\sigma$ is $m$-fine ⟦Homology/SmallChains · fineOrder⟧ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · isFine_fineOrder⟧ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · fineOrder_le⟧. By step 3, $m(\sigma \circ \delta^i) \le m(\sigma)$ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · fineOrder_comp_faceMap_le⟧; by step 1, $m(\sigma) = 0$ for small $\sigma$ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · fineOrder_eq_zero⟧; and $S^m\sigma$ is small for every $m \ge m(\sigma)$ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · ssd_pow_mem_of_le⟧, by step 4 and because $S$ preserves small chains.
6. *Hatcher's $D$ and $\rho$.* Let $D_m = \sum_{i<m} TS^i$ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · partialH⟧ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · partialH_apply⟧. Summing $\partial T + T\partial = 1 - S$ at $S^i c$, $i < m$, and using $\partial S^i = S^i\partial$ telescopes to $\partial D_m + D_m\partial = 1 - S^m$ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · partialH_telescope⟧. $D_m$ vanishes on $0$-chains ⟦Homology/SmallChains · partialH_zero_degree⟧, and $TS^i\sigma$ is small for $i \ge m(\sigma)$ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · ssdH_ssd_pow_mem⟧. Define $D\sigma = D_{m(\sigma)}\sigma$ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · smallH⟧ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · smallH_single⟧ and $\rho = 1 - \partial D - D\partial$ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · smallRetr⟧ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · smallRetr_zero_apply⟧ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · smallRetr_succ_apply⟧; $D = 0$ in degree $0$ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · smallH_zero_degree⟧. Then $\rho$ is a chain map ⟦Homology/SmallChains · sbd_smallRetr⟧, because $\partial\partial = 0$.
7. *$\rho$ lands in the small chains* ⟦Homology/SmallChains · smallRetr_mem_supported⟧. In degree $0$, $\rho = 1$, and every $0$-simplex is small: $\Delta^0$ is a point, whose image lies in some $\operatorname{int} U_i$. In degree $n + 1$, with $m = m(\sigma)$ and $\sigma_j = \sigma \circ \delta^j$, the telescope gives Hatcher's formula
$$\rho\sigma = S^m\sigma + \big(D_m(\partial\sigma) - D(\partial\sigma)\big) = S^m\sigma + \sum_j (-1)^j \sum_{m(\sigma_j) \le i < m} TS^i\sigma_j,$$
the inner sums making sense because $m(\sigma_j) \le m$. Every term is small by step 5.
8. *The homotopy equivalence.* $D = 0$ on small chains, because $m(\sigma) = 0$ there ⟦Homology/SmallChains · smallH_eq_zero⟧, so $\rho = 1$ on them ⟦Homology/SmallChains · smallRetr_eq_self⟧; and $D$ and $\rho$ preserve the chains of every family closed under precomposition ⟦Homology/SmallChains · smallH_mem_supported⟧ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · smallRetr_mem_supported_of⟧. As complexes: the chains of such a family form a subcomplex ⟦Homology/SmallChains · supp⟧ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · suppChains⟧ with a quotient ⟦Homology/SmallChains · quotChains⟧; $C^U_\bullet(X)$ is one of them ⟦Homology/SmallChains · smallChains⟧, with inclusion $\iota$ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · smallChainsι⟧; $\rho$ is a chain map into it ⟦Homology/SmallChains · smallRetraction⟧ with $\rho\iota = 1$ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · smallChainsι_smallRetraction⟧; and $D$ is a homotopy $1 \simeq \iota\rho$, $1 - \iota\rho = \partial D + D\partial$ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · smallRetractionHomotopy⟧. For the relative statement ⟦Homology/SmallChains · suppSmall⟧ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · smallQuotChains⟧ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · smallQuotMap⟧ ⟦Homology/SmallChains · smallQuotRetraction⟧, the homotopy descends to the quotients because $D$ preserves the chains of the family (§IV.8). $\square$

*Where the formal proof departs from Hatcher.* Hatcher takes $m(\sigma)$ to be the least $m$ with $S^m\sigma \in C^U_\bullet(X)$ and uses $m(\sigma_j) \le m(\sigma)$ for the faces. For that definition the inequality is not obvious: $S^m\sigma$ is a formal sum in which terms may cancel, so its being small says nothing directly about the subdivisions of the faces. The geometric $m(\sigma)$ of step 5 makes the inequality immediate (step 3).

### IV.13 Excision

**Theorem (excision; Hatcher, Thm. 2.20; V3.28).** Let $R$ be a commutative ring and $E$ a space in the universe of $R$; all the sets below are subsets of $E$, and interiors and closures are taken in the subspace $X$ unless said otherwise.

1. If $A, B \subseteq X$ have interiors covering $X$, then the inclusion $(B, A \cap B) \to (X, A)$ induces isomorphisms $H_n(B, A \cap B) \cong H_n(X, A)$ for all $n$ ⟦Homology/Excision · excision_of_union⟧; more generally so does $(B, C) \to (X, A)$ for any $C$ equal to $A \cap B$ ⟦Homology/Excision · excision_of_eq_inter⟧.
2. If $Z \subseteq A \subseteq X$ and the closure of $Z$ lies in the interior of $A$, then $(X \setminus Z, A \setminus Z) \to (X, A)$ induces isomorphisms in all degrees ⟦Homology/Excision · excision⟧; the same holds with closure and interior taken in $E$ ⟦Homology/Excision · excision_of_closure_subset_interior⟧.

*The proof as formalized* has two halves: excision for the concrete chains of §IV.8, and a bridge from the relative chains of §IV.5 to concrete quotients.

1. *Simplices along an embedding.* If $j : Y \to X$ is an embedding, a singular simplex of $X$ with image in $j(Y)$ lifts to $Y$ ⟦Homology/Excision · exists_comp_eq_of_range_subset⟧: choose preimages pointwise; the lift is continuous because $j$ is an embedding. $j_\sharp$ is injective on chains ⟦Homology/Excision · push_injective⟧, its image is the set of chains made of simplices in $j(Y)$ ⟦Homology/Excision · range_push⟧, and $j_\sharp c$ is made of simplices in a set $S$ exactly when $c$ is made of simplices in $j^{-1}(S)$ ⟦Homology/Excision · push_mem_supported_iff⟧.
2. *The excision map at the chain level.* For a continuous $j : Y \to X$ and $A \subseteq X$, $j_\sharp$ induces $C_\bullet(Y)/C_\bullet(j^{-1}A) \to C_\bullet(X)/C_\bullet(A)$ ⟦Homology/Excision · excisionMap⟧ ⟦Homology/Excision · push_mem_inSet_preimage⟧, where $C_\bullet(A)$ now means the chains made of simplices in $A$; for the inclusion of $B$ it is $C_\bullet(B)/C_\bullet(A \cap B) \to C_\bullet(X)/C_\bullet(A)$. Let $U = \{A, B\}$, indexed by the two booleans ⟦Homology/Excision · coverOf⟧ ⟦Homology/Excision · iUnion_interior_coverOf⟧; a simplex is $U$-small exactly when it lies in $A$ or in $B$ ⟦Homology/Excision · mem_smallSet_coverOf⟧. When $j$ is an embedding with image $B$, $j_\sharp$ lands in the $U$-small chains ⟦Homology/Excision · push_mem_smallSet⟧ ⟦Homology/Excision · pushSmall⟧ ⟦Homology/Excision · pushSmall_ι⟧, and the excision map factors as
$$C_\bullet(B)/C_\bullet(A \cap B) \to C^U_\bullet(X)/C_\bullet(A) \to C_\bullet(X)/C_\bullet(A)$$
⟦Homology/Excision · excisionMapSmall⟧ ⟦Homology/Excision · excisionMapSmall_comp⟧.
3. *The first factor is an isomorphism* ⟦Homology/Excision · bijective_excisionMapSmall_f⟧ ⟦Homology/Excision · isIso_excisionMapSmall⟧. It is injective: a chain of $B$ whose image is made of simplices in $A$ is made of simplices in $A \cap B$, by step 1. It is surjective: a $U$-small chain $x$ splits as the part $x_A$ of its terms that lie in $A$ plus the rest, whose terms are small and not in $A$, hence in $B$. By step 1 the rest is $j_\sharp c$ for a chain $c$ of $B$, and $x - j_\sharp c = x_A$ lies in $C_\bullet(A)$, so $x$ and $j_\sharp c$ have the same class.
4. *Excision at the chain level* ⟦Homology/Excision · quasiIso_excisionMap⟧. The second factor is a chain homotopy equivalence by the relative small-simplex theorem of §IV.12, with $P$ the simplices in $A$; so the excision map is a quasi-isomorphism.
5. *Relative chains are concrete quotients.* For a pair $(X, A)$ with $S \subseteq X$ the image of $A$, let $C_\bullet(X) \to C_\bullet(X)/C_\bullet(S)$ be the concrete projection ⟦Homology/Excision · quotπ⟧, which kills the image of $C_\bullet(A)$ ⟦Homology/Excision · pushHom_comp_quotπ⟧. The concrete sequence $0 \to C_\bullet(A) \to C_\bullet(X) \to C_\bullet(X)/C_\bullet(S) \to 0$ is short exact ⟦Homology/Excision · concreteRelShortComplex_shortExact⟧, by §IV.8 and step 1. Transported through the comparison isomorphism of §IV.8 ⟦Homology/Excision · mathlibRelShortComplexIso⟧, so is the sequence that starts with Mathlib's $C_\bullet(A) \to C_\bullet(X)$ ⟦Homology/Excision · mathlibRelShortComplex_shortExact⟧, and the concrete quotient is a cokernel of Mathlib's map ⟦Homology/Excision · isColimitMathlibRelShortComplex⟧. Two cokernels of one map are canonically isomorphic: $C_\bullet(X, A) \cong C_\bullet(X)/C_\bullet(S)$ ⟦Homology/Excision · relChainsIsoQuot⟧ ⟦Homology/Excision · relπ_relChainsIsoQuot_hom⟧, compatibly with maps of pairs ⟦Homology/Excision · relChainsIsoQuot_naturality⟧.
6. *Excision* ⟦Homology/Excision · excision_of_eq_inter⟧. Let $j : B \to X$ be the inclusion, with image $B$; the image of $C = A \cap B$ in $B$ is $j^{-1}(A)$. By step 5 the map of relative chains induced by $(B, C) \to (X, A)$ is isomorphic, as an arrow, to the excision map of step 2, a quasi-isomorphism by step 4; so it is a quasi-isomorphism and induces isomorphisms in relative homology. Form 2 ⟦Homology/Excision · excision⟧ is form 1 with $B = X \setminus Z$: then $A \cap B = A \setminus Z$, and the interior of $X \setminus Z$ is the complement of the closure of $Z$, which together with the interior of $A$, containing that closure, covers $X$. For closures and interiors in $E$ ⟦Homology/Excision · excision_of_closure_subset_interior⟧: the closure in $X$ of $Z \cap X$ lies in the closure of $Z$ in $E$, and the interior of $A$ in $E$, intersected with $X$, lies in the interior of $A$ in $X$. $\square$

### IV.14 The Mayer–Vietoris sequence

**Theorem (V3.29).** With coefficients in a commutative ring, if $A, B \subseteq X$ have interiors covering $X$, the sequence
$$\cdots \to H_{n+1}(X) \xrightarrow{\ \partial\ } H_n(A \cap B) \xrightarrow{(i_*,\, -i'_*)} H_n(A) \oplus H_n(B) \xrightarrow{\ j_* + j'_*\ } H_n(X) \xrightarrow{\ \partial\ } H_{n-1}(A \cap B) \to \cdots$$
is exact at every place ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · mv_exact₁⟧ ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · mv_exact₂⟧ ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · mv_exact₃⟧, where $i, i', j, j'$ are the inclusions ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · mvMap₁⟧ ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · mvMap₂⟧ and $\partial$ is defined in the proof ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · mvδ⟧.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. Inside the concrete chains of $X$, the chains made of simplices in a subset $S$ form a subcomplex $C_\bullet(S)$ ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · chainsIn⟧, with inclusions for $S \subseteq S'$ ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · chainsInIncl⟧ and into the $\{A, B\}$-small chains for $S = A, B$ ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · chainsInToSmall⟧.
2. The sequence $0 \to C_\bullet(A \cap B) \to C_\bullet(A) \oplus C_\bullet(B) \to C^U_\bullet(X) \to 0$, with maps $x \mapsto (x, -x)$ and $(y, z) \mapsto y + z$ ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · mvShortComplex⟧, is short exact ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · mvShortComplex_shortExact⟧, degree by degree. The first map is injective. If $y + z = 0$ with $y$ made of simplices in $A$ and $z$ of simplices in $B$, then $y = -z$ is made of simplices lying in both, hence in $A \cap B$ ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · inSet_inter⟧, and $(y, z)$ is the image of $y$; an element of the biproduct is determined by its two components ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · biprod_X_eq_add⟧. And a small chain is a chain in $A$ plus a chain in $B$, by splitting off its terms in $A$ as in §IV.13.
3. The homology of the concrete subcomplexes is identified with Mathlib's homology of the subspaces. The singular chains of a subspace $S$ are the chains of $X$ made of simplices in $S$ ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · subspaceChainsHom⟧ ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · bijective_subspaceChainsHom_f⟧ ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · subspaceChainsIso⟧; composed with the comparison of §IV.8 this gives ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · mathlibChainsIso⟧, compatibly with inclusions ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · mathlibChainsIso_incl⟧ ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · mathlibChainsIso_val⟧. Homology commutes with biproducts, being an additive functor, and $C^U_\bullet(X)$ has the homology of $X$ by the small-simplex theorem ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · isIso_homologyMap_smallChainsι⟧. These give three isomorphisms ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · mvIso₁⟧ ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · mvIso₂⟧ ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · mvIso₃⟧ under which the maps of the homology sequence become $(i_*, -i'_*)$ and $j_* + j'_*$ ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · mvIso₁_hom_comp_mvMap₁⟧ ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · mvIso₂_hom_comp_mvMap₂⟧, and $\partial$ is the connecting morphism transported by them.
4. Exactness is transported along isomorphisms of short complexes ⟦Homology/MayerVietoris · exact_mk_of_iso⟧. $\square$

Mayer–Vietoris is not used in §5; it belongs to the infrastructure, and the naturality of its $\partial$ is not proved.

### IV.15 Excision for an attached cell

Milnor's "by excision" on p. 29 identifies $H_*(M^{a_{i-1}} \cup e^\lambda, M^{a_{i-1}})$ with $H_*(e^\lambda, \dot e^\lambda)$. As it stands this is not an excision. One would excise $M^{a_{i-1}} \setminus \dot e^\lambda$, but the closure of that set contains points of $\dot e^\lambda$, and these are not interior points of $M^{a_{i-1}}$ in $M^{a_{i-1}} \cup e^\lambda$, being limits of points of the open cell. The formal proof first removes the centre of the cell and deforms what is left onto the bottom set; then excision applies.

*Setting.* $E$ is a Hausdorff space and $Y \subseteq X \subseteq E$, with $Y$ closed in $X$. $V$ is a real normed space whose closed balls are compact (for instance a finite-dimensional one), with closed unit ball $D$ and unit sphere $S$. $\chi : V \to E$ is continuous and injective on $D$, with $\chi(z) \in Y \iff \|z\| = 1$ for $z \in D$, and $X = Y \cup \chi(D)$. So $e = \chi(D)$ is a closed cell meeting $Y$ exactly in its boundary sphere $\chi(S)$. The pair $(\chi(D), \chi(S))$ is the *cell pair* of $\chi$ ⟦Homology/CellExcision · cellPair⟧, and $\chi(S) \subseteq Y$ ⟦Homology/CellExcision · image_sphere_subset⟧.

**Lemma (the inverse of a parametrization).** If $f$ is continuous and injective on a compact set $s$, with values in a Hausdorff space, its inverse is continuous on $f(s)$ ⟦Homology/CellExcision · continuousOn_invFunOn_image⟧: the preimage under the inverse of a closed set $t$ is $f(s \cap t)$, which is compact and hence closed.

**The radial deformation.** For $z \ne 0$ and $0 \le t \le 1$ let $\rho_t(z) = \big((1 - t) + t/\|z\|\big)\, z$ ⟦Homology/CellExcision · radialPush⟧. Then $\rho_0 = \mathrm{id}$ ⟦Homology/CellExcision · radialPush_zero⟧; $\rho_t$ fixes the unit sphere ⟦Homology/CellExcision · radialPush_of_norm_eq_one⟧; $\|\rho_t(z)\| = (1 - t)\|z\| + t$ ⟦Homology/CellExcision · norm_radialPush⟧, so $\rho_t(z) \ne 0$ ⟦Homology/CellExcision · radialPush_ne_zero⟧, $\rho_t$ maps the punctured ball into the ball ⟦Homology/CellExcision · radialPush_mem_closedBall⟧, and $\|\rho_1(z)\| = 1$ ⟦Homology/CellExcision · norm_radialPush_one⟧; and $(t, z) \mapsto \rho_t(z)$ is continuous away from $z = 0$ ⟦Homology/CellExcision · continuousOn_radialPush⟧.

**Lemma (the space minus the centre retracts onto $Y$; V3.31).** In the setting above, with $p = \chi(0)$, $Y$ is a strong deformation retract of $X \setminus \{p\}$ ⟦Homology/CellExcision · isStrongDeformationRetract_sdiff_center⟧.

*Proof.* Let $g$ be the inverse of $\chi$ on $\chi(D)$, continuous by the lemma, $D$ being compact. Define
$$k(t, x) = x \ \text{ for } x \in Y, \qquad k(t, x) = \chi\big(\rho_t(g(x))\big) \ \text{ for } x \in \chi(D) \setminus Y .$$
For $x \ne p$ in the cell, $g(x) \ne 0$, so the second formula makes sense. At $t = 0$ it gives $\chi(g(x)) = x$; at $t = 1$, $\rho_1(g(x))$ lies on the sphere, so $k(1, x) \in Y$; and $k(t, x) \ne p$, because $\rho_t(g(x)) \ne 0$ and $\chi$ is injective on $D$. The second formula also gives $x$ on $Y \cap \chi(D)$: there $\chi(g(x)) = x \in Y$ forces $\|g(x)\| = 1$, which $\rho_t$ fixes. $Y$ is closed in $X$ by hypothesis, and $\chi(D)$ is compact, hence closed in the Hausdorff space $E$; these two closed sets cover $X \setminus \{p\}$, and on each of them $k$ is continuous, so $k$ is continuous by the pasting lemma. It fixes $Y$ at all times. $\square$

The same tools show that a cell contracts onto any of its points, by the straight-line homotopy $(t, x) \mapsto \chi\big((1 - t) g(x) + t z_0\big)$ in the convex ball ⟦Homology/CellExcision · isStrongDeformationRetract_singleton_image⟧. Excision is used in a form that accepts the excised pair up to equality of sets ⟦Homology/CellExcision · excision_of_eq⟧.

**Theorem (excision for an attached cell; V3.31).** In the setting above, with coefficients in a commutative ring and $E$ in its universe, the inclusion $(\chi(D), \chi(S)) \to (X, Y)$ induces isomorphisms $H_n(\chi(D), \chi(S)) \cong H_n(X, Y)$ in all degrees ⟦Homology/CellExcision · isIso_relHomology_map_cellPair⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Let $p = \chi(0)$ and $e = \chi(D)$; $p \notin Y$ since $\|0\| \ne 1$.

1. $(X, Y) \to (X, X \setminus \{p\})$ induces isomorphisms: $Y$ is a strong deformation retract of $X \setminus \{p\}$ by the lemma, and the bottom of a pair moves along such retracts (§IV.7).
2. $(e, e \setminus \{p\}) \to (X, X \setminus \{p\})$ induces isomorphisms, by excision of $Z = X \setminus e$. Indeed $X \setminus Z = e$ and $(X \setminus \{p\}) \setminus Z = e \setminus \{p\}$. Since $X = Y \cup e$, $Z \subseteq Y$, so the closure of $Z$ lies in the closed set $Y$, hence in $X \setminus \{p\}$, which is open in $X$ (points are closed in a Hausdorff space) and is therefore its own interior.
3. $(e, \chi(S)) \to (e, e \setminus \{p\})$ induces isomorphisms: step 1 for the cell alone, with $e$ in place of $X$ and $\chi(S)$ in place of $Y$. The sphere $\chi(S)$ is closed, as a compact image, and $\chi(z) \in \chi(S) \iff \|z\| = 1$ by injectivity.
4. The square of inclusions, $(e, \chi(S)) \to (X, Y) \to (X, X \setminus \{p\})$ against $(e, \chi(S)) \to (e, e \setminus \{p\}) \to (X, X \setminus \{p\})$, commutes ⟦Homology/CellExcision · subsetPairHom_comp⟧. The second route induces isomorphisms by steps 3 and 2, and the second map of the first route by step 1; so the first map of the first route does. $\square$

The domain of $\chi$ plays no role in the homology: only subsets of $E$ appear.

### IV.16 The homology of disks and spheres

**Theorem (V3.30).** Let $R$ be a commutative ring (in the lowest universe, where Euclidean space lives), and $D^n$, $S^{n-1}$ the closed unit ball and the unit sphere of Euclidean $\mathbb R^n$, with $S^{-1} = \emptyset$. Then $H_n(D^n, S^{n-1}) \cong R$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · relHomologyDiskPairIso⟧ and $H_i(D^n, S^{n-1}) = 0$ for $i \ne n$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · isZero_relHomology_diskPair⟧; over a field, $\dim H_i(D^n, S^{n-1}) = [i = n]$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · finrank_relHomology_diskPair⟧. For $n \ge 1$, $H_0(S^n) \cong R$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · sphereHomologyZeroIso⟧ and $H_n(S^n) \cong R$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · sphereHomologyTopIso⟧; $H_0(S^0) \cong R \oplus R$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · sphereHomologyZeroIsoBiprod⟧; $H_i(S^n) = 0$ for $i \ne 0, n$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · isZero_sphereHomology⟧; and over a field $\dim H_i(S^n) = [i = 0] + [i = n]$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · finrank_sphereHomology⟧.

Everything is proved for embedded cells and spheres in a Hausdorff space $E$ in the universe of $R$. If $\chi : \mathbb R^k \to E$ is continuous and injective on $D^k$, the cell pair $(\chi(D^k), \chi(S^{k-1}))$ has the homology of $(D^k, S^{k-1})$; the theorem is the case $\chi = \mathrm{id}$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · diskPair⟧ ⟦Homology/Spheres · diskPairIsoCellPair⟧ ⟦Homology/Spheres · sphereHomology⟧.

**Hemispheres.** For $s = \pm 1$ the map $h_s : z \mapsto \big(z, s\sqrt{1 - \|z\|^2}\big)$ from $\mathbb R^n$ to $\mathbb R^{n+1}$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · hemisphere⟧ is continuous ⟦Homology/Spheres · continuous_hemisphere⟧ and injective ⟦Homology/Spheres · hemisphere_injective⟧; since $\|(z, t)\|^2 = \|z\|^2 + t^2$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · norm_sq_hemisphere⟧, it maps $D^n$ into $S^n$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · hemisphere_mem_sphere⟧. $S^n$ is the union of the lower hemisphere $h_{-1}(D^n)$ and the upper hemisphere $h_1(D^n)$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · sphere_eq_union_hemisphere⟧: for $x \in S^n$ with first coordinates $z$, $\|z\|^2 = 1 - x_{n+1}^2 \le 1$ and $\sqrt{1 - \|z\|^2} = |x_{n+1}|$, so $x = h_{\pm 1}(z)$ according to the sign of $x_{n+1}$. A point of the upper hemisphere lies in the lower one exactly on the equator ⟦Homology/Spheres · hemisphere_one_mem_iff⟧: equal first coordinates and $\sqrt{1 - \|z\|^2} = -\sqrt{1 - \|z\|^2}$ force $\|z\| = 1$.

**Splitting off a retract.** If the inclusion $A \hookrightarrow X$ of a pair has a retraction $r$, the sequence of the pair splits, $H_n(X) \cong H_n(A) \oplus H_n(X, A)$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · homologyIsoBiprodOfRetraction⟧. Indeed $r_* \circ i_* = 1$ makes $i_*$ injective in every degree, so $\partial = 0$, being followed by the injective $i_*$ with $i_* \circ \partial = 0$; hence $H_n(X) \to H_n(X, A)$ is onto (for $n = 0$ by §IV.5, for $n = m + 1$ by exactness at $H_{m+1}(X, A)$); and an exact sequence $H_n(A) \to H_n(X) \to H_n(X, A) \to 0$ whose first map has a retraction splits ⟦Mathlib/Algebra/Homology/ShortComplex/Exact · Splitting.ofExactOfRetraction⟧. For a point $q \in B$ and the constant retraction ⟦Homology/Spheres · retractionSingleton⟧ ⟦Homology/Spheres · map_retractionSingleton⟧ this is reduced homology, $H_n(B) \cong H_n(\{q\}) \oplus H_n(B, \{q\})$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · homologyIsoBiprodSingleton⟧. A point has $H_n = 0$ for $n \ne 0$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · isZero_homology_singleton⟧, by §IV.7, and $H_0 \cong R$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · homologyZeroSingletonIso⟧, by Mathlib's augmentation, an isomorphism for path-connected spaces ⟦Mathlib/AlgebraicTopology/SingularHomology/HomologyZero · singularHomology₀ε⟧. Over a field, dimensions are read off from such isomorphisms ⟦Homology/Spheres · finrank_eq_one_of_iso⟧ ⟦Homology/Spheres · finrank_eq_zero_of_isZero⟧ ⟦Homology/Spheres · finrank_biprod⟧ ⟦Homology/Spheres · finite_and_finrank_eq_ite⟧.

**Theorem (cells; V3.30).** If $\chi : \mathbb R^k \to E$ is continuous and injective on $D^k$, then $H_k(\chi(D^k), \chi(S^{k-1})) \cong R$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · cellPairIso⟧ and $H_i(\chi(D^k), \chi(S^{k-1})) = 0$ for $i \ne k$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · isZero_relHomology_cellPair⟧; over a field the dimension is $[i = k]$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · finrank_relHomology_cellPair⟧.

*The proof as formalized,* by induction on $k$.

1. *The base case.* For $k = 0$, $D^0$ is a point and $S^{-1}$, a sphere of radius $1$ in the zero space, is empty ⟦Homology/Spheres · image_closedBall_fin_zero⟧ ⟦Homology/Spheres · image_sphere_fin_zero⟧; so the pair is $(\{\chi(0)\}, \emptyset)$, whose homology is that of a point ⟦Homology/Spheres · relHomologyCellPairZeroIso⟧.
2. *A cell has no homology relative to one of its points:* $H_*(\chi(D), \{\chi(z_0)\}) = 0$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · isZero_relHomology_cell_singleton⟧, the point being a strong deformation retract of the cell (§IV.15).
3. *The boundary of a triple.* For $\chi$ on $\mathbb R^{k+1}$ let $q = \chi(h_{-1}(0))$, the image of the south pole $-e_{k+1}$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · hemisphere_neg_one_zero_mem_sphere⟧ ⟦Homology/Spheres · singleton_subset_image_sphere⟧. In the triple $\{q\} \subseteq \chi(S^k) \subseteq \chi(D^{k+1})$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · cellTriple⟧, the middle terms $H_*(\chi(D^{k+1}), \{q\})$ vanish by step 2. So the boundary $H_{i+1}(\chi(D^{k+1}), \chi(S^k)) \to H_i(\chi(S^k), \{q\})$ is an isomorphism ⟦Homology/Spheres · isIso_cellTriple_δ⟧ (a connecting morphism is invertible when the middle complex has no homology in the two degrees involved), and $H_0(\chi(D^{k+1}), \chi(S^k))$, a quotient of $H_0(\chi(D^{k+1}), \{q\}) = 0$ by §IV.6, vanishes.
4. *The sphere lemma* ⟦Homology/Spheres · sphereRelIso⟧. Let $\chi$ be continuous and injective on $S^k \subseteq \mathbb R^{k+1}$, and $q$ as in step 3, with the pair $(\chi(S^k), \{q\})$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · spherePointPair⟧. Then $H_i(\chi(S^k), \{q\}) \cong H_i(\chi(D_+), \chi(S^{k-1}))$, where $\chi(D_+) = \chi(h_1(D^k))$ is the upper hemisphere, the cell of the map $\chi \circ h_1$ on $\mathbb R^k$. First, $(\chi(S^k), \{q\}) \to (\chi(S^k), \chi(D_-))$ induces isomorphisms, because the lower hemisphere $\chi(D_-) = \chi(h_{-1}(D^k))$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · lowerHemisphere_subset⟧ ⟦Homology/Spheres · singleton_subset_lowerHemisphere⟧ contracts onto $q$ (§§IV.7, IV.15). Second, $\chi(S^k) = \chi(D_-) \cup \chi(D_+)$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · image_sphere_eq_union⟧, $\chi(D_-)$ is closed, being compact ⟦Homology/Spheres · isClosed_lowerHemisphere⟧, and $\chi(h_1(z))$ lies in $\chi(D_-)$ exactly when $\|z\| = 1$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · upperHemisphere_mem_iff⟧, by the injectivity of $\chi$ on $S^k$ and the hemisphere lemma. So the upper hemisphere is a $k$-cell attached to the lower one along the equator, and excision for an attached cell (§IV.15) makes $(\chi(D_+), \chi(S^{k-1})) \to (\chi(S^k), \chi(D_-))$ an isomorphism in homology. The maps $\chi \circ h_{\pm 1}$ are continuous and injective on $D^k$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · continuousOn_comp_hemisphere⟧ ⟦Homology/Spheres · injOn_comp_hemisphere⟧.
5. *Induction.* $H_{k+1}(\chi(D^{k+1}), \chi(S^k)) \cong H_k(\chi(S^k), \{q\}) \cong H_k(\chi(D_+), \chi(S^{k-1})) \cong R$, the last by induction for the $k$-cell $\chi \circ h_1$. For $i + 1 \ne k + 1$ the same chain gives $H_{i+1}(\chi(D^{k+1}), \chi(S^k)) \cong H_i$ of the $k$-cell pair, which vanishes by induction since $i \ne k$; and $H_0 = 0$ by step 3. $\square$

**Embedded spheres.** For $\chi$ continuous and injective on $S^n$: $H_n(\chi(S^n), \{q\}) \cong R$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · spherePointPairIso⟧ and $H_i(\chi(S^n), \{q\}) = 0$ for $i \ne n$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · isZero_relHomology_spherePointPair⟧, by the sphere lemma and the theorem on cells. Reduced homology then gives $H_0(\chi(S^n)) \cong R$ for $n \ne 0$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · homologyImageSphereZeroIso⟧, $H_n(\chi(S^n)) \cong R$ for $n \ne 0$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · homologyImageSphereTopIso⟧, $H_i(\chi(S^n)) = 0$ for $i \ne 0, n$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · isZero_homology_imageSphere⟧, $H_0(\chi(S^0)) \cong R \oplus R$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · homologyImageSphereZeroIsoBiprod⟧, and over a field $\dim H_i(\chi(S^n)) = [i = 0] + [i = n]$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · finrank_homology_imageSphere⟧.

**Mathlib's disk, in any universe.** Mathlib's CW complexes use the pair $(\mathbb D^n, \partial\mathbb D^n)$, the closed ball and the sphere of $\mathbb R^n$ lifted to an arbitrary universe ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Category/TopCat/Sphere · disk⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Category/TopCat/Sphere · diskBoundary⟧. The project makes it a pair ⟦Homology/Spheres · topDiskPair⟧ ⟦Homology/Spheres · isEmbedding_diskBoundaryInclusion⟧, and shows that $\chi$ induces a map of pairs $(\mathbb D^k, \partial\mathbb D^k) \to (\chi(D^k), \chi(S^{k-1}))$ ⟦Homology/Spheres · diskToCell⟧ that is an isomorphism in relative homology ⟦Homology/Spheres · isIso_relHomology_map_diskToCell⟧: both components are continuous bijections from compact spaces onto Hausdorff spaces, hence homeomorphisms, and in particular homotopy equivalences ⟦Homology/Spheres · homotopyEquivOfBijective⟧. With $\chi$ the lift of $\mathbb R^n$ into the universe, this gives $H_n(\mathbb D^n, \partial\mathbb D^n) \cong R$ and $H_i = 0$ for $i \ne n$, in every universe ⟦Homology/Spheres · relHomologyTopDiskPairIso⟧ ⟦Homology/Spheres · isZero_relHomology_topDiskPair⟧ ⟦Homology/Spheres · finrank_relHomology_topDiskPair⟧.

### IV.17 The cell-attachment theorem

On p. 29 Milnor needs the homology of $M^{c-\varepsilon}$ with cells attached, relative to $M^{c-\varepsilon}$; with Remark 3.3 several cells are attached on one level at once.

**Theorem (one cell; V3.31).** Let $E$ be a Hausdorff space, $Y \subseteq X \subseteq E$ with $Y$ closed, and $\chi : \mathbb R^k \to E$ continuous and injective on $D^k$, with $\chi(z) \in Y \iff \|z\| = 1$ for $z \in D^k$ and $X = Y \cup \chi(D^k)$. Let the coefficients be a commutative ring $R$ in the universe of $E$. Then the characteristic map $\chi : (\mathbb D^k, \partial\mathbb D^k) \to (X, Y)$ ⟦Homology/CellAttach · cellAttachMap⟧, which passes through the cell pair ⟦Homology/CellAttach · cellPairHom⟧, induces isomorphisms in all degrees ⟦Homology/CellAttach · relHomology_cellAttach_iso⟧ ⟦Homology/CellAttach · relHomologyCellAttachIso⟧. Hence $H_k(X, Y) \cong R$ ⟦Homology/CellAttach · relHomologyCellAttachTopIso⟧, $H_i(X, Y) = 0$ for $i \ne k$ ⟦Homology/CellAttach · isZero_relHomology_cellAttach⟧, and over a field $H_i(X, Y)$ is finite-dimensional, of dimension $[i = k]$ ⟦Homology/CellAttach · finite_and_finrank_relHomology_cellAttach⟧ ⟦Homology/CellAttach · finrank_relHomology_cellAttach⟧.

*Proof.* The map factors as $(\mathbb D^k, \partial\mathbb D^k) \to (\chi(D^k), \chi(S^{k-1})) \to (X, Y)$. The first factor is an isomorphism in homology by §IV.16, the second by excision for an attached cell (§IV.15). $\square$

**Theorem (several disjoint cells, the situation of Remark 3.3; V3.31).** Let moreover $\chi_j : \mathbb R^{k_j} \to E$, for $j$ in a finite set $s$, be maps of the same kind with pairwise disjoint cells $e_j = \chi_j(D^{k_j})$, and $X = Y \cup \bigcup_{j \in s} e_j$. Over a field, $H_i(X, Y)$ is finite-dimensional, of dimension $\#\{j \in s : k_j = i\}$ ⟦Homology/CellAttach · finite_and_finrank_relHomology_cellsUnion⟧ ⟦Homology/CellAttach · finrank_relHomology_cellsAttach⟧ ⟦Homology/CellAttach · finiteDimensional_relHomology_cellsAttach⟧ ⟦Homology/CellAttach · finrank_relHomology_cellsAttach_univ⟧. With any commutative ring of coefficients, $H_i(X, Y) \cong R^{c_i}$, $c_i$ the number of cells of dimension $i$ ⟦Homology/CellAttach · nonempty_relHomology_cellsUnion_iso⟧ ⟦Homology/CellAttach · relHomology_cellsAttach_iso⟧; in particular $H_i(X, Y) = 0$ if no cell has dimension $i$ ⟦Homology/CellAttach · isZero_relHomology_cellsUnion⟧ ⟦Homology/CellAttach · isZero_relHomology_cellsAttach⟧.

*The idea.* Attach the cells one at a time. The exact sequence of the triple $Y \subseteq X_s \subseteq X_{s \cup \{a\}}$ alone gives only an inequality of dimensions. Disjointness makes the sequence split, and then dimensions add.

*The proof as formalized,* by induction on $s$, writing $X_s = Y \cup \bigcup_{j \in s} e_j$ ⟦Homology/CellAttach · cellsUnion⟧.

1. *Bookkeeping.* $X_{s \cup \{a\}} = X_s \cup e_a$ ⟦Homology/CellAttach · cellsUnion_insert⟧ ⟦Homology/CellAttach · cellsUnion_subset_insert⟧. $X_s$ is closed, the cells being compact ⟦Homology/CellAttach · isClosed_cellsUnion⟧. A cell disjoint from the others meets $X_s$ only in $Y$ ⟦Homology/CellAttach · inter_cellsUnion_subset⟧, so for $a \notin s$, $\chi_a(z) \in X_s \iff \|z\| = 1$ ⟦Homology/CellAttach · mem_cellsUnion_iff⟧: $e_a$ is a cell attached to the closed set $X_s$.
2. *The base case.* $X_\emptyset = Y$, and $H_*(Y, Y) = 0$ ⟦Homology/CellAttach · isZero_relHomology_cellsUnion_empty⟧.
3. *The splitting* ⟦Homology/CellAttach · relHomologyCellsUnionInsertIso⟧. For $a \notin s$ and $X = X_{s \cup \{a\}}$, $H_i(X, Y) \cong H_i(X_s, Y) \oplus H_i(X, X_s)$. The inclusion $\varepsilon : (X_s, Y) \to (X, Y \cup e_a)$ is the excision of $Z = e_a \setminus Y$: $X \setminus Z = X_s$, and $(Y \cup e_a) \setminus Z = Y$ because $e_a \cap X_s \subseteq Y$. The closure of $Z$ lies in the closed set $e_a$, which lies in $X \setminus \bigcup_{j \in s} e_j$, the cells being disjoint; that set is open in $X$ and contained in $Y \cup e_a$, so $e_a$ lies in the interior of $Y \cup e_a$. So $\varepsilon$ induces isomorphisms. Now $\varepsilon$ is the inclusion $(X_s, Y) \to (X, Y)$ followed by $\pi : (X, Y) \to (X, Y \cup e_a)$, so $(\varepsilon_*)^{-1} \circ \pi_*$ is a retraction of $H_*(X_s, Y) \to H_*(X, Y)$. A triple whose first map has a retraction in every degree splits ⟦Homology/CellAttach · TopTriple.isoBiprodOfRetraction⟧, by the argument of §IV.16 for the triple $Y \subseteq X_s \subseteq X$.
4. *Induction.* $H_i(X, X_s)$ is the homology of one cell attached to the closed set $X_s$ (step 1 and the one-cell theorem): of dimension $[k_a = i]$ over a field, and $R$ in degree $k_a$, $0$ elsewhere, in general. Dimensions add over the direct sum, and $\#\{j \in s \cup \{a\} : k_j = i\} = \#\{j \in s : k_j = i\} + [k_a = i]$; with general coefficients, $R^c \oplus R \cong R^{c+1}$. $\square$

The disjointness of the cells is used exactly in step 3: it makes $e_a$ lie in the interior of $Y \cup e_a$ inside $X$, which is what makes the connecting maps of the triple vanish.

The form in which §IV.19 uses this is a property of a field $\Bbbk$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · CellAttachHomology⟧: for every Hausdorff space in the universe of $\Bbbk$, every closed $Y$ and every finite family of pairwise disjoint cells $\chi_j$, $j < m$, attached to $Y$ as above, $H_i(Y \cup \bigcup_j e_j, Y; \Bbbk)$ is finite-dimensional, of dimension $\#\{j : k_j = i\}$. It holds for every field ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyCells · cellAttachHomology⟧, by the theorem above.

### IV.18 Singular homology as a homology of a filtration

With the sequences of triples in hand, singular homology over a field is an instance of the abstract structure of §IV.3, and all of §§IV.3–IV.4 applies to it (V3.32).

**The sequence of a triple as a long exact sequence.** For every triple, with coefficients in a field $\Bbbk$, the spaces $A_i = H_i(Y, Z)$, $B_i = H_i(X, Z)$, $C_i = H_i(X, Y)$ and the maps of §IV.6 form a long exact sequence in the sense of §IV.1 ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · TopTriple.longExactSeq⟧: exactness in the category of $\Bbbk$-modules is exactness of the underlying linear maps, and an epimorphism of modules is a surjection. For subsets $A \subseteq B$ of a space, write $H_i(B, A; \Bbbk)$ for the homology of the pair of subsets ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · subsetHomology⟧. It depends only on the subsets ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · finrank_subsetHomology_congr⟧ ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · finiteDimensional_subsetHomology_congr⟧, $H_i(A, A) = 0$ ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · subsingleton_subsetHomology_self⟧, and a triple of subsets $A \subseteq B \subseteq D$ has its sequence ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · subsetTripleLongExactSeq⟧, also when the pair $(D, B)$ is replaced by an equal one ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · subsetTripleLongExactSeq'⟧.

**The filtration.** Let $X_0 \subseteq X_1 \subseteq \cdots$ be subsets of a space. The abstract structure needs a group for every pair of indices, and the choice is $H(i, a, b) = H_i(X_{\max(a, b)}, X_a; \Bbbk)$ ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · filtrationGroup⟧. This is a pair of subsets for all $a, b$, and equals $H_i(X_b, X_a)$ when $a \le b$ ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · finrank_filtrationGroup⟧. Because $\max(a, b) = b$ holds only by a proof, the sequences of triples are transported along equalities of subsets. If every step $(X_{k+1}, X_k)$ has finite-dimensional homology, the result is a homology of a filtration ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · filtrationHomology⟧, built by the constructor of §IV.3, with $H_*(X_k, X_k) = 0$ on the diagonal.

The final theorems should assume finiteness only for the steps $k < n$ that are used. So the construction is applied to the truncated filtration $k \mapsto X_{\min(k, n)}$ ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · monotone_truncate⟧ ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · truncatedFiltrationHomology⟧, constant from $n$ on. Its steps are the steps $k < n$ of $X$ followed by copies of $(X_n, X_n)$, which have zero homology ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · finiteDimensional_truncate_step⟧; and it has the same total groups $H_i(X_n, X_0)$ ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · finrank_truncate_total⟧ and the same Morse numbers $C_i = \sum_{k<n} \dim H_i(X_{k+1}, X_k; \Bbbk)$ ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · singularMorseNumber⟧ ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · morseNumber_truncate⟧.

**Theorem (the Morse inequalities for a filtration of a space; V3.32).** Let $X_0 \subseteq X_1 \subseteq \cdots$ be subsets of a space, $n \ge 1$, and suppose that the steps $(X_{k+1}, X_k)$, $k < n$, have finite-dimensional homology over $\Bbbk$. Then $H_i(X_n, X_0; \Bbbk)$ is finite-dimensional ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · finiteDimensional_subsetHomology_of_steps⟧, and with $b_i = \dim H_i(X_n, X_0; \Bbbk)$:

- $b_l \le C_l$ ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · singular_weak_morse_inequality⟧;
- $\operatorname{alt}_l(b) \le \operatorname{alt}_l(C)$ ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · singular_morse_inequality⟧;
- $\sum_{i \le N} (-1)^i b_i = \sum_{i \le N} (-1)^i C_i$ if no step has homology in degree $N + 1$ ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · singular_euler_characteristic⟧.

Under the cell hypothesis of §IV.3 for a finite set $P$ with an index and a step, $C_i = \#\{p \in P : \operatorname{ind}(p) = i\}$ ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · singularMorseNumber_eq_card⟧, and the three statements hold with these numbers ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · singular_weak_morse_inequality_of_cells⟧ ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · singular_morse_inequality_of_cells⟧, the alternating sum being $\sum_{p \in P} (-1)^{\operatorname{ind}(p)}$ when every index is at most $N$ ⟦Homology/TripleFiltration · singular_euler_characteristic_of_cells⟧.

*Proof.* §§IV.3–IV.4 for the truncated filtration and the chain $0 \le 1 \le \dots \le n$, rewritten with the equalities of the totals and of the Morse numbers. For the version with cells, (cells) for the steps of $X$ gives (cells) for the abstract groups, and the count of §IV.3 applies; the Euler characteristic is regrouped by index as there. $\square$

This holds for any filtration of any space by subsets, and the finiteness of $H_*(X_n, X_0)$ is a consequence, not an assumption.

### IV.19 The homology of a slab

Milnor computes the homology of $(M^{a_i}, M^{a_{i-1}})$ in one line (p. 29): it equals that of $(M^{a_{i-1}} \cup e^{\lambda_i}, M^{a_{i-1}})$ by Theorems 3.1 and 3.2, which equals $H_*(e^{\lambda_i}, \dot e^{\lambda_i})$ by excision. The formal proof makes each equality a lemma.

*What the first equality hides.* It is a statement about pairs, not spaces. Theorem 3.2 is stated up to homotopy type, and the cell is attached to $M^{c-\varepsilon}$, $c$ the critical value, not to $M^{a_{i-1}}$. The top of the pair moves along deformations that fix $M^{c-\varepsilon}$: the deformation of $M^{a_i}$ onto $M^{c+\varepsilon}$ (Theorem 3.1) and that of $M^{c+\varepsilon}$ onto $M^{c-\varepsilon} \cup e$ (the proof of Theorem 3.2, in the strong form of Part II, §§II.13–2.14). The bottom cannot be moved along a deformation of pairs, since the deformation of $M^{c-\varepsilon}$ onto $M^{a_{i-1}}$ does not extend to $M^{c+\varepsilon}$. It moves in homology all the same, because $H_*(M^{c-\varepsilon}, M^{a_{i-1}}) = 0$.

**Lemma (moving the bottom over a field; V3.33).** In a long exact sequence (§IV.1) all of whose terms $A_i$ vanish, every $g_i : B_i \to C_i$ is bijective ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · LongExactSeq.bijective_g_of_subsingleton⟧ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · LongExactSeq.equivOfSubsingleton⟧: it is injective because $\ker g_i = \operatorname{im} f_i = 0$, and surjective for $i = 0$ by the end of the sequence and for $i + 1$ because $\delta_i$ lands in $A_i = 0$. Hence, for subsets $A \subseteq B \subseteq D$ with $A$ a strong deformation retract of $B$, $H_i(D, A; \Bbbk) \cong H_i(D, B; \Bbbk)$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · subsetHomologyEquivOfIsSDRBottom⟧: the sequence of the triple $A \subseteq B \subseteq D$ has $H_*(B, A) = 0$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · subsingleton_subsetHomology_of_isSDR⟧. The top moves along strong deformation retracts by §IV.7 ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · subsetHomologyEquivOfIsSDR⟧.

**Lemma (the slab, abstractly; V3.33).** Let $A \subseteq B \subseteq X' \subseteq X$ be subsets of a Hausdorff space with $B$ closed, and let $e_j = \chi_j(D^{k_j})$, $j < m$, be pairwise disjoint cells attached to $B$ as in §IV.17, with $B \cup \bigcup_j e_j \subseteq X'$. If $A$ is a strong deformation retract of $B$, $B \cup \bigcup_j e_j$ one of $X'$, and $X'$ one of $X$, then $H_i(X, A; \Bbbk)$ is finite-dimensional, of dimension $\#\{j : k_j = i\}$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · finrank_subsetHomology_of_cells⟧.

*Proof.*
$$H_i(X, A) \cong H_i(X, B) \cong H_i(X', B) \cong H_i\big(B \cup \textstyle\bigcup_j e_j,\ B\big),$$
by moving the bottom, then the top twice; the last group is given by the cell-attachment theorem (§IV.17). $\square$

For the slab, $A = M^{a_{i-1}}$, $B = M^{c-\varepsilon}$, $X' = M^{c+\varepsilon}$ and $X = M^{a_i}$.

**The submanifold setting.** Part II proves Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 and Remark 3.3 for a slice submanifold $N$ of a finite-dimensional real normed space $V$, modelled on a finite-dimensional $Z$ (§0.1) ⟦Milnor/Chapter2/Submanifold · IsSliceSubmanifold⟧, and a function $f = F|_N$ restricted from a smooth $F : V \to \mathbb R$; here $N$ is moreover compact. The sublevel sets are $N^a = \{q \in N : f(q) \le a\}$ ⟦Chapter2/Crossing · sublevel⟧. Homology is taken over a field $\Bbbk$ of some universe, while $N$ lives in $V$, possibly in another. The statements are therefore made for a space $T$ in the universe of $\Bbbk$ together with a homeomorphism $e : T \cong N$, homology being read in $T$ through $e$; for $N$ itself one takes $e = \mathrm{id}$ (§IV.20), and §IV.21 uses the freedom for abstract manifolds. Strong deformation retracts are carried by homeomorphisms ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · IsStrongDeformationRetract.preimage_homeomorph⟧ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · IsStrongDeformationRetract.preimage_subtype⟧, and the number of elements satisfying a predicate does not depend on the enumeration ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · card_filter_equiv⟧.

**Theorem (one critical level; V3.34).** Let $S$ be a finite set of non-degenerate critical points of $f$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsNondegenerateCriticalPointM⟧, all with $f = c$, and $a' < c < a$ such that every critical point outside $S$ has value $< a'$ or $> a$. Then $H_i(N^a, N^{a'}; \Bbbk)$, read in $T$, is finite-dimensional, of dimension the number of points of $S$ of index $i$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomology · sublevel_step_critical⟧.

*The proof as formalized.*

1. Let $\varepsilon_0 = \min(c - a', a - c) > 0$. The set $f^{-1}[c - \varepsilon_0, c + \varepsilon_0]$ is compact, being closed in the compact $N$, and its critical points lie in $S$, the others having values outside $[a', a]$.
2. Remark 3.3 at $c$ (Part II, §II.14) ⟦Chapter2/SeveralCritical · remark_3_3_submanifold⟧ gives $\varepsilon_1 > 0$ such that for every $0 < \varepsilon \le \varepsilon_1$ there are cells $\chi_p$, $p \in S$, of dimension the index of $p$: continuous and injective on the closed ball, meeting $N^{c-\varepsilon}$ exactly in the image of the sphere, contained in $N^{c+\varepsilon}$, pairwise disjoint, and with $N^{c-\varepsilon} \cup \bigcup_p e_p$ a strong deformation retract of $N^{c+\varepsilon}$. Take $\varepsilon = \min(\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_0/2)$, so that $a' < c - \varepsilon$ and $c + \varepsilon < a$.
3. Theorem 3.1 for a compact submanifold (Part II, §II.5) ⟦Chapter2/Submanifold · theorem_3_1_submanifold_of_isCompact⟧, on $[a', c - \varepsilon]$ and on $[c + \varepsilon, a]$, which contain no critical values: $N^{a'}$ is a strong deformation retract of $N^{c-\varepsilon}$, and $N^{c+\varepsilon}$ one of $N^a$.
4. Index the cells by $\{0, \dots, \#S - 1\}$ through an enumeration of $S$, and carry everything to $T$ by $e^{-1}$: the maps $e^{-1} \circ \chi_p$ are continuous and injective on the ball, their cells are pairwise disjoint and attached to the closed set $e^{-1}(N^{c-\varepsilon})$, and the strong deformation retracts are carried along.
5. The slab lemma with $A = e^{-1}(N^{a'})$, $B = e^{-1}(N^{c-\varepsilon})$, $X' = e^{-1}(N^{c+\varepsilon})$, $X = e^{-1}(N^a)$ gives the dimension $\#\{j : k_j = i\}$, which is the number of points of $S$ of index $i$, counted through the enumeration. $\square$

**Theorem (the homology of a slab, p. 29; V3.34).** Let every critical point of $f$ be non-degenerate, $P$ the finite set of critical points, and $a' \le a$ two values taken by no critical point, such that all critical values in $(a', a)$ are equal. Then $H_i(N^a, N^{a'}; \Bbbk)$ is finite-dimensional, of dimension the number of critical points of index $i$ in $f^{-1}(a', a)$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomology · sublevel_step⟧.

*Proof.* If some critical point $q_0$ has $a' < f(q_0) < a$, let $c = f(q_0)$ and let $S$ be the set of critical points on the level $c$. A critical point outside $S$ cannot have its value in $(a', a)$, where all critical values equal $c$, nor at $a'$ or $a$, which are no critical values; so the previous theorem applies, and its count is the stated one, the critical points in $f^{-1}(a', a)$ being exactly those of $S$. Otherwise no critical point has its value in $[a', a]$; Theorem 3.1 makes $N^{a'}$ a strong deformation retract of $N^a$, the group vanishes (§IV.7), and the count is $0$. $\square$

### IV.20 The Morse filtration and the inequalities on a compact submanifold

**Lemma (separating values: Milnor's choice of the $a_i$).** For a finite set $s$ of reals all larger than $L$ there are $n > 0$ and a monotone sequence $a_0 \le a_1 \le \cdots$ with $L < a_0$, no $a_k$ in $s$, every element of $s$ in $(a_0, a_n)$, and at most one element of $s$ in each interval $(a_k, a_{k+1})$, $k < n$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · exists_separating_seq⟧.

*Proof.* Induction on $s$, adding each time a new minimum $m$. For $s$ empty, take $n = 1$ and the constant sequence $L + 1$. Given, by induction, a sequence for $s$ with lower bound $m$, so that $m < a_0$, prepend $(L + m)/2$: the new first interval $\big((L + m)/2, a_0\big)$ contains $m$ and no element of $s$, all of which lie above $a_0$. $\square$

If $a_0 < c < a_n$ and $c$ is none of the $a_k$, then $c$ lies in some $(a_k, a_{k+1})$ with $k < n$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · exists_mem_Ioo_of_lt⟧, $k + 1$ being the least index with $c < a_{k+1}$; for a monotone sequence these intervals are disjoint ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · eq_of_mem_Ioo_of_monotone⟧. A global maximum is a critical point ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · isCriticalPointM_of_forall_ge⟧, like a global minimum ⟦Chapter2/CWType · isCriticalPointM_of_forall_le⟧: the local representative has a local extremum there, so its derivative vanishes.

**Theorem (the Morse filtration, p. 29; V3.34).** Let $N$ be a compact slice submanifold of $V$ and $F : V \to \mathbb R$ smooth with every critical point of $f = F|_N$ non-degenerate. There are $n > 0$ and values $a_0 \le a_1 \le \cdots$, taken by no critical point, with $N^{a_0} = \emptyset$, $N^{a_n} = N$, at most one critical value in each $(a_k, a_{k+1})$, and $H_i(N^{a_{k+1}}, N^{a_k}; \Bbbk)$ finite-dimensional, of dimension the number of critical points of index $i$ in $f^{-1}(a_k, a_{k+1})$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomology · exists_morse_filtration⟧ ⟦Homology/MorseHomology · exists_morse_filtration'⟧.

*Proof.* A Morse function on a compact manifold has finitely many critical points ⟦Chapter1/ManifoldMorse · IsMorseFunction.finite_setOf_isCriticalPointM⟧; let $P$ be their set and $s$ the set of critical values, all larger than $L = -\sum_{c \in s} |c| - 1$. Take the separating sequence of the lemma. $N^{a_0} = \emptyset$, because the minimum of $f$ is a critical value and so lies above $a_0$; $N^{a_n} = N$, because the maximum is a critical value below $a_n$. Each step is a slab of §IV.19, its ends being taken by no critical point and its interior containing at most one critical value. $\square$

Milnor starts his chain at $M^{a_0} = \emptyset$ without naming $a_0$, and ends it at $M^{a_k} = M$; both rest on the minimum and the maximum of $f$ being critical values. The filtration is repackaged for §IV.18 ⟦Homology/MorseHomology · exists_cells_filtration⟧: $X_k = N^{a_k}$ read in $T$, with $X_0 = \emptyset$ and $X_n = T$, steps with finite-dimensional homology, and the cell hypothesis of §IV.3 with $\operatorname{step}(p)$ the unique $k < n$ such that $a_k < f(p) < a_{k+1}$; it exists because $a_0 < f(p) < a_n$, and is unique because the intervals are disjoint. The numbers $C_\lambda$ of §0.2, the numbers of critical points of index $\lambda$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerChar · critCount⟧, are the counts in $P$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomology · critCount_eq_card⟧.

**Betti numbers (V3.33).** $H_i(X; \Bbbk)$ is Mathlib's singular homology ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · spaceHomology⟧, and the Betti number $b_i(X) = \dim_\Bbbk H_i(X; \Bbbk)$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · bettiNumber⟧ (§0.6) is Milnor's $R_i(X)$; by convention it is $0$ when $H_i(X; \Bbbk)$ is infinite-dimensional, which does not happen for the spaces below. Homeomorphic spaces have isomorphic homology and the same Betti numbers ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · spaceHomologyEquivOfHomeomorph⟧ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · bettiNumber_eq_of_homeomorph⟧, and the pair of subsets $(E, \emptyset)$ has the homology of $E$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · subsetHomologyUnivEquiv⟧ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · finrank_subsetHomology_eq_bettiNumber⟧ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · finiteDimensional_spaceHomology_of_subsetHomology⟧.

**Theorem 5.2, $(4_\lambda)$ and Corollary 5.4 on a compact submanifold (V3.35–V3.37).** In this setting, for every field $\Bbbk$, with $b_i = \dim H_i(N; \Bbbk)$, $C_\lambda$ the number of critical points of index $\lambda$, and $n = \dim N$:

- $H_i(N; \Bbbk)$ is finite-dimensional ⟦Homology/MorseHomology · finiteDimensional_spaceHomology⟧;
- (2) $b_\lambda \le C_\lambda$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomology · bettiNumber_le_critCount⟧ ⟦Homology/MorseHomology · bettiNumber_le_critCount'⟧;
- $(4_\lambda)$: $b_\lambda - b_{\lambda-1} + \dots \pm b_0 \le C_\lambda - C_{\lambda-1} + \dots \pm C_0$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomology · altSum_bettiNumber_le⟧ ⟦Homology/MorseHomology · altSum_bettiNumber_le'⟧;
- (3) $\sum_{i \le n} (-1)^i b_i = \sum_{\lambda \le n} (-1)^\lambda C_\lambda$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomology · sum_bettiNumber_eq_sum_critCount⟧ ⟦Homology/MorseHomology · sum_bettiNumber_eq_sum_critCount'⟧;
- $b_i = 0$, indeed $H_i(N; \Bbbk) = 0$, for $i > n$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomology · bettiNumber_eq_zero_of_finrank_lt⟧ ⟦Homology/MorseHomology · subsingleton_spaceHomology_of_finrank_lt⟧, so that the left side of (3) is the whole alternating sum;
- **Corollary 5.4:** if $\lambda \ge 1$ and $C_{\lambda+1} = C_{\lambda-1} = 0$, then $b_\lambda = C_\lambda$ and $b_{\lambda+1} = b_{\lambda-1} = 0$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomology · bettiNumber_eq_critCount_of_succ⟧; if $C_1 = 0$, then $b_0 = C_0$ and $b_1 = 0$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomology · bettiNumber_eq_critCount_of_zero⟧; and if no two consecutive $C_i$ are nonzero, $b_i = C_i$ for every $i$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomology · bettiNumber_eq_critCount_of_lacunary⟧.

All of these hold for every space homeomorphic to $N$ (the forms with $e$) and for $N$ itself (the primed forms).

*Proof.* The filtration above in §IV.18: $X_0 = \emptyset$ and $X_n = T$, so $H_i(X_n, X_0) = H_i(T)$. For (3), no step has homology in degree $\dim N + 1$, because no index exceeds $\dim N$ ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · indexM_le_finrank⟧. Vanishing above the dimension is (2) with $C_i = 0$, and Corollary 5.4 and the lacunary principle are §IV.4 for $b$ and $C$, which satisfy $(4_l)$ for every $l$. $\square$

Milnor's hypothesis that the critical points be isolated (p. 29) is redundant: non-degenerate critical points are isolated, so on a compact manifold there are finitely many (Part I, §§I.11, 1.15).

### IV.21 Compact manifolds, $\chi = \sum_i (-1)^i b_i$, and three examples

**Compact submanifolds of $\mathbb R^n$ (V3.39).** For a compact slice submanifold $N \subseteq \mathbb R^n$ of dimension $k$, some $L_p = \|\cdot - p\|^2$ restricts to a Morse function on $N$ (Theorem 6.6; Part III, §III.15) ⟦Chapter4/Focal · exists_isMorseFunction_normSq_sub⟧, and $L_p$ is smooth on $\mathbb R^n$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyManifold · contDiff_normSq_sub⟧. Hence $H_i(N; \Bbbk)$ is finite-dimensional ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyManifold · finiteDimensional_spaceHomology_of_isCompact⟧ and $b_i(N) = 0$ for $i > k$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyManifold · bettiNumber_eq_zero_of_isCompact⟧, for $N$ and for every space homeomorphic to it.

**Theorem ($\chi$ is the homological Euler characteristic; V3.38).**

1. Let $N \subseteq \mathbb R^{K+1}$, $K \ge 1$, be a compact nonempty slice submanifold of dimension $d + 1$, and $\chi_N$ an Euler number of $N$ in the sense of Part III, §III.9 ⟦Chapter3/EulerChar · IsEulerNumber⟧: the index sum of every smooth tangent field with finitely many zeros. Then $\chi_N = \sum_{i=0}^{d+1} (-1)^i \dim H_i(N; \Bbbk)$ for every field $\Bbbk$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyManifold · IsEulerNumber.eq_sum_bettiNumber⟧.
2. For a compact Hausdorff smooth manifold $M$ without boundary, modelled on a finite-dimensional $E$, the Euler number $\chi(M)$ of Part III, §III.21 ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · eulerNumberM⟧, which is the alternating count $\sum_\lambda (-1)^\lambda C_\lambda$ of every Morse function ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · sum_critCount_eq_eulerNumberM⟧, equals $\sum_{i \le \dim M} (-1)^i \dim H_i(M; \Bbbk)$ for every field $\Bbbk$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyManifold · eulerNumberM_eq_sum_bettiNumber⟧.

*Proof.* (1) Take $L_p$ Morse on $N$. The gradient field and Poincaré–Hopf give $\sum_\lambda (-1)^\lambda C_\lambda(L_p) = \chi_N$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerChar · IsEulerNumber.sum_critCount_eq⟧, and formula (3) of §IV.20 for $L_p$ gives $\sum_i (-1)^i b_i(N) = \sum_\lambda (-1)^\lambda C_\lambda(L_p)$. (2) By a Whitney embedding, $M$ is diffeomorphic to a compact slice submanifold $N \subseteq \mathbb R^{K+1}$ modelled on $\mathbb R^{\dim M}$ ⟦Chapter4/Whitney · exists_diffeomorph_sliceSubmanifold⟧, on which some $L_p$ is Morse; through the diffeomorphism $\Phi : M \to N$, $L_p \circ \Phi$ is a Morse function on $M$ ⟦Chapter4/WhitneyInvariance · IsMorseFunction.comp_diffeomorph_symm⟧ with the same numbers $C_\lambda$ ⟦Chapter4/WhitneyInvariance · critCount_comp_symm⟧ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyManifold · exists_whitney_normSq⟧. Then
$$\chi(M) = \sum_\lambda (-1)^\lambda C_\lambda(L_p \circ \Phi) = \sum_\lambda (-1)^\lambda C_\lambda(L_p|_N) = \sum_i (-1)^i b_i(M),$$
the last by formula (3) for $N$, read in $M$ through the homeomorphism $\Phi$. $\square$

The left side of (2) does not involve the field, so the alternating sum of the Betti numbers is the same for every field. Milnor computes $\chi(M)$ over a field $F$ on p. 29 and uses it in §6 without reference to $F$. For the individual $b_i$ no such statement holds, and none is claimed.

**Theorem (the Morse inequalities on a compact manifold; V3.39).** Let $M$ be a compact Hausdorff smooth manifold without boundary, modelled on a finite-dimensional real normed space $E$, of dimension $n$, and $\Bbbk$ a field in the universe of $M$. Then $H_i(M; \Bbbk)$ is finite-dimensional ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyManifold · finiteDimensional_spaceHomology_of_compactSpace⟧ and vanishes for $i > n$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyManifold · bettiNumber_eq_zero_of_compactSpace⟧. For every Morse function $g$ on $M$ (§0.2) ⟦Chapter1/Manifold · IsMorseFunction⟧, with $b_i = \dim H_i(M; \Bbbk)$:

- (3) $\sum_{i \le n} (-1)^i b_i = \sum_{\lambda \le n} (-1)^\lambda C_\lambda(g)$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyManifold · sum_bettiNumber_eq_sum_critCount_of_compactSpace⟧;
- $(4_\lambda)$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyManifold · altSum_bettiNumber_le_of_compactSpace⟧;
- (2) $b_\lambda \le C_\lambda(g)$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyManifold · bettiNumber_le_critCount_of_compactSpace⟧;
- the lacunary principle ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyManifold · bettiNumber_eq_critCount_of_lacunary_of_compactSpace⟧.

*The proof as formalized.* Finiteness and vanishing come from the Whitney embedding and the first paragraph of this section, carried back by the homeomorphism. Formula (3) is part 2 of the previous theorem: both sides equal $\chi(M)$. For $(4_\lambda)$ there are two cases. In dimension $0$ the inequalities are equalities, $b_i = C_i$ for every $i$: for $i = 0$ by (3), whose sums then have a single term; for $i > 0$ both sides vanish, there being no homology above the dimension and no index above $0$. In dimension $d + 1 \ge 1$, $g$ is realized as an ambient function on an embedded copy ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyManifold · exists_ambient_of_isMorseFunction⟧: $M \cong N \subseteq \mathbb R^{K+1}$, $g \circ \Phi^{-1}$ is Morse on $N$, and it is the restriction of a smooth $G$ on $\mathbb R^{K+1}$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerChar · exists_contDiff_comp_val_eq⟧ (built from the nearest-point retraction and a cut-off; for $N$ empty, $G = 0$), whose restriction has non-degenerate critical points and the numbers $C_\lambda(g)$. Then $(4_\lambda)$ of §IV.20 applies. (2) and the lacunary principle follow from $(4_\lambda)$ by §IV.4. $\square$

The detour through ambient functions is needed because Part II proves Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 for restrictions $F|_N$. Milnor's appeal to Whitney (p. 36) is silent about the invariance of homology under homeomorphisms, which carries the results back to $M$.

**Examples (V3.40).**

- *Two critical points* (Reeb's situation, p. 25, with the lacunary principle). If $\dim M = n \ge 2$ and a smooth $f$ on $M$ has exactly two critical points, both non-degenerate, then $b_0 = b_n = 1$ and $b_i = 0$ otherwise, over every field ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyManifold · bettiNumber_of_ncard_eq_two⟧. The two critical points are the minimum, of index $0$, and the maximum, of index $n$ (Part III, §III.4) ⟦Chapter3/Reeb · exists_min_max_of_ncard_eq_two⟧, so $C_\lambda = [\lambda = 0] + [\lambda = n]$, and for $n \ge 2$ no two consecutive $C_\lambda$ are nonzero.
- *The torus* of Milnor's first diagram (Part III, §§III.5–III.6). Its height function has $(C_0, C_1, C_2) = (1, 2, 1)$ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · Torus.critCount_height⟧, so $b_0 \le 1$, $b_1 \le 2$, $b_2 \le 1$, $b_i = 0$ for $i \ge 3$, and $b_0 - b_1 + b_2 = 0$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyManifold · torus_bettiNumber⟧, in agreement with its Euler number $0$ ⟦Chapter3/Torus · isEulerNumber_torus⟧. The Betti numbers $1, 2, 1$ themselves do not follow from these inequalities and are not derived.
- *The spheres.* $\sum_i (-1)^i b_i(S^n) = 1 + (-1)^n$ for $n \ge 1$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyManifold · sphere_sum_bettiNumber⟧, from the Euler number of the sphere (Part III, §III.10) ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharSphere · isEulerNumber_sph⟧. For $n \ge 2$ the height function, with $C_0 = C_n = 1$ and all other $C_\lambda = 0$ ⟦Chapter3/EulerCharSphere · critCount_heightFun⟧, is lacunary, so $b_0 = b_n = 1$ and $b_i = 0$ otherwise ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyManifold · sphere_bettiNumber⟧, in agreement with the direct computation of §IV.16. For the circle the principle does not apply ($C_0 = C_1 = 1$), and Morse theory gives only $b_0 = b_1 \le 1$ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyManifold · circle_bettiNumber⟧; §IV.16 gives $b_0 = b_1 = 1$.

### IV.22 What the formalization taught us

- **The homology had to be built.** Relative singular homology, the sequences of pairs and triples, excision, Mayer–Vietoris and the homology of spheres were absent from Mathlib, and Milnor's single line on p. 29 rests on all of them ⟦Homology/Relative · relHomology⟧ ⟦Homology/Excision · excision⟧ ⟦Homology/Spheres · cellPairIso⟧.
- **The algebra needs only exact sequences of triples**: no functoriality, no compatibility between triples, no excision ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · FiltrationHomology⟧. The finiteness of $H_*(M)$ follows from that of the slabs ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · finiteDimensional_of_steps⟧.
- **Small hypotheses of the algebra**: Lemma 5.1 needs $n \ge 1$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · le_sum_of_chain⟧; the additivity of $\chi$ on a triple needs the vanishing of one group ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · LongExactSeq.altSum_eq_of_finrank_eq_zero⟧; Corollary 5.4 needs only $R_{\lambda+1} \ge 0$ ⟦Chapter3/Inequalities · altSum_eq_of_altSum_le⟧.
- **Homotopy invariance for pairs comes from the absolute theorem**, through the cylinder and the five lemma, without the naturality of Mathlib's chain homotopy ⟦Homology/RelativeHomotopy · relHomology_map_eq_of_homotopy⟧.
- **Hatcher's order of fineness was replaced** by a geometric one, for which the inequality on faces is immediate ⟦Homology/SmallChains · fineOrder_comp_faceMap_le⟧.
- **"By excision" is excision after a deformation**: remove the centre of the cell, push the rest radially onto the bottom set, which must be closed, and then excise ⟦Homology/CellExcision · isIso_relHomology_map_cellPair⟧.
- **Several cells on one level need disjointness**, which splits the sequence of a triple; exactness alone gives only an inequality of dimensions ⟦Homology/CellAttach · relHomologyCellsUnionInsertIso⟧.
- **The first equality of p. 29 is about pairs**: the top moves along strong deformation retracts that fix the bottom, the bottom only in homology ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · subsetHomologyEquivOfIsSDRBottom⟧ ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · finrank_subsetHomology_of_cells⟧.
- **Milnor's levels need distinct critical values**; one critical value per slab, with any number of critical points on it, removes the hypothesis ⟦Homology/MorseHomology · sublevel_step⟧, and the two ends of the filtration rest on the extrema being critical ⟦Homology/MorseHomology · exists_morse_filtration⟧.
- **$\chi(M)$ does not depend on the field**, a consequence of formula (3) ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyManifold · eulerNumberM_eq_sum_bettiNumber⟧; the passage from an abstract manifold to an embedded copy uses the invariance of homology under homeomorphisms ⟦Homology/MorseHomologyBasic · bettiNumber_eq_of_homeomorph⟧.
